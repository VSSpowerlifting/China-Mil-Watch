A Clean Wind Rises Over the Taihang Mountains: Following Them to Understand the Continuation and Inheritance of Red Genes

太行山上清风扬，跟随他们感悟红色基因的赓续传承

About this analysis
Summary
Officers and soldiers from an unnamed station under the Naval Aviation University conducted a political education (红色基因传承, 'red gene inheritance') field trip through the Taihang Mountains, visiting the Eighth Route Army Taihang Memorial Hall, the Red Flag Canal Memorial Hall, and the Huangyaidong Arsenal ruins, with discussion focused on clean governance and resistance to financial misconduct. The article documents standard political work content — specifically, the PLA's recurring use of revolutionary-era frugality narratives to reinforce discipline among logistics and finance personnel at a rear-echelon installation. Its value is as a record of how the institution frames anti-corruption norms for junior officers at the unit level, not as evidence of a new policy line or changed posture at the Naval Aviation University.

**A Clean Wind Rises Over the Taihang Mountains**

■ Zhang Yidong, Yuan Yifeng

*In the 1960s, Red Flag Canal construction workers labored on sheer cliff faces as if "swinging on a pendulum." Photo provided by the Red Flag Canal Memorial Hall.*

Early summer in the Taihang Mountains: range upon range of peaks, all things competing to flourish.

Following the Zhuozhang River upstream, winding through a twisting mountain road, one arrives in an instant at a heroic land — Wuxiang.

More than 80 years ago, the Eighth Route Army General Headquarters was stationed here for an extended period. This land bore witness to the unyielding and courageous struggle of revolutionary forebears, and also bore witness to the people's army's enduring spirit of purity and honor. The smoke of battle over the Taihang Mountains has long since cleared, but the officers and soldiers of a certain station under the Naval Aviation University remain garrisoned on this land. On this day, the author joined the station's officers and soldiers on a journey to seek their roots, exploring the spiritual bloodline that has endured without cease on this land.

Rounding a bend, the vehicle stopped before the Eighth Route Army Taihang Memorial Hall. Before them stood a massive sculpture of the Taihang Mountain anti-Japanese war, and the atmosphere of beacon fires and a shaking land and rivers struck them full in the face.

Stepping inside the hall, two yellowed letters in a prominent position drew everyone to a halt. They were a loan letter and a family letter written by Zhu De, then Commander-in-Chief of the Eighth Route Army.

In the winter of 1937, Zhu De learned that his hometown of Yilong, Sichuan had been struck by famine and that his mother was living in hardship. Yet after more than ten years devoted to the revolution, he had accumulated almost no savings, and could only write to a close friend to borrow money. In the letter he candidly stated, "For these dozen or more years I have truly had not a single coin, and so it shall be in the future as well," adding, "This sum I also cannot repay you; please consider it a donation."

The other family letter was written by Zhu De to his relatives in the same year: "Henceforth it is not appropriate to spend useless money to come and see me; none should come except those who can fight and serve the nation." He went on to explain: "In order to uphold the good rules of the revolutionary army, I have never once asked for a single coin. Any idle person who comes will be difficult for the organization or for me to receive." These words, which seem almost unfeeling, conceal behind them a heart that sacrificed private interests for the public good. In 2016, Chairman Xi specifically referenced these two letters at a symposium commemorating the 130th anniversary of Comrade Zhu De's birth, saying: "The Commander-in-Chief of the Eighth Route Army, with his illustrious battle achievements, was so impoverished and so incorrupt — it commands the deepest respect!"

"As Commander-in-Chief of the Eighth Route Army, he never felt embarrassed by 'having not a single coin for more than ten years,' and said plainly, 'I have shared hardships with the soldiers for more than ten years — I am extremely happy.' We should also ask ourselves: do we have this kind of mindset?" After reading the letters, Section Chief Li of the station's finance section shared his thoughts with everyone. The finance section has a longstanding tradition: section cadres write a clean-governance pledge every year, read it aloud before everyone, and then post it themselves in a prominent position in the hall, voluntarily accepting the supervision of their comrades. "Every account handled, every receipt processed, must be worthy of the handprint pressed onto that clean-governance pledge," he said.

Carrying reflection and contemplation, the group continued eastward over the Taihang Mountains. As the vehicle passed through Hongtijuan Pass, the elevation began to rise, the mountains outside the window grew increasingly steep, and a canal of water like a silver chain threaded its way along the mountainside, glinting like scattered gold in the afternoon sunlight — the Red Flag Canal had arrived.

In the 1960s, the people of Linxian County, Henan, in order to change their harsh living conditions of depending on the heavens for rain, used hammers, chisels, and bare hands to carve out an "artificial river in the sky" through the sheer, knife-cut cliffs of the Taihang Mountains.

At the Red Flag Canal Memorial Hall, a set of figures filled the officers and soldiers with reverence: nearly ten years of canal construction; a project with a total investment of 68.65 million yuan; cumulative consumption of 123.5 tons of steel, 6,705 tons of cement, and 2,740 tons of explosives — and not a single incident of embezzlement or misappropriation of materials from start to finish, with the allocation of tens of thousands of tons of materials fully open and transparent throughout. The worn explosive crates and yellowed receipts in the display cases silently testified to the forebears' clean and honest character: "one chisel and one hammer to carve the mountains and rivers, one fen and one hao to guard the original aspiration (一钎一锤凿山河，一厘一毫守初心)."

Taking seats in the memorial hall's lecture theater, the officers and soldiers held a discussion around the theme of "how to let a clean and upright wind (正气清风) blow away unhealthy tendencies (不正之风)."

Discipline inspection officer Chai Lin recalled an experience from the previous year — while supervising the procurement of non-staple foodstuffs, he noticed that the price of a certain vegetable was slightly higher than in the past. He immediately retrieved that month's delivery receipts and then went to nearby supermarkets and markets to inquire about prices, ultimately confirming that it was a normal price fluctuation caused by seasonal factors.

In truth, a few extra jiao per jin of vegetables is inconspicuous within the overall food account, and some might feel it is not worth quibbling over a few jiao. But Chai Lin felt it was entirely worth it: "Even if each jin of vegetables costs only a few extra jiao per day, accumulated over time it becomes a substantial figure. And if you let a few jiao slide today, tomorrow you may let several hundred yuan slide." After visiting the Red Flag Canal Memorial Hall, his feelings ran even deeper: "In building such a massive project as the Red Flag Canal, they could account clearly for every single steel chisel consumed. In our use of funds, we absolutely cannot be 'roughly right' (大概齐)."

Is there the mistaken mentality of "others steal big, I steal small"? Is there the absurd notion that "public property is there to be used, so why not use it"? Is there the improper desire to use the convenience of one's position to run a "private abacus (小算盘)"? During the discussion, everyone raised problems without sparing anyone's feelings, pointing directly to specific individuals and specific incidents, and analyzed and reflected on whether their resolve to resist temptation was strong enough.

Faces flushed and brows sweating, but this "wind-chasing" journey was not yet over. The vehicle set off again and stopped before the ruins of the Huangyaidong Arsenal.

The old stone walls of the ruins were mottled, silent as ever. Political work section officer Ding Shuai noticed several regulations posted on the wall: practice diligence and frugality, eliminate all waste; those who fail to care for machinery and tools or who waste materials shall be penalized; absolutely prohibited is the theft and sale of tools and raw materials or their private transfer to others...

Upon further inquiry, everyone learned that there was also a story behind this notice. In the autumn of 1940, Yang Hongzhang, a munitions worker who had just arrived at the arsenal, was cleaning and swept a piece of high-speed steel the size of a fingernail from a lathe, mistaking it for iron shavings and discarding it. When the master craftsman found out, he rushed to the rubbish heap to search through it: "This was brought back by our comrades at the risk of their lives from enemy-occupied territory — it is more precious than gold!" They used every inch of steel and every gram of explosives where it mattered most, wasting not a fraction of public property, and it was precisely this that allowed the arsenal, starved of raw materials, to supply the front lines with a steady stream of high-quality weapons and ammunition.

"When it comes to integrity and clean governance (清正廉洁), merely not taking and not occupying is not enough." This self-discipline and dedication to the public good, etched into the bone, left Ding Shuai feeling no small measure of shame: "Sometimes we think that 'not taking and not obstructing' makes one a good comrade, but thinking about it now, if one does not calculate and economize to the greatest possible extent and feels no pain at wasting consumables, is that not another form of dereliction of duty?" Measuring themselves against the notice, everyone came to a deep appreciation that the spirit of the revolutionary forebears — "every fen and every li must be converted into combat power" — is the true foundation of purity (纯洁底色).

As dusk approached, the setting sun dyed the Taihang Mountains a scorching gold. On the return journey, the vehicle was considerably quieter than on the way out. Some scrolled through photos of exhibits they had taken on their phones; others were writing something in their notebooks. The upright spirit (盈盈正气) of those fiery wartime years had already filled the hearts of the officers and soldiers.

Original Chinese
近日，笔者跟随海军航空大学某场站官兵穿越太行山，感悟红色基因的赓续传承—— 太行山上清风扬 ■张依栋 原益峰 上世纪60年代，红旗渠施工队员像“荡秋千”一样，在悬崖绝壁间施工。红旗渠纪念馆提供 初夏太行，层峦叠嶂，万物争荣。 沿着浊漳河溯流而上，穿过蜿蜒曲折的盘山公路，转眼就到了英雄的土地——武乡。 80多年前，八路军总部长期驻扎于此，这片土地见证了革命先辈顽强不屈的英勇斗争，也见证了人民军队永葆纯洁光荣的精神本色。太行山上的硝烟早已散尽，但海军航空大学某场站的官兵们依然驻守在这片土地上。这天，笔者跟随场站官兵踏上寻根之旅，探寻这片土地上生生不息的精神血脉。 转过一道弯，车子停在八路军太行纪念馆门前，眼前是巨大的太行山抗战雕塑，烽火连天、山河激荡的气息扑面而来。 走进馆内，显眼位置有两封泛黄的信件，引得大家驻足。那是时任八路军总司令朱德写下的借款信和家书。 1937年冬，朱德得知家乡四川仪陇遭遇饥荒，母亲生活困苦，可他投身革命10余年竟无多少积蓄，只得写信向好友借款。信中他坦言，“我十数年实无一钱，即将来亦如是”，还说“此款我亦不能还你，请作捐助吧”。 另一封家书是朱德在同一年写给亲属的：“以后不宜花去无用之钱来看我，除了能作战报国的人外均不宜来。”他接着解释，“我为了保持革命军队的良规，从来也没有要过一文钱，任何闲散人来，公家及我均难招待”。这看似不近人情的话，背后却是一颗因公废私的苦心。2016年，习主席在纪念朱德同志诞辰130周年座谈会上专门讲到这两封信，说道：“战功赫赫的八路军总司令清贫如此、清廉如此，让人肃然起敬！” “身为八路军总司令，从不因‘十数年实无一钱’而感到窘迫，直言‘我与战士同甘苦已十几年，快愉非常’。我们也应问问自己，有没有这种心态？”读罢信件，场站财务股李股长与大家分享，财务股有个老传统——股室干部每年都要写下廉洁承诺书，并当众念出，然后亲手张贴在大厅显眼位置，自觉接受战友们的监督。“经手的每一笔账、每一张票，都要对得起自己在廉洁承诺书上按下的那个手印。”他说。 带着反思和思考，队伍继续向东翻越太行山。车过虹梯关，海拔开始上升，窗外山势渐陡，一条银链般的渠水从山腰处穿行而来，在午后的阳光下闪着碎金——红旗渠到了。 上世纪60年代，河南林县人民为改变靠天等雨的恶劣生存环境，靠一锤一钎一双手，在万仞壁立、峭壁如削的太行山，硬生生凿出一条“人工天河”。 在红旗渠纪念馆，一组数据让官兵心生敬意：修渠近10年，总投资6865万元的工程，累计消耗钢材123.5吨、水泥6705吨、炸药2740吨，从未发生一起贪污或挪用物资的事件，上万吨物资的调配全程公开透明。展柜里磨损的炸药箱、泛黄的收据，无声诉说着先辈们“一钎一锤凿山河，一厘一毫守初心”的清廉本色。 落座纪念馆报告厅，官兵围绕“如何让正气清风吹走不正之风”主题开展讨论。 纪检干事柴林想起去年的一段经历——那次监督副食品采购工作，他发现某种蔬菜价格略高于以往，当即调取当月的送货单，又跑到附近超市、菜市场询价，最终确定是季节原因导致的价格正常波动。 其实，一斤蔬菜贵了几毛钱，放在整个伙食账里不起眼，有人还会觉得为了几毛钱斤斤计较不值得。但柴林觉得很值：“哪怕每天每斤菜只多几毛，日积月累也是个庞大的数字。而且今天放过了几毛钱，明天就可能放过几百元。”参观完红旗渠纪念馆，他感触更深：“修建红旗渠这么大的工程，他们都能把消耗的每一根钢钎记得清清楚楚，我们在经费使用上更不能‘大概齐’。” 有没有“别人贪大我贪小”的错误心态？有没有“公家的东西不用白不用”的荒唐念头？有没有借着职务便利打“小算盘”的非分之想？讨论中，大家讲问题不留情面，直接点到具体人、具体事，并剖析反思抵抗诱惑的定力够不够强。 红了脸、出了汗，但这场“追风”之旅并未结束。车子重新上路，停在了黄崖洞兵工厂旧址前。 旧址石墙斑驳，静默如初。政治工作处干事丁帅发现墙上张贴的几项规定：奉行勤俭节约，杜绝一切浪费；不爱护机器工具、浪费材料者要受罚；绝对禁止盗卖工具原料或私送他人…… 细问之下，大家了解到，这则标语背后还有一个故事。1940年秋，刚到兵工厂的军工杨鸿章打扫卫生时，将车床上一块指甲盖大的锋钢当作铁屑倒掉，师傅得知后，急忙跑到垃圾堆翻找：“这可是我们同志冒着生命危险从敌占区搞来的，它比金子还贵重！”他们把每一寸钢铁、每一克炸药都用在刀刃上，不浪费公家分毫，才让原材料匮乏的兵工厂，能够源源不断为前线输送质量过硬的枪支弹药。 “讲清正廉洁，光不贪不占是不够的。”这份刻进骨子里的克己奉公，让丁帅不免羞愧，“有时我们觉得‘不拿不卡’就是好同志，但现在想想，没有最大程度精打细算，浪费耗材也不心疼，何尝不是另一种形式的失职？”对照标语，大家深刻体会到，革命前辈身上那种“一分一厘都要化作战斗力”的精神，才是真正的纯洁底色。 天色渐晚，夕阳把太行山染成滚烫的金色。返程路上，车厢里比来时安静了许多。有人翻着手机里拍下的展品照片，有人在本子上写着什么。那些烽火岁月里的盈盈正气，已充溢在官兵心底。
