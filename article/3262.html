About this analysis
Summary
The Xinjiang Military District's Hongshishan Border Defense Company and its subordinate Hongshishan Outpost, positioned on a black Gobi desert along the border with annual precipitation of just 30 millimeters, received piped well water this year after a drilling team spent six months boring 126 meters through hard rock to reach a source yielding 50 cubic meters per day — ending what the article describes as a decades-long water hauling routine that required soldiers to drive dozens of kilometers twice weekly. This is a human-interest feature in the PLA's frontier-unit genre; its value is as a record of how official military media frames hardship endurance (扎根, "putting down roots") and incremental infrastructure improvement as the moral core of border defense service, not as evidence of a capability or posture change. The article documents baseline living conditions at a named Xinjiang Military District outpost and the institutional framing used to recruit and retain soldiers in remote postings.

A Desolate Gobi, A Flourishing Youth

■ Yan Zhenyu, Zhu Mengna, Ma Yaohui

Those who have never been to the Gobi can hardly imagine what kind of "forbidden zone for life" it truly is.

The Xinjiang Military District's Hongshishan Border Defense Company and its subordinate Hongshishan Outpost are situated on a barren black Gobi along the border. Annual average precipitation here is only 30 millimeters, while evaporation exceeds 3,000 millimeters — an extreme arid zone.

The wind and sand have carried away the years, but they cannot carry away the convictions held deep in the heart, nor scatter the stubborn perseverance of those who stand watch. Over the years, the officers and soldiers of the company have fought against the harsh natural environment, guarding the territory beneath their feet and leaving behind patches of green oasis across the Gobi. Now, the soldiers at the outpost have finally seen a trickle of water arrive, and in their hearts, "a green oasis of hope has taken root."

Standing unyielding in the desert, keeping watch over the mountains and rivers in solitude — generation after generation of Hongshishan soldiers, like the old elm tree in the garrison courtyard, have put down deep roots and pushed stubbornly upward, showing the world that in a desolate Gobi, a flourishing youth grows.

After water was connected to the Xinjiang Military District's Hongshishan Outpost, a soldier cups a handful of well water.

A Pool of Well Water, Like a Warm Current Flowing into the Heart

As the well water gurgled through the insulated pipes and flowed into the Hongshishan Outpost, First Sergeant Shao Zuo could hardly believe it — water had truly "arrived" in the depths of the desert! He cupped a handful, took a small sip, and the cool, clear taste refreshed him to the core.

"In the past, water had to be rationed by the day, counted out by the number of days remaining." The story of the outpost soldiers and water is a long one.

With no water source within a hundred li in any direction, the outpost soldiers drove dozens of kilometers to the company to haul water year-round. The company's water came from a well several kilometers away, and the supply was not abundant enough for the entire company, so the water truck could only make two trips per week.

In his second year of service, Shao Zuo learned to drive under the guidance of the senior squad leader. The water truck bounced violently on the long "washboard road," and his head repeatedly struck the cab ceiling with a thud. By the time he finally got the water back to the outpost, he felt as though he had been shaken to pieces.

The most harrowing time came when the water truck broke down halfway. It had to be towed back to the company for repairs, but the water in the tank could not be brought along. Comrades who rushed to help brought large and small containers to collect the water, filling less than a third of them. So everyone dug a small channel and directed the remaining water to a few saxaul trees nearby.

Shao Zuo often said: "On the Gobi, water is more precious than oil." Every time he pumped water from the truck into the storage tank, he habitually placed a basin at the connection point, determined not to waste a single drop.

First Sergeant Lu Zhiming, who is from Guangdong, would often murmur to newly arrived soldiers in his not-quite-standard Mandarin whenever water was being drawn and distributed: "Use it sparingly, use it sparingly." Those were the same words the old squad leader had once impressed upon him.

Back then, Lu Zhiming — who had grown up beside great rivers and the sea — used water rather freely. The old squad leader, pained to watch, gave him several dedicated lessons on how to conserve water. Now, after thirteen years guarding the border, Lu Zhiming has developed considerable expertise in water conservation. Before doing laundry, he first beats the mud off trouser cuffs and sleeve openings with a broom handle, then pours in just half a basin of water to scrub. The used water is then used to mop the floor; the dirty water wrung from the mop is poured into a recycling bucket and kept for "emergencies."

The water level in the storage tank also tugged at everyone's heartstrings. Every night after completing his rounds, Company Commander Wang Xiaojiao would go and check the water reserves: "Everyone depends on this water for cooking, washing up, and doing laundry. Once the level drops below the red line, we have to rush out and haul more."

To solve the outpost's water supply problem, the regimental Party committee had on multiple occasions joined with the local government and construction teams to conduct on-site surveys and probe for water sources. Unfortunately, due to unclear hydrological data and equipment limitations, the idea of drilling a well near the outpost was shelved time and again. Last year, the final stretch of road connecting the company to the outpost was completed, and large machinery could at last reach the outpost via a wide asphalt road.

"Equipment is no longer the constraint — what remains is finding water." In early spring this year, a well-drilling team entered the outpost. For a full six months, the drilling rig hummed at the foot of the mountain. The Gobi's surface looks soft, but underneath lies layer upon layer of hard rock. On the slowest day, the construction team wore out two drill bits and advanced less than two meters. When the drill reached a depth of one hundred meters, murky water surged up. Though undrinkable, it gave everyone a glimpse of hope.

In mid-July, the drilling rig reached a depth of 126 meters, and a jet of clear water suddenly burst forth. The water bubbled and churned, shimmering in the sunlight, and the cheers of the soldiers and construction workers echoed across the Gobi.

The well yields a steady 50 cubic meters of water per day, completely resolving the decades-long drinking water problem for the outpost soldiers. The well water is still slightly bitter and must be filtered before drinking, but in everyone's hearts it is incomparably sweet, soaking into their very souls.

Shao Zuo immediately shared the good news with former battalion commander Dong Zhilin, an old soldier who had always kept the outpost close to his heart; on the other end of the phone, he could not hold back his tears. Cook squad leader, Sergeant Wang Hai, joyfully boiled a pot of pulled noodles (拉面) for everyone: "Before, simmering a pot of broth meant carefully rationing every drop of water — now we can finally enjoy 'pulled-noodle freedom' (拉面自由)." Patrol soldiers returning from duty took a long, satisfying hot shower, and the fatigue of their bodies was swept away in an instant. Private First Class Deng Qiyun, who loves to draw, sketched the outpost's new look onto pieces of Gobi stone. Under his brush, a pool of blue well water flows gently — becoming an eternal warm current in everyone's hearts.

Soldiers Who Put Down Roots Are as Precious as an Oasis in the Gobi

On Hongshishan in the Gobi, what is as precious as water is the touch of green oasis amid the barrenness.

Inside the garrison courtyard of the Hongshishan Border Defense Company stand rows of sand jujube, poplar, and red willow trees — species that endure wind, sand, and drought. Outside the greenhouse, a row of elm trees sway their green leaves, their canopies full and graceful. One tree has the thickest trunk of all, its diameter exceeding 45 centimeters — like a stubborn, tenacious sentinel.

The story begins in 1986. "Every time a big wind blew, yellow sand blotted out the sky and sun — you couldn't even see the road." Located in a vast uninhabited zone, mountain after mountain, peak after peak, the black Gobi stretching endlessly to the horizon looked as though it had been scorched by the blazing sun. At that time, veteran soldier Wang Baoguo had just arrived at the company and always felt that life, like the scenery all around, was "dark and oppressive."

Before returning from one leave, Wang Baoguo deliberately made a detour to a nearby city, where he carefully selected 34 elm saplings, wrapped their roots layer by layer, bound them tightly with straw rope, and carried them back to the company on his back. Water was desperately scarce then — the water brought by three camels was barely enough for the soldiers to use for a single day. He and his comrades poured out the water they had saved up day by day, ladle by ladle, onto the saplings, hoping they would survive and bring a bright splash of color to the Gobi.

The following spring, one elm tree truly survived. And Wang Baoguo also "put down roots" in the harsh Gobi for more than ten years — like that elm tree, his perseverance cast a dense canopy of shade over the border defense.

In 2023, Wang Baoguo, despite his failing health, insisted on returning to visit the old company he had missed day and night. Seeing the old elm tree he had planted with his own hands, he could not help but step forward and stroke the rough trunk again and again: "If a tree can survive, a person can put down roots too. Border defense conditions keep getting better and better — you must hold Hongshishan firm."

"Indeed, we must let the green that represents hope and vitality flourish on Hongshishan." Moved by Wang Baoguo's words, Political Instructor Huo Qi resolved to open up the wasteland in the garrison area and plant a "Roots Grove" (扎根林).

The wasteland was covered in rocks. Everyone moved them by hand and by vehicle, never once complaining of hardship or exhaustion. Blowing sand and floating dust turned the soldiers into mud-covered figures; they simply swung their pickaxes and shovels in a competition to see who could dig deeper and faster. Later, Second Class Staff Sergeant Zheng Linguo devised a simple drip irrigation system. Nourished by water, the saplings raced to put out new leaves, and every time soldiers passed by, they could not help but slow their pace and linger a little longer.

The trees on Hongshishan grow more numerous day by day, and the soldiers of Hongshishan are also "growing toward the sun" (向阳而生).

In one corner of the "Roots Grove," the red willow that Private First Class Wang Shenqi planted with his own hands accompanied him through two years of preparing for the military academy entrance examination. After one practice test, he was disappointed with his score and fell into low spirits; during daytime training he was listless, and several times "fell out of rhythm," drawing sharp criticism from the company commander.

That night, Wang Shenqi came to the "Roots Grove" and gazed intently at the red willow branches swaying in the wind. He suddenly recalled what the company commander had once told him: the plants of the Gobi are short and grow slowly because they channel the bulk of their nutrients into their extensive root systems.

"Settle your mind, put down firm roots — only then will you not be blown over." That slender, fragile sapling gradually straightened its back in the wind and sand, and Wang Shenqi's dream was finally realized through "never giving up, fighting harder in the next round." At his send-off ceremony, eyes red, he said loudly: "Brothers, when I get to the military academy I will study hard, and after I graduate I will come back to Hongshishan and guard the border together with all of you."

Sergeant Xu Penglong and his wife Zhang Ning also exchanged vows of love in the "Roots Grove." This past summer, Zhang Ning traveled for two days across more than 2,000 kilometers to visit him at the company.

Seeing for herself how steep the patrol mountain paths were and how fierce the Gobi winds were, Zhang Ning felt an indescribable ache in her heart. "I used to always complain that he was too busy with work and could barely even make a few phone calls — but after coming here, every grievance in my heart just disappeared." She and Xu Penglong planted several false indigo (紫穗槐) shrubs in the "Roots Grove," and made a promise: when they bloom next year, she will come back to see him.

The Endless Yellow Sand Buries Footprints, but Cannot Cover the Original Aspiration and the Watch Kept

Deep in the Gobi, the harsh natural environment is an unavoidable challenge for all work and life.

This year marks the tenth year Second Class Staff Sergeant Zhang Lijian has guarded the border. On the front line of border defense, he knows by heart which gully mouths are prone to "gravel rain" (沙石雨) and where the gravel is especially sharp.

Zhang Lijian remembers the first time he set out on a patrol ten years ago — the sun was so fierce the rocks seemed about to smoke. Halfway through, his canteen was empty and his lips were so dry they nearly stuck together. In the vast desert, there was nowhere to refill; the squad leader passed his own canteen over, and Zhang Lijian felt too embarrassed to take it, but the squad leader pressed it firmly into his hands. He took a small sip and felt that water was sweeter than honey.

From new recruit to squad leader — today, Zhang Lijian has become "the one who passes the canteen."

During one patrol, a violent wind suddenly struck. After the patrol vehicle came to a stop in the designated area, everyone pushed the doors open with great effort; the gale hit like a thick, heavy wall, sending people stumbling. After adjusting their gear, the team pressed into the wind toward the boundary marker.

Though shielded by wind goggles, Private Huang Qiang — on his first patrol — still got sand in his eyes. Just as he instinctively reached for his canteen to rinse his face, Zhang Lijian had already unclipped his own canteen and held it out: "Use mine — keep yours for drinking."

After years of standing guard on the Gobi, Zhang Lijian's skin has been bronzed to a deep copper color by intense ultraviolet radiation, his face marked by sun spots of varying sizes, and his rough palms frequently cracked by the dry air — yet he is deeply optimistic, regarding these as a distinctive "border-guard medal" (戍边勋章).

"We have our own remedy for a hard life!" Zhang Lijian said that rich cultural and sports activities keep this desolate stretch of Gobi full of vitality. The soldiers leveled a patch of ground on the eastern side of the garrison as a football pitch — no turf, no safety netting; they assembled goalposts from steel pipes and ran freely across the black Gobi, sweat flying.

One team is called the "Gobi Yellow Antelope" (戈壁黄羊), the other the "Gobi Swimming Fish" (戈壁游鱼). "The yellow antelope — that's the Mongolian gazelle — runs like the wind across the Gobi. That name suits us best: we can charge, run, and jump!" Zhang Lijian was already raring to go.

Platoon Leader Yue Jiaxin on the opposing side heard this, patted his chest, and said: "Our 'Gobi Swimming Fish' is the poetic one — gliding steadily through the sea of sand, swimming freely." With the whistle of the weekend, another football match kicked off on schedule.

Every year when new recruits join the company, the unit organizes a screening of the film A Sacred Flame at the Edge of the Sky (《天边有一簇圣火》), which tells the story of a group of soldiers holding the border deep in the desert. The officers and soldiers of the Hongshishan Border Defense Company are among the real-life prototypes for the film's characters. Zhang Lijian was struck by one line of dialogue in the film: "A soldier is himself a poem." Along the long border line, the endless yellow sand buries footprints, but cannot cover the original aspiration (初心) and the watch kept. Looking back on the past, he often tells new soldiers: the poem that belongs to us has far more passion than bitterness.

荒地上全是石头，大伙儿用手搬、用车推，从不叫苦叫累。扬沙、浮尘把官兵变成一个个泥人，大家干脆挥着镐头铁锹比赛，看谁刨得深、挖得快。后来，二级上士郑林国又想方设法搭起一套简易滴灌系统，有了水的滋润，一棵棵树苗争相抽出新叶，每次路过这里，官兵总忍不住放慢脚步多看两眼。 红石山的树一天比一天多，红石山的兵也在“向阳而生”。 “扎根林”的一角，上等兵王审奇亲手栽下的红柳，陪伴他度过备考军校的2年时光。一次自测考试，他成绩不理想、心情低落，白天训练也无精打采，好几次“没跟上节奏”，被连长严厉批评。 夜晚，王审奇来到“扎根林”，凝神看着随风摇曳的红柳枝，忽然记起连长曾告诉他，戈壁里的植株低矮、生长缓慢，是因为它们把大量的养分供给发达的根系。 “沉下心来，把根扎牢，才能不被吹倒。”那棵枝丫细弱的幼苗，在风沙中渐渐挺起腰杆，王审奇的梦想也终于在“始终不放弃、再战更努力”中实现。欢送会上，他红着眼眶大声说：“兄弟们，到军校后我一定好好学习，毕业后再回到红石山，和大家一起守边。” 中士徐鹏龙和妻子张宁也在“扎根林”里相互许下爱的誓言。今年夏天，张宁辗转2天、跨越2000多公里，千里迢迢赶到连队探亲。 真正看到巡逻的山路有多陡、戈壁的风有多大，张宁心里萦绕着说不出的酸楚。“以前老抱怨他工作忙，连电话都打不了几个，可来了这，心里什么疙瘩也没了。”她和徐鹏龙在“扎根林”种下几棵紫穗槐，约定等明年开了花，再来看他。 哨所通水前，邵佐利用水车运水。 战士们在戈壁滩唱歌。 徐鹏龙和张宁栽种紫穗槐。 漫漫黄沙掩埋了足迹，却无法遮住初心和守望 戈壁深处，恶劣的自然环境是一切工作和生活绕不过的难题。 今年是二级上士章丽建戍边的第10年。边防一线，哪条沟口容易下“沙石雨”，哪里的砾石格外锋利，他熟稔于心。 章丽建记得，10年前第一次踏上巡逻路，太阳晒得石头都要冒烟。走到一半，他的水壶就见了底，嘴唇干得快粘在一起。茫茫荒漠，没有地方补水，班长把自己的水壶递过来，他没好意思接，班长硬是塞到他手里。他抿了一小口，感觉那水比蜜还甜。 从新兵变成班长，如今的章丽建成了“递水的人”。 一次巡逻途中，狂风忽然袭来。巡逻车停稳在预定地域后，大家用力推开车门，大风像一堵厚重的墙，撞得人直踉跄。整理好装具，队伍逆风朝界碑走去。 虽然有防风镜遮挡，第一次巡逻的列兵黄强还是被风沙迷了眼睛。就在他下意识摸向水壶想要洗把脸时，章丽建早已摘下自己的水壶递了过来：“用我的吧，你的留着喝。” 常年戍守戈壁，章丽建的皮肤被强烈的紫外线晒成古铜色，脸上长出大小不一的日晒斑，粗糙的手掌因干燥的空气时常生出裂纹，但他十分乐观，认为这是不一样的“戍边勋章”。 “我们自有对抗艰苦生活的良方！”章丽建说，丰富的文体活动让这片荒凉的戈壁热气腾腾。官兵在营区东侧平整了一块土地作为足球场，没有草坪，没有护网，大家用钢管组装球门，在黑戈壁上尽情奔跑，挥洒汗水。 球队一支叫“戈壁黄羊”，一支叫“戈壁游鱼”。“黄羊就是鹅喉羚，在戈壁滩上跑得飞快，这名字最适合我们，能冲、能跑、能跳！”章丽建已经跃跃欲试。 对面的排长岳佳鑫听了，拍拍胸脯说：“我们‘戈壁游鱼’才叫有诗意，能在沙海里稳稳穿行、游来游去。”随着周末的一声哨响，又一场足球赛准时开始了…… 每年新兵下连，连队会组织大家观看电影《天边有一簇圣火》，影片讲述了一群官兵在大漠深处坚守边防的故事，红石山边防连官兵是电影人物的原型之一。章丽建对影片中的一句台词印象深刻：“军人本身就是一首诗。”长长的边防线上，漫漫黄沙掩埋了足迹，却无法遮住初心和守望。回忆过往，他常常告诉新兵，属于咱们的诗，激昂多过苦涩。 图片由母文钊、李 阳摄 版式设计：杨 磊
