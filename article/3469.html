军营观察丨一份演练方案背后的作风之变

About this analysis
Summary
An Air Force unit—identified only as such, with no group army or base designator given—used deepening political rectification training (政治整训) to overhaul how battalion and company commanders draft exercise plans, conduct confrontation drills, and integrate older equipment into networked air-defense operations on a northwest Gobi training ground, with Battalion Commander Chen leading a Party-member assault team (党员攻坚小组) to develop new coordinated tactics for legacy systems. The article documents a specific institutional problem the PLA has named repeatedly but struggled to fix: commanders optimizing for inspection compliance—copying templates, padding logbooks, defaulting to procedural caution—rather than for combat outcomes, and the unit's Party committee response was to impose three hard approval criteria (on-site survey data, wargaming support, grassroots input) and a Commander Competency Checklist (指挥员能力清单) as structural enforcement mechanisms. The framing points to 政治整训 being used not just as ideological conditioning but as the lever for changing concrete planning and training behavior at the company and battalion level, though whether these procedural fixes persist beyond the campaign period remains unknown.

"Wherever the Commander Stands, There the Banner Flies"

■ PLA Daily Special Correspondent Xu Qiushi

Midsummer. This correspondent entered a command-and-control shelter on a field training site, where Battalion Commander Chen of an Air Force unit was rapidly working the control panel, issuing intercept orders. As the blips representing the missile and its target converged on schedule, a brief, firm round of applause broke out inside the shelter.

The unit's leadership acknowledged candidly that some commanders had previously "prioritized process over effectiveness" during live training, causing the command chain to link up poorly. The thoroughly transformed command style seen today is inseparable from the ideological tempering and capability reforging brought about by the deepened political rectification training (政治整训).

"If commanders don't stand at the front, how will the troops dare to charge?" An unexpected confrontation exercise, and the figures who stepped forward one after another, showed this correspondent the sense of responsibility these commanders carry—

At 0300, the unit received an emergency movement order. Commanders at every level were the first to shoulder their packs and board vehicles, leading the troops to assemble rapidly. En route, the column came under simulated enemy satellite reconnaissance; the commander made an immediate decision to adjust the march route, successfully concealing the force. Upon reaching the designated operational area, the command element immediately entered the command-and-control shelter and at the first opportunity established a layered air-defense network.

A commander's exemplary role is not confined to moments of crisis—it shows in the small details of daily life. At an equipment maintenance site, Company Political Instructor Wang Yajun lay flat on the ground alongside a crew member, troubleshooting an electronic equipment fault. His face was smeared with grease; his copy of the Equipment Operating Manual was covered in handwritten annotations, with simple circuit diagrams sketched in the margins. "When even the political instructor pushes this hard, we'd be embarrassed not to learn well," said Private First Class Xiao Li.

"I am a political instructor, but I am also a commander. If I don't understand the equipment, I can't integrate into the combat chain," Wang Yajun told this correspondent. Now that new equipment has been fielded, it has become a standing rule that commanders must "pass the test" first. Stepping into the field command post, a "Commander Competency Checklist" (指挥员能力清单) is prominently displayed, encompassing a series of combat-realistic standards including command skills, battle-position proficiency, and emergency decision-making capability.

"Commander assessments now align far more closely with actual combat," said Company Commander Sun of a certain company, who had just returned from the firing position, his camouflage uniform still stained with launcher grease. He told this correspondent that every commander must be repeatedly tested under unscripted complex environments and extreme conditions: "Whether the results are good or not, the battlefield decides."

At dusk, the stand-down whistle sounded. The command element gathered around the communications vehicle for a serious after-action review of the confrontation exercise: "Next time, establish the communications link with sister units earlier during coordination"—"Initiate anti-jamming operations sooner"—The animated debate drew in many officers and soldiers, who silently committed the commanders' words to memory.

"Wherever the commander stands, there the banner flies." This correspondent heard that phrase repeatedly throughout the visit. As night deepened, the lights in the command-and-control shelter came back on. Not far away, radar antennas rotated without pause, guarding the peaceful night sky—and guarding this spirit of responsibility and inheritance captured in the phrase "each level leading the next" (一级带着一级干).

The Change in Work Style Behind an Exercise Plan

■ Tang Chongding, Ma Lingchen, PLA Daily Special Correspondents Xu Qiushi and Wang Nan

On the northwest Gobi, night winds carrying fine sand beat steadily against the outer shell of the command-and-control shelter. Having typed the final revision, Deputy Company Commander Li Zhenlin of a battalion in an Air Force unit carefully reviewed the updated exercise plan on screen, then set aside the previous version—covered in red-ink annotations—and put it away.

"To make the exercise plan fit actual combat, we have already adjusted and refined it through more than a dozen versions," Li Zhenlin said.

The annotation marks across those pages bear witness to this plan's transformation from a "fixed script" copied from templates into a "guide to victory" (胜战指南) closely aligned with the battlefield. They also reflect the commitment of commanders at every level in the unit to anchor themselves to winning.

In recent days, this correspondent traveled deep into the desert, tracing the revision history of this operational plan in an effort to explore the ideological reforging and work-style transformation that deepening political rectification training has brought to grassroots commanders.

**Getting Clear on "What the Battlefield Needs and What the Troops Should Train"**

Li Zhenlin remembers that when he first received the battalion's assignment to draft the exercise plan, he felt entirely confident.

As a seasoned veteran, his plans had always been properly formatted, complete in all required elements, and precise in language—the battalion's universally recognized "inspection-exempt model." As the cross-regional exercise drew closer, Li Zhenlin opened his computer's "template folder" out of long habit, drew material from exercise plans of previous years, and adjusted the time, location, personnel, and tactical information for the current mission. After pulling several all-nighters, he produced a finished exercise script of more than thirty pages.

"At the time I thought: the template draft has all the elements and a smooth flow—it would at least pass the initial review without trouble, and I could fill in the on-site survey content later," Li Zhenlin said. He did not expect that this complacency would trip him up at the very first plan review meeting.

The moment the plan was projected onto the screen, it was marked up beyond recognition with red ink. The polished standard procedures and scripted language he had so carefully crafted crumbled under the questioning of those present: "What is the operational basis for the preset intercept range? Have you conducted live-equipment electromagnetic interference reconnaissance of this exercise area?" "The response to contingencies only states general principles—where are the specific handling procedures and backup plans?" "The anticipated contingencies are far too narrow. Can this plan hold up against compound jamming or a multi-target surprise attack?"

At the meeting, Li Zhenlin's knuckles went white around his pen, yet he could not answer a single question. Of the more than thirty neatly formatted, element-complete pages of the draft plan, virtually none contained actual measured data from the current mission.

At the subsequent combat-and-training review conference (议战议训会), the battalion's military political instructor (武教导员) took the lead in self-examination, holding up the red-annotated draft: "Operational requirements are the root of a plan; battlefield reality is its soul. As the political officer, I failed to run the requirements of deepening political rectification training through the entire plan-drafting process, and I failed to put 'how the war is fought is how the troops must train' (仗怎么打、兵就怎么练) into practice." He cut straight to the point: copying templates and going through the motions may look efficient, but it is a formalism (形式主义) of hollow training style—a stumbling block to combat readiness.

The political instructor's words made the grassroots commanders in the room flush with embarrassment. Li Zhenlin then placed the plan in a prominent spot on his desk, took his technical specialists into the exercise area, and collected every category of measured data. Through seven rounds of revision, they trimmed the plan from more than thirty pages to just over ten, cutting every empty phrase and stock formulation, leaving only concrete, combat-realistic measures. This time, Li Zhenlin brought the final version to the review board and, as expected, received unanimous approval.

It is understood that, to deepen political rectification training and reshape commanders' work style, the unit's Party committee established three hard criteria for plan drafting: no approval without on-site survey data; no approval without combat-realistic wargaming support; no approval without input from grassroots officers and soldiers. Drafting in isolation and copying templates are strictly prohibited, forcing commanders at every level to go down to the front line to understand the real situation, identify shortcomings, and formulate concrete measures.

"Before, I was picking at words and piecing together procedures in a cubicle, and I couldn't help feeling anxious because I had no solid foundation. Now, immersed in the actual position, I not only understand the path to fighting more thoroughly and get the plan sorted out more clearly—I also find it much easier to grasp what the battlefield needs and what the troops should train," Li Zhenlin reflected.

**Combat Confrontation Means Stepping Out of the "Comfort Zone" (舒适区) of Seeking Safety at All Costs**

Even after the exercise plan had been refined through multiple iterations, the first time it was put into practice on the ground, things still went wrong.

On the Gobi, wind and sand swirled as an intense air-ground confrontation unfolded. "Enemy aircraft" used terrain masking for a low-altitude penetration while simultaneously releasing strong electromagnetic jamming; false signals wrapped in clutter instantly flooded the radar screens. "Target detected!" Hearing the crew member's report, Company Commander Sun instinctively called out his familiar line: "Take it steady!"

What he did not expect was that this choice—which he considered prudent—would change the outcome of the engagement. On screen, before the radar could acquire the target, the "enemy aircraft" had already crossed into the edge of the engagement envelope, and they had completely lost the optimal intercept window. When the exercise ended, Sun stared at the brief but unbridgeable acquisition time gap on the screen, his mind blank.

For as long as he could remember, Sun had always sought "steadiness" above all else. "Better to be three seconds slow than to risk a single moment's danger" was a phrase he kept constantly on his lips. When organizing training, he invariably followed the plan's procedures to the letter, avoided high-risk training content whenever possible, and dispensed with extreme-condition drills whenever he could.

This time, however, Sun's "steadiness" cost him dearly. The headquarters assessment team confronted him directly: "The time lost by 'taking it steady' was enough for the target to break through the fire envelope!"

Those words hit Sun like a thunderclap. After the meeting broke up, he walked alone across the Gobi. Night wind carrying grit stung his face, and Sun came to a sudden realization: the "no mistakes" approach he had upheld for years was not prudence—it was an evasion born of fear of responsibility. To bring the exercise plan onto the battlefield, change was needed not only in the design and planning phase, but in the execution phase, where combat thinking had to be rebuilt.

"Only by turning the blade inward (刀口向内) to identify shortcomings can combat training be targeted and effective." Seizing this opportunity, the unit's Party committee organized a position-level discussion forum on "eliminating the accumulated ills of peacetime and rebuilding the performance concept oriented toward victory" (破除和平积弊、重塑胜战政绩观). Battalion and company officers took the lead in self-criticism, examining their own work deviation of "prioritizing safety over actual combat" in training and combat preparation. The forum made one point explicit: "A commander's duty has never been to monitor procedures or recite plans—it is to lead the charge against the hard points and produce real results. A force preparing for war would rather have lessons learned through bold action than safety bought through avoidance."

Sun then conducted a thorough self-reflection in front of his company's officers and soldiers and solicited their practical combat suggestions widely. He wrote that time gap in his notebook as a constant reminder. At the same time, Sun completely reversed his former training style of "seeking safety and fearing mistakes": he took the lead in high-risk subjects, repeatedly drilled extreme-condition operations, and even voluntarily requested additional training in difficult and dangerous subjects under conditions of extreme weather, compound jamming, and multi-target surprise attack.

Not long afterward, during an air-ground confrontation, the radar suddenly malfunctioned, and the troops' nerves instantly went taut. Sun did not panic. He simultaneously conducted pre-battle motivational work to steady the troops and organized his technical specialists to troubleshoot the fault, ultimately succeeding in intercepting the target.

"True responsibility is not dodging difficulties to prevent mistakes—it is being able to step up and win at the critical moment." Walking off the training ground, Sun gazed at the wind-driven sand filling the sky, his feelings complex.

Driven by the unit's Party committee, commanders at every level stepped out of the "comfort zone" of seeking safety at all costs, bringing the difficult and dangerous subjects in the exercise plan squarely onto the training ground. The troops' drive and sense of direction in preparing for war grew stronger and clearer.

**A Defeat Catalyzes a "Cognitive Upgrade" (认知升级)**

Deep in the night, the command post blazed with light. Battalion and company commanders, technical specialists, and headquarters staff officers sat around the conference table, conducting a focused discussion on the topic of "how older equipment can be integrated into new combat systems." Working through the exercise plan—now updated and supplemented through who knows how many versions—they scrutinized it sentence by sentence, refining it item by item.

It turned out that the version of the plan approved before the exercise had said very little about how older equipment should be employed within the system. In the eyes of most officers and soldiers, older equipment that had been fielded for years had fixed performance characteristics and could not keep pace with newer equipment—it was a drag. Accordingly, whenever a system confrontation was lost, the tendency was to attribute it to "the old equipment not being up to it."

Battalion Commander Chen had previously proposed detailing the tactics for employing older equipment within the system in the plan, but the idea had not gained wide acceptance. Many felt: "The performance ceiling of the old equipment is right there—it's hard to break through." Then a defeat in a system confrontation shattered everyone's habitual assumptions.

In that exercise, their new-type equipment successfully tied down the opposing force's main elements, creating an excellent intercept window. However, because the older equipment responsible for coordinated supplementary coverage could not keep pace with the system's operational tempo and failed to lock onto the target in time, they missed the optimal intercept opportunity.

The defeat left everyone deeply dispirited. Some grumbled privately: "If we had new equipment, this definitely wouldn't have happened." At the after-action review, Commander Chen cut straight to the point: "When we lose, we blame the equipment? Old equipment is not the biggest problem—outdated thinking and the unwillingness to take responsibility are!"

The room fell instantly silent. Chen picked up the plan—once again filled with red annotations—and led the self-examination: "Deepening political rectification training requires us to transform our training style and step out of the 'comfort zone.' Even older equipment cannot be isolated from the system." Those present could not help but bow their heads in reflection: for all this time, what had constrained the older equipment was not its performance, but their own conservative thinking of "not daring to break through, not willing to take responsibility." Only by breaking through cognitive boundaries could they shoulder the mission of winning.

Chen then took the lead in forming a Party-member assault team (党员攻坚小组), leading the command element and technical specialists to dig in at the Gobi training ground. With no ready-made experience to draw on, they measured data set by set, ran wargames again and again, and optimized coordinated tactics time after time. With no training conditions available, they proactively coordinated with neighboring units, repeatedly refining their approach through exercises in which they alternated as adversaries and teammates, converting unknown risks into controllable contingency plans.

Not long afterward, in a system confrontation, the opposing force launched multi-wave, multi-axis attacks with advanced equipment. The battalion's new-type combat elements were tied down, and everyone held their breath. At that moment, Chen decisively commanded the older equipment to move covertly to a preset position and, leveraging its stable advantage in medium-to-low-altitude engagement, wove a layered defensive net together with neighboring units and precisely struck the incoming targets—a clean comeback victory.

Deep in the night, the command post lights remained bright. After completing the after-action review of the day's confrontation, Chen picked up his red pen and added another line of annotation to the latest version of the plan. This exercise plan continues to be refined through each successive round of practical exploration. The commanders of this unit have never stopped expanding their capability boundaries and deepening their integration into the system.

"Only by breaking through the shackles of thinking (思维桎梏) and boldly breaking boundaries to tackle hard problems can every piece of equipment be brought to its maximum effectiveness—and on the future battlefield, can we shoulder the mission and responsibility of being able to fight and win," Chen said.

---

As the interview concluded, this correspondent was left with much to reflect on. This exercise plan—repeatedly added to and revised—reflects the unit's deepening of political rectification training and its determined effort to "establish a good example for commanders" (立起指挥员好样子). Each update to the plan discards the ideological ailments accumulated through peacetime complacency and accumulates the confidence to march toward war and win.

Not long ago, the smoke of a live exercise had barely cleared, and the comrades were all feeling pleased with themselves over a good score. During the after-action debrief, however, the officer leading the unit threw cold water on us: "Does the score achieved on the exercise ground equal actual combat capability? Will it still hold up on the real battlefield?"

The tent fell instantly silent. The leader's question suddenly brought to mind a mistake I had made during an end-of-year assessment—

At the time, I had only recently taken up my post. With the year-end military training assessment approaching, and in order to prepare for the training department's inspection of the battalion, my comrades and I pulled several all-nighters filling every logbook to the brim.

What we did not expect was that the assessment that followed exposed us completely. Breakdown operations under a tactical scenario, emergency equipment repair in unfamiliar terrain—one after another, subjects close to actual combat left everyone scrambling. "Deputy Company Commander, get over here at the double!" An angry order rang out. I set down the cable I was holding and ran, gasping, to the assessment team. The training department chief's brow was tightly furrowed as he demanded: "Does combat effectiveness show up in the logbooks, or on the exercise ground?"

In that moment, my face burned. No matter how neatly the data in the notebooks was filled in, it was not the same as real training. I had been focused on "building" combat effectiveness on paper while ignoring the smell of gunpowder on the drill ground.

A subsequent field cantonment training mission made me even more alert. That mission incorporated complex electromagnetic jamming, live-force confrontation, full-element contingencies, and multiple other conditions. The "coordinated training" that had been glossed over with a single entry in the logbook became a brutal test in combat. A single error at one battle position could knock an entire fire unit out of action. During those days, we ground ourselves down again and again on the exercise ground, going through a thousand hammerings and a hundred refinements. The sweat the officers and soldiers shed and the effort they put in left a deeper mark than any ink in a logbook.

Stepping out of that memory and looking back at this exercise, I began to understand the intent behind the leading officer's question. When the after-action review ended, I took out my training journal. Instead of rushing to fill in data, I kept turning over questions in my mind: "Can we add one more contingency to tomorrow's training? Have we truly grasped the performance parameters of the equipment? Can we handle the adversary's attack patterns?"

Those question marks serve as warning bells, driving me toward the future battlefield with a clearer and more sober mind.

**PLA Daily Special Correspondent in Dialogue with the Director of the Political Work Department of an Air Force Unit**

Since the military-wide deepening of political rectification training, how to establish a good example for commanders and become the "lead goose" (领头雁) capable of winning battles has become a critical subject for commanders at every level.

Whether a commander's work style is tough, whether their capabilities are strong, and whether their sense of responsibility is genuine directly affects the morale, fighting spirit, and actual combat capability of the force. Around this topic, this newspaper's special correspondent spoke with Director Lü of the Political Work Department of an Air Force unit.

**Correspondent:** In your view, what should a commander who can fight look like?

**Director Lü:** The most essential qualities are: politically solid, professionally capable, and daring to fight and charge. Deepening political rectification training focuses on correcting orientation and purifying loyalty (提纯忠诚). Commanders must engrave political loyalty to "following the Party's command" (听党指挥) into their bones, and resolutely correct the hollow work style of "prioritizing declarations over implementation, prioritizing process over effectiveness." They must embody the results of political rectification training on the front line of training and combat preparation—understanding both tactics and political requirements—and through the concrete actions of "standing firm, stepping up, and winning," build up the troops' confidence in achieving victory.

**Correspondent:** What thresholds must a commander cross in order to set a good example?

**Director Lü:** First, the ideological threshold: through deepening political rectification training, break the habitual thinking of "prioritizing form over actual combat" and discard the hollow mentality that "procedures in order equals work accomplished." Second, the capability threshold: commanders must understand both politics and military affairs; they must proactively address their weak points and shortcomings, take the lead in drilling skills and studying tactics, and avoid the situation of "wanting to charge but unable to, wanting to lead but unable to move the troops." Third, the responsibility threshold: use political rectification training as a lever to correct the conservative mentality of "fearing mistakes and fearing responsibility," and through the concrete action of taking the lead in charging at critical moments, transform the work-style requirements of political rectification training into a conscious commitment to combat readiness.

**Correspondent:** "To lead the way, first scout the path; to charge, I go as the foot soldier" (领路先探路，冲锋我为卒)—how should this be reflected in a commander's daily actions?

**Director Lü:** The core meaning of that phrase is "taking the lead" (带头). In training: be the first to explore new paths and drill hard skills; when new equipment is fielded, be the first to study it; be the first to tackle difficult and dangerous subjects; identify the hard points and bottlenecks in training, then lead the troops through them. In missions: be willing to serve as the vanguard—wherever the danger is greatest, charge there; wherever the stakes are highest, hold there. In daily life: claim no special privileges; consistently eat, live, and train alongside the officers and soldiers; transform a commander's self-imposed standards into a centripetal force that elevates morale.

**Correspondent:** In your view, what is the oldest tradition that commanders must never abandon?

**Director Lü:** There are many that must never be abandoned. One is the combat tradition of leading from the front and daring to fight and struggle (身先士卒、敢打敢拼). Our army's march from victory to victory is closely bound up with the outstanding tradition of commanders leading the charge and officers and soldiers sharing life and death. One of the important tasks of deepening political rectification training is to purge corrupting influences and return to fundamentals—to have commanders reclaim the traditional character of "follow me" (跟我上) and discard the slack mentality of the "hands-off manager" (甩手掌柜). In cantonment training, combat preparation, and exercise breakthroughs, commanders must take the lead in going to the front line and shouldering the hardest tasks, carrying forward the spirit of self-sacrifice exemplified by the older generation of revolutionaries who led the charge.

作为经验丰富的老骨干，他拟制的方案向来格式工整、要素齐全、语言规范，是全营公认的“免检范本”。随着跨区演练的日子越来越近，李振林按照以往习惯打开电脑里的“模板文件夹”，从历年演训方案中四处“取材”，又结合此次任务调整时间、地点、人员、战术等信息。熬了几个通宵，他最终完成一份30多页的演练脚本。 “那时我觉得，模板稿要素全、流程顺，至少能稳过初审，实地调研的内容后续再补也来得及。”李振林没想到，正是这份松懈，让他在第一次方案研讨会上栽了跟头。 方案刚投影到幕布，就被红笔勾画得“面目全非”。他精心打磨的规范流程、标准话术，在与会人员的追问下缺陷毕露：“预设拦截距离有何实战依据？结合本次演训区域的电磁环境做过实装干扰侦察吗？”“应对特情只提了原则方向，具体处置流程、备选预案在哪？”“预想特情过于单一，遭遇复合干扰、多目标突袭时，这份方案顶得住吗？” 会议现场，李振林握着笔的指节攥得发白，却一个字也回答不上来。30多页格式工整、要素齐全的方案初稿，几乎没有一项结合本次任务的实测数据。 接下来的议战议训会上，该营武教导员对着这份写满红批注的方案初稿，带头自我检视：“作战需求是方案的根，战场实际是方案的魂。作为政治主官，我没有把深化政治整训的要求贯穿到方案拟制全流程，也没有把‘仗怎么打、兵就怎么练’落到实处。”他一针见血地指出，套模板、走流程看似省事，实则是训风不实的形式主义，是备战打仗的“绊脚石”。 武教导员一番话，让在场的基层指挥员红了脸。随后，李振林把这本方案放在办公桌的醒目位置，带着技术骨干扎进演训地域，采集各项实测数据。历经7次修改，他们将30多页方案精简到10多页，删掉了所有空话套话，留下的全是实打实的实战举措。这一次，李振林带着最终版方案走上评审会，果不其然收获了全票通过。 据了解，为深化政治整训、重塑指挥员作风，该部党委专门为方案拟制定下3条硬指标：无实地勘测数据的不通过、无实战化推演支撑的不通过、无基层官兵意见的不通过。严禁闭门造车、照搬模板，倒逼各级指挥员沉到一线摸实情、找短板、定实策。 “以前在格子间里抠字眼、凑流程，心中难免因不托底而焦虑；现在泡在阵地现场，不仅把打仗路子摸得更透、方案捋得更清，也更容易搞懂战场需要什么、官兵该练什么。”李振林感慨道。 实战对抗必须走出一味求稳的“舒适区” 尽管演练方案已几经打磨，第一次落地训练时，依然出了“岔子”。 戈壁滩上，风沙漫卷，一场空地对抗激烈展开。“敌机”借地形掩护低空突防，同步释放强电磁干扰，虚假信号裹挟着杂波瞬间铺满雷达屏幕。“发现目标！”听到号手报告，某连孙连长下意识喊出那句熟悉的话：“稳着来！” 没想到，就是这个他认为稳妥的选择改变了战局。屏幕上，还没等雷达捕获目标，“敌机”已冲进杀伤区边界，他们彻底丧失了最佳抗击时机。演练结束，孙连长看着屏幕上那短暂却犹如天堑的捕获时间差，脑子一片空白。 一直以来，孙连长总是一心求“稳”。“宁慢三秒钟，不冒一分险”， 是他常挂在嘴边的话。组织训练，他永远严格按方案流程走，高风险的训练内容能避则避，极限操作能免就免。 可这次，孙连长的“稳”却吃了大亏。机关考核组当面指出：“稳一稳造成的时差，足够目标突破火力圈！” 一句话，像闷雷在孙连长头顶炸开。散会后，他一个人走在戈壁滩上。夜风裹着沙砾拍在脸上，孙连长幡然醒悟——自己坚持多年的“不出错”，不是稳妥，而是一种怕担责的逃避。要将演练方案带上战场，不仅要从设计筹划环节改变，更要在执行环节重塑作战思维。 “只有刀口向内查摆不足，练兵备战才能有的放矢。”借此契机，该部党委组织了一场“破除和平积弊、重塑胜战政绩观”的阵地研讨会，营连干部带头自我批评，检讨自己在战训方面“重安全、轻实战”的工作偏差。会上明确了一个要点：“指挥员的职责，从来不是盯流程、念方案，而是要带队攻难点、创实绩。部队准备打仗，宁要闯来的教训，不要躲来的平安。” 而后，孙连长当着连队官兵的面进行深刻反思，并广泛听取大家的实战建议。他把那个时间差写在记录本上，用来时刻提醒自己。与此同时，孙连长一改往日“求稳怕错”的训练作风，高风险课目带头练，极限操作反复磨，甚至主动申请加练以极端天气、复合干扰、多目标突袭为条件的险难课目。 不久后一场空地对抗中，雷达突发故障，官兵们瞬间绷紧神经。孙连长没有慌乱，一边开展临战动员稳军心，一边组织技术骨干排除故障，最终成功拦截目标。 “真正的担当，不是为防出错躲着困难走，而是关键时刻顶得上、打得赢。”走下训练场，孙连长看着漫天风沙，感慨万千。 在该部党委推动下，各级指挥员纷纷走出一味求稳的“舒适区”，把演练方案里的险难课目切实落到演训场，官兵备战打仗的劲头更足、方向更明。 一场失利催化“认知升级” 深夜，指挥所内灯火通明。营连指挥员、技术骨干与机关参谋围坐在会议桌旁，围绕“老装备如何适配新作战体系”专题展开研讨。大家对着那本不知更新增补到第几版的演练方案，逐句推敲、逐项打磨。 原来，演练前通过的那版方案，对于老装备的体系运用并未提及过多。在大部分官兵眼里，列装多年的老装备性能固化、跟不上新装备节奏，就是“拖后腿”的存在。因此，每次体系对抗失利，大家往往会归因于“老装备不行”。 该营陈营长曾提出，在方案里细化老装备的体系运用战法，却并未得到广泛认可。不少人觉得：“老装备性能的天花板就在那里，很难有所突破。”直到一次体系对抗失利，打破了大家的惯性认知—— 那次演练，他们的新型装备成功牵制住蓝方主力，创造了绝佳的拦截窗口。然而，由于负责协同补防的老装备没能跟上体系作战节奏，迟迟未锁定目标，他们错失了最佳抗击时机。 对抗失利，让大家十分低落，有人私下抱怨：“要是换了新装备肯定不会这样。”复盘研讨会上，陈营长的话一针见血：“失利了就怪装备吗？装备老不是最大的问题，思想守旧、不敢担当才是！” 会议室瞬间安静下来，陈营长拿起再次被红色批注填满的方案，率先检讨剖析：“深化政治整训，要求我们转变练兵作风，跳出‘舒适区’。哪怕是老装备，也不能孤立于体系之外。”在场官兵不禁低头反思，这么久以来，束缚老装备的不是性能，而是他们“不敢突破、不愿担当”的守旧思维。唯有打破认知边界，才能扛起胜战使命。 而后，陈营长牵头成立党员攻坚小组，带领指挥班子和技术骨干铆在戈壁训练场。没有现成经验，他们就一组组测数据，一遍遍搞推演，一次次优化协同战术；没有训练条件，他们主动对接友邻部队，在互为对手、互为队友的演练中反复打磨，把未知风险转化为可控预案。 不久后一场体系对抗，面对蓝方先进装备的多批次立体突袭，该营新型作战力量被牵制，所有人都捏了把汗。就在这时，陈营长果断指挥老装备隐蔽机动至预设点位，凭借中低空抗击稳定的优势，与友邻部队共同织就一张立体防御网，精准命中来袭目标，打了一场漂亮的翻身仗。 深夜，指挥所的灯光依旧明亮。陈营长复盘完白天的对抗流程，拿起红笔，在最新版方案上又添了一行批注。这本演练方案，仍在一次次实践探索中持续优化。该部指挥员拓展能力边界、不断融入体系的脚步，也从未停歇。 “唯有打破思维桎梏、勇于破界攻坚，才能让每件装备都发挥最大效能，才能在未来战场上，扛起能打仗、打胜仗的使命职责。”陈营长说。 采访告终，记者感触良多——这本反复增删的演练方案，映照着该部深化政治整训，着力“立起指挥员好样子”的求索与行动。方案的每一次更新，摒弃的是和平积弊的思想沉疴，积聚的是向战而行的胜战底气。 前不久，实战演练的硝烟尚未散去，战友们都为打了个好成绩志得意满。复盘讲评中，带队领导却给我们泼了一盆冷水：“演训场上打出来的成绩，是不是等于实战能力？上了战场还行不行？” 帐篷里瞬间陷入安静，领导的追问，让我蓦然想起自己在那次年底考核中犯的错—— 那时，我刚任职不久。年终军事训练考核在即，为了应对训练科来营检查，我和战友连熬几个通宵，把所有登记本填得满满当当。 没想到，接下来的考核，却让我们“露了底”。战术背景下撤架操作，陌生地域抢修装备，一个个贴近实战的课目让大家手忙脚乱。“副连长，跑步过来！”带着怒气的命令突然传来，我放下手中的电缆，气喘吁吁跑到考核组前。训练科科长眉头紧锁，质问道：“战斗力到底体现在登记本上，还是演训场上？” 那一刻，我脸上火辣辣的。本子数据填补得再漂亮，也不等同于真实训练。我只顾着在纸面上“构建”战斗力，却忽略了演兵场上的硝烟味。 而后的一场野外驻训任务，让我愈发警醒。那次任务融入了复杂电磁干扰、实兵对抗、全要素特情等多种情况。以往登记本上一笔带过的“协同训练”，化作战斗中的严酷考验。一个战位失误，就可能导致整个火力单元出局。那段日子，我们在演训场上反复磨合，经历千锤百炼。官兵们挥洒的汗水、付出的努力，比登记本上的墨痕更加深刻。 跳出回忆，再次回顾这场演训，我开始理解带队领导的用意。复盘结束，我拿出训练日志，没有急于填写数据，而是不停思量：“明天的训练能否再加一个特情？装备性能底数真的掌握了吗？对手的进攻样式我们能应对吗？” 问号恰如警钟，让我以更加清醒的头脑，走向未来战场。 解放军报特约记者对话空军某部政治工作部主任—— 全军深化政治整训以来，如何立起指挥员好样子、成为能打胜仗的“领头雁”，成了各级指挥员的重要课题。 指挥员作风硬不硬、本领强不强、担当实不实，直接影响着部队的士气斗志和实战能力。围绕这一话题，本报特约记者与空军某部政治工作部吕主任展开了交流。 记者：在您看来，能打仗的指挥员应该是什么样子？ 吕主任：最核心的样子，是政治过硬、本领过硬、敢打敢冲。深化政治整训，重点是校准方向、提纯忠诚。指挥员要把“听党指挥”的政治忠诚刻进骨子，坚决纠治“重表态轻落实、重流程轻实效”的虚浮作风；要将政治整训成效体现在练兵备战一线，做到既懂战术战法，又懂政治要求，用“站得稳、顶得上、打得赢”的实际行动，为部队官兵积蓄胜战底气。 记者：指挥员要立起好样子，需要跨过哪些关卡？ 吕主任：一是思想关，通过深化政治整训破除“重形式轻实战”的惯性思维，摒弃“流程到位等于工作到位”的虚功心态。二是能力关，指挥员要既懂政治又懂军事，必须主动补短板强弱项，带头练技能研战法，避免“想冲冲不上、想带带不动”。三是担当关，要以政治整训为抓手，纠治“怕出错、怕担责”的保守心态，用关键时刻带头冲锋的实际行动，把政治整训的作风要求转化为备战打仗的自觉担当。 记者：“领路先探路，冲锋我为卒”，该如何体现在指挥员的日常行动中？ 吕主任：这句话的核心要义是“带头”——训练中，要先探新路、先练硬功，新装备列装先钻研，险难课目先上手，把训练中的难点堵点摸清楚，再带着部队练；任务中，要甘当先锋，哪里最危险就冲向哪里，哪里最关键就守在哪里；生活中，不搞特权，坚持与官兵同吃同住同训，把一名指挥员的自我要求转化为激扬士气的向心力。 记者：您觉得指挥员身上最不能丢的老传统是什么？ 吕主任：最不能丢的有很多，其一就是身先士卒、敢打敢拼的战斗传统。我军从胜利走向胜利，与指挥员带头冲锋、官兵生死与共的优良作风紧密相关。深化政治整训的重要任务之一，就是肃清流毒、回归本真，让指挥员重拾“跟我上”的传统本色，摒弃“甩手掌柜”的懈怠心态。在驻训备战、演训攻坚中，指挥员要带头上一线、扛硬活，传承好老一辈革命家带头冲锋的牺牲精神。
