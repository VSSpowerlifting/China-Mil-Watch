Grassroots Dispatch | He Who Has No Soldiers in His Heart Should Not Lead Soldiers

基层传真丨心中无兵莫带兵
PLA Daily (解放军报) 28 May 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
Yuan Peng, political instructor (教导员) of a battalion in a brigade of the 83rd Group Army, has spent three years practicing 'rotational billeting' (流动住宿)—moving his bedroll unannounced into different squads and platoons each week—and PLA Daily's Grassroots Dispatch column on April 2 elevated him as a model, prompting organ cadres and troop leaders from other units to visit him directly and adapt his methods. The article documents a persistent institutional problem the PLA labels 'the difficulty of leading soldiers and the difficulty of knowing soldiers'—the gap between officers and enlisted personnel that formal channels fail to close—and the framing suggests the military press is using Yuan's case to push back against what it treats as a widespread tendency toward superficial political work (蜻蜓点水) at the grassroots level. This is standard political work content; its value is as a record of how the institution frames the officer-soldier relationship problem and the remedies it endorses, not as evidence of a new policy directive.
Translation

On April 2, the PLA Daily's Grassroots Dispatch column published a piece titled "Pack Up Your Bedroll and Move into the Squads and Platoons," reporting on Yuan Peng, political instructor (教导员) of a battalion in a brigade of the 83rd Group Army, and his three years of persisting in "rotational billeting" (流动住宿)—packing up his bedroll and living among the squads and platoons. The report sparked widespread discussion in grassroots units.

Many grassroots troop leaders said that Yuan Peng's steadfast commitment had untangled their confusion over "the difficulty of leading soldiers and the difficulty of knowing soldiers," and provided a visible, learnable example for grassroots troop-leading work in the new era. One grassroots company political officer (指导员) said during a study exchange: "We rack our brains every day over how to close the distance with officers and soldiers. Instructor Yuan's persistence tells us that as long as you put down your airs and sink your body down into the ranks, understanding what officers and soldiers carry in their hearts and winning their trust is actually quite easy."

Yuan Peng's approach also drew the attention of organs at various levels. Some organ cadres made special trips to learn about his specific deeds, held themselves up against his example to identify shortcomings in their own work, and adjusted and improved their working methods. Some units drew on the concept of "rotational billeting" to promote "cadres billeting in squads, backbone personnel embedded in platoons," providing concrete help in resolving difficulties. Other units, in light of their actual mission conditions, refined their "heart-to-heart tea talks" (谈心茶话会) and "soldier-situation candid forums" (兵情恳谈会), building platforms for officers and soldiers to speak their minds.

Yuan Peng's approach also drove a cohort of troop leaders to transform their work style and innovate their methods of leading soldiers. During this period, Yuan Peng's phone has rung frequently, as troop-leading cadres from other units have come upon hearing the news to exchange and discuss with him the specifics of how to carry out "rotational billeting," how to resolve difficulties encountered, and the experience and techniques of leading and cultivating soldiers.

In this issue, we invite Instructor Yuan to be a guest on "Grassroots Appointment" (基层有约) to explore together how to exchange sincerity for sincerity and use solid work to rally the hearts of soldiers (聚兵心), offering experience that more grassroots troop leaders can draw on and be inspired by.

Host: What was your original motivation for persisting in "rotational billeting"? What formative experiences have shaped you?

Yuan Peng: The decision to do "rotational billeting" was not a momentary impulse. It grew out of deliberation step by step, one small spark of feeling at a time. Three years ago, when I was serving as acting political instructor, I felt that every company had its own hard book to read. I held quite a few meetings and had many heart-to-heart talks, yet my words never seemed to reach the hearts of the officers and soldiers. Everyone says you have to go among the soldiers, but when I thought about actually living in the squads and platoons, I hesitated—I was afraid people would say I was putting on a show, and at the time, as acting commanding officer, I also worried that the officers and soldiers wouldn't buy into it. Later I thought it through: if I genuinely wanted to do real things for the officers and soldiers, I couldn't be afraid of being talked about. So I made up my mind and declared my intention in front of all the officers and soldiers of the battalion. After formally assuming the position of commanding officer, I kept up this habit.

When I was a platoon leader, there was a soldier who was rather introverted and sensitive. Because of his unusual family situation, he was unwilling to communicate with others. I didn't deliberately seek him out for heart-to-heart talks; I simply stayed by his side, chatted with him for a few minutes during training breaks, and talked with him before lights out. Later he came to me on his own initiative to pour out his feelings. Once the knot in his heart was untied, he became more cheerful, trained harder and harder, and eventually competed in skills competitions and achieved good results. That experience made me understand: only by bending your body down and exchanging sincerity for sincerity can you hear the most genuine voices.

After moving into the squads and platoons, I did indeed hear many things I would never have heard back at the battalion headquarters. For example, during internal affairs inspections, one soldier muttered: "Some things have nowhere to be stored, so we have to hide them—every inspection has us on edge." Another soldier said the wardrobe layout was unreasonable and that reaching for equipment always knocked his elbows. In formal settings they would never say these things—they're afraid of causing trouble, afraid of being criticized. Yet it is precisely these trivial matters that affect the training and daily lives of officers and soldiers, and that is exactly the direction in which we need to improve our work.

Host: What difficulties have you encountered over these years of "rotational billeting"? What has kept you going?

Yuan Peng: In the early period it was genuinely taxing. During the day I was coordinating the battalion's overall work and interfacing with higher organs, sometimes also attending meetings and training sessions; at night I moved into the squads and platoons, which squeezed out the time I had for organizing materials. One year, from June to August, the tasks were especially heavy—assessments and summaries came one after another without pause, and I was exhausted in body and spirit. At one point I had the fleeting thought of giving up, feeling like I was pushing down one gourd only to have another pop up—all effort and no reward.

The turning point came that September, when the veteran soldiers were discharged. Several of them took hold of me and said that the reasoning we had talked through together in the platoon rooms stayed with them to this day, that it had untied many knots in their thinking, and that they felt the barracks were especially warm. One veteran, eyes reddening, said: "Serving in this battalion, we feel at ease—we feel a sense of gain." Hearing those words, all my exhaustion dissolved like smoke. From that point on, I thought about how to carry out "rotational billeting" in a substantive way without neglecting my primary duties and responsibilities.

Gradually I worked out a staggered-schedule work method (错峰工作法). When soldiers are resting they are most relaxed, so I put down my work and accompany them—playing ball, chatting, talking about things they're interested in. In the half hour after meals, people are most likely to open up, so I listen quietly and respond patiently, without interrupting or passing judgment. After the officers and soldiers have gone to drills or to sleep and the battalion quiets down, I return to the office to handle business in concentrated bursts, improving efficiency. If there is urgent work, I handle it first and then return to the squad or platoon. Sometimes after meals the soldiers want time to themselves, and I don't deliberately intrude—I just sit nearby. With these gradual adjustments, I have basically managed to keep both work and billeting on track.

To prevent superficial engagement (蜻蜓点水), I make every effort to be fully present once I enter the squads and platoons—when spending time with officers and soldiers, I absolutely (决不) allow myself to be distracted or absent-minded. At the same time, I do not give advance notice in the process: I decide on Sunday which platoon I will stay in, ensuring that I see the most authentic picture of the grassroots. This persistence stems from a promise I made to myself and to all the officers and soldiers of the battalion. Its weight is hidden in the everyday details—making every officer and soldier feel that I am genuinely helping them resolve difficulties, not going through the motions.

Host: Now that the battalion's training results have improved significantly and the atmosphere is getting better and better, what positive role do you think "rotational billeting" has played?

Yuan Peng: I believe there is definitely a causal relationship between "rotational billeting" and the changes in the battalion, and that it has played an important role. Its role lies not in the "billeting" itself, but in closing the distance between me and the officers and soldiers, gaining a clear picture of their ideological dynamics and actual needs, and consolidating the battalion's collective strength. By living in the squads and platoons, I can grasp the ideological dynamics of officers and soldiers at the first moment, coordinate and resolve difficulties in a timely manner, and thereby stimulate everyone's enthusiasm for training and their sense of collective honor.

On one occasion, I noticed that several soldiers liked to tinker with unmanned aerial vehicles (无人机) during their rest time. After learning more about the situation, I established a UAV interest group across the entire battalion, coordinating equipment and inviting instructors… allowing their interest to be converted into capability. Not long ago, a higher-level command organized a UAV skills competition. These several soldiers prepared with full effort and ultimately achieved good results.

I also strive to ensure that every demand raised by officers and soldiers receives a response. For example, when soldiers reported that the wardrobe layout was unreasonable, I kept it in mind and pushed forward the relevant work as quickly as possible. At the time, some people raised concerns about disrupting the uniformity of internal affairs, and others felt it wasn't worth the trouble. I first coordinated with the organ, then selected one platoon as a pilot; after the adjustment, soldiers all said it was more convenient, and within one week the change was rolled out across the entire battalion. This matter shows that as long as you are genuinely acting for the good of officers and soldiers, you need not be stumped by problems.

Yuan Peng: The greatest gain has been polishing the original aspiration (初心) of grassroots work and understanding that only by sinking down to the front line can you get a feel for the real situation. There are no shortcuts in grassroots management. Regardless of which method of leading soldiers you choose, as long as you hold the attitude of genuinely serving the soldiers and working with a sincere heart—sinking down, blending in, treating officers and soldiers as family, treating their affairs as your own—you can manage the unit well and lead soldiers well, and you can make the battalion's atmosphere increasingly upright and the relationships among officers and soldiers increasingly harmonious.

I place greater value on "work below the horizon" (地平线以下的工作)—the small things that are invisible and intangible yet can warm soldiers' hearts and rally soldiers' spirit (暖兵心聚兵气). In my mind, the political achievements (政绩) of Party member cadres are not numbers on a report form or slogans on a wall, but rather doing real things for officers and soldiers in a concrete way—making everyone aware that when they encounter difficulties there is an organization to turn to, that when they have something on their minds there is someone to listen, that when they have opinions and suggestions there is someone who takes them seriously—so that they feel they are growing and have something to look forward to.

Of course, living in the squads and platoons is not the only way to manage the grassroots, and still less is it a "prescribed movement" (规定动作) that must be performed. There are many methods of leading soldiers: some people are skilled at heart-to-heart talks, some are good at blending guidance into training, some can close the distance with officers and soldiers through everyday acts of care. As long as these methods are carried out with genuine sincerity and done in a solid manner, they are equally capable of managing the unit well and leading soldiers well.

Yuan Peng: I feel that leading soldiers requires passing through "three checkpoints" (三关). The first is the identity checkpoint (身份关): put down your official airs and regard yourself as a member of the squad or platoon. The second is the mindset checkpoint (心态关): listen to complaints without getting angry, and treat the opinions of officers and soldiers as opportunities for improvement. The third is the self-discipline checkpoint (自律关): do not let busyness and fatigue cause arbitrary interruptions—persist over the long term and influence and inspire officers and soldiers through your own words and deeds.

Flipping through the notebooks from these three years, I can see both the demands and the day-to-day details of officers and soldiers, and it also allows me to quietly review and reflect. If there is one most important lesson from leading soldiers, it is this: "Sincerity is always the ultimate weapon (真诚永远是必杀技)." Getting along with officers and soldiers requires no fancy techniques—as long as you treat people with genuine sincerity and handle matters in a concrete, down-to-earth way, they will trust you. I often tell my comrades: there is no true failure in life—you either gain success or you gain growth. The pursuit of those who lead soldiers should not be limited to their own political achievements, but should be to make more soldiers become more excellent and more confident because of our efforts. Every time I see them make progress, I am happier than when I myself receive commendation.

Original Chinese
4月2日，解放军报基层传真版刊登了《打起背包住班排》稿件，报道了第83集团军某旅某营教导员袁鹏任职3年来，坚持“流动住宿”、打起背包进班排的经历。该报道在基层部队引发热议。 不少基层带兵人谈到，袁鹏的这份坚守，解开了大家“带兵难、知兵难”的困惑，也为新时代基层带兵工作提供了看得见、学得来的好例子。一位基层指导员在学习交流时说：“我们天天琢磨怎么跟官兵拉近关系，袁教导员用自己的坚持告诉我们，只要把架子放下来、身子沉下去，读懂官兵内心、赢得官兵信任是很容易的事。” 袁鹏的做法同样引起各级机关的关注。一些机关干部专程前来了解他的具体事迹，对照梳理自身工作不足，调整改进工作方法。有的单位借鉴“流动住宿”的思路，推行“干部住班、骨干蹲排”，实打实帮助解决困难；还有的单位结合任务实际，完善“谈心茶话会”“兵情恳谈会”，为官兵搭建说心里话的平台。 袁鹏的做法还带动了一批带兵人转变工作作风、创新带兵方法。这段时间，袁鹏的电话时常响起，不少其他单位的带兵干部闻讯而来，与他一起交流探讨“流动住宿”具体怎么开展、遇到难题怎么化解，以及带兵育人的经验技巧。 本期，我们邀请袁教导员做客《基层有约》，一起探讨如何用真心换真心、用实干聚兵心，给更多基层带兵人提供可借鉴、可启发的经验。 主持人：您坚持“流动住宿”的初衷是什么？什么样的成长经历影响着您？ 袁鹏：“流动住宿”这个决定，不是一时心血来潮，是一步步琢磨、一点点触动产生的。3年前，我做代理教导员工作，感觉每个连都有本难念的经，会开了不少、心也谈了很多，可话总说不到官兵心里。都说要走进战士中间，可想到住班排时也犹豫，怕别人说我作秀，而且我当时作为代理主官，也曾担心官兵不买账。后来想通了，既然想为官兵办实事，就不能怕被议论，于是下了决心，在全营官兵面前表了态。正式担任主官后，这样的习惯也一直坚持下来。 我以前担任排长时，有名战士比较内向敏感，因家庭情况特殊不愿与人沟通。我没有刻意谈心，只是陪在他身边，训练间隙聊几句，熄灯之前说说话。后来他主动找我倾诉，心结解开后，人也开朗了，训练越来越刻苦，后来还参加比武，取得了好成绩。这次经历让我明白，只有身子俯下去，用真心换真心，才能听到最真实的声音。 住进班排后，确实听到了很多在营部听不到的话。比如检查内务，有战士念叨“有些东西没地方放，只能藏起来，每次检查提心吊胆”；还有战士说衣柜布局不合理，取装具总磕胳膊。这些小事在正式场合他们不会说，怕麻烦、怕被批评。可恰恰是这些琐碎事影响官兵训练生活，这也正是我们改进工作的方向。 主持人：“流动住宿”这几年遇到过哪些困难？是什么让您坚持了下来？ 袁鹏：初期确实比较吃力。白天统筹全营工作、对接机关，有时还要开会集训，晚上住进班排，整理材料的时间就被挤占了。有一年6月到8月任务特别重，考核、总结连轴转，身心俱疲。当时一度萌生了放弃的念头，觉得“按下葫芦起来瓢”，费力不讨好。 转机是那年9月老兵退伍。几名老兵拉着我说，平时在排房里聊的道理他们至今都记得，解开了不少思想疙瘩，觉得军营特别温暖。一名老兵红着眼眶说：“能在这个营当兵，我们觉得很安心，有获得感。”听到这话，所有疲惫都烟消云散。从那时起，我就琢磨怎么既不耽误主责主业，又把“流动住宿”落到实处。 慢慢地，我摸索出错峰工作法。休息时战士最放松，我就放下工作，陪他们打球、唠嗑，聊他们感兴趣的事；饭后半小时大家最容易吐露心声，我就静静倾听、耐心回应，不打断不评判；等官兵操课或就寝后，营里安静下来，我再回办公室集中处理业务，提高效率。如果有紧急工作就先处理完再回班排，有时饭后战士想自己待着，我也不刻意打扰，就在一旁坐着。这样一点点调整，基本做到了工作、住班两不误。 为防止蜻蜓点水，我努力做到进班排就全身心投入，和官兵相处时决不分心走神、心不在焉。同时流程上不搞提前通知，周日临时确定住哪个排，保证看到最真实的基层。这份坚持，源自我自己、也是对全营官兵的承诺。它的分量就藏在日常点滴里——让每名官兵感到我是真心实意帮他们解难，不是走过场。 主持人：如今全营训练成绩大幅提升、氛围越来越好，您觉得“流动住宿”起到了什么积极作用？ 袁鹏：我觉得，“流动住宿”和营队的变化，肯定是有因果关系的，而且起到了重要作用。它的作用不在于“住”本身，而是拉近了我和官兵的距离，摸清了官兵的思想动态和实际需求，凝聚了营队的合力。通过住班排，我能第一时间掌握官兵思想动态，及时协调解决困难，从而激发大家的训练热情和集体荣誉感。 有一次，我发现有几名战士平时休息时总爱捣鼓无人机，了解情况后，我便在全营范围内成立无人机兴趣小组，协调器材、邀请指导……让他们的兴趣能转化为本领。前段时间，上级组织无人机项目比武，这几名战士全力备战，最终取得了不错的成绩。 官兵的诉求我也力求件件有回应。比如战士反映衣柜布局不合理，我就记在心上，尽快推动相关工作。当时曾有人提出担心影响内务统一，还有人觉得没必要折腾。我先找机关沟通，再选一个排试点，调整后战士都说方便，然后一周内全营推广完成。这件事说明，只要真心为官兵好，就不怕被问题难住。 袁鹏：最大的收获是擦亮了基层工作的初心，明白了只有沉到一线才能摸到实情。基层管理没有捷径，不管选择哪种带兵方式，只要抱着真心为兵、实心干事的态度，沉下去、融进去，把官兵当亲人，把他们的事当自己的事，就能管好队伍、带好兵，就能让营队风气越来越正，官兵关系越来越融洽。 我更看重“地平线以下的工作”——那些看不见、摸不着，却能暖兵心聚兵气的小事。在我心里，党员干部的政绩不是报表上的数字、墙上的口号，而是实实在在为官兵办事，让大家意识到遇到困难有组织、心里话有人听、意见建议有人重视，从而觉得有成长有奔头。 当然，住班排并不是管理基层的唯一方式，更不是必须要做的“规定动作”。带兵方式有很多种，有人擅长谈心谈话，有人善于在训练中融入引导，有人能通过日常点滴关怀拉近与官兵的距离，这些方式只要投入真心、做得扎实，同样能管好队伍、带好兵。 袁鹏：我感到，带兵需要过“三关”。第一是身份关，放下官架子，把自己当成班排一员；第二是心态关，倾听抱怨不生气，把官兵意见当成改进契机；第三是自律关，不被忙和累随意中断，长期坚持，用自身言行影响感召官兵。 翻看这3年的笔记本，既能看到官兵的诉求和点滴，也能让我静心复盘。若说最重要的带兵体会，那就是“真诚永远是必杀技”。和官兵相处不需要花哨技巧，只要你真心待人、实实在在办事，他们就会信任你。我常跟战友说，人生没有真正的失败，要么收获成功，要么收获成长。带兵人的追求，不应只是自己的政绩，而应是让更多战士因为我们的付出变得更优秀、更自信，每次看到他们进步，我比自己受表扬还开心。
