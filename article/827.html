Exercises Doctrine

Measuring Training Details Against the Yardstick of Actual Combat: Conditions for Adversarial Training Must Be Grounded in Real War

对标实战标尺衡量训练细节，设置对抗条件应以实战为“据”
PLA Daily (解放军报) 29 May 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
A brigade of the 71st Group Army used combined-arms tactical training to surface a recurring Blue Force design flaw: obstacle belts sized for maximum length rather than calibrated to actual troop strength, terrain, and fire integration — producing unrealistic difficulty that masked Red Force command-and-control and coordination shortfalls rather than exposing them. The article documents an internal corrective cycle in which Blue Force commanders, prompted by after-action criticism, adopted war-gaming, battle-case study, and Red-Blue role-reversal methods to derive training conditions from Red Force capability gaps rather than from a 'harder is better' default. The framing — built around the 从难从严 ('train hard and strictly') standard and the 磨刀石 ('whetstone') metaphor — points to a recognized institutional problem with Blue Force professionalism that PLA Daily is using this unit's experience to address across the force.
Translation

How Long Should an Obstacle Be? Actual Combat Has the Final Say

■ PLA Daily Special Correspondents Song Shijie, Jiang Yucheng, Yan Zichang; Illustration by Xie Yan

"Company Commander, do we go or not?"

In the height of summer, a certain brigade of the 71st Group Army organized combined-arms tactical training. Before breaching a lane, Red Force commander Company Commander Qian hesitated as he reviewed intelligence relayed back from reconnaissance — the depth of the Blue Force's forward obstacle belt had been shortened by nearly one-third compared to previous exercises.

In the past, to delay Red Force attacks, Blue Force had mostly followed the principle of "set them as long as possible" when emplacing obstacles. The current situation was decidedly abnormal. Was it an "oversight" or a "trap"?

After a moment's deliberation, Company Commander Qian decided to seize the opportunity: "Breach according to the plan, quickly, and reinforce security to guard against a surprise attack."

Because the forward obstacle depth had been significantly reduced, the Red Force engineer element, under fire cover, successfully breached the lane in only half the planned time.

However, the moment they completed the breach, Blue Force surged forward and struck. Red Force ultimately prevailed by virtue of numerical superiority, but the assault was fiercely contested and "casualties" exceeded expectations.

"Every time we attack, breaching is a major problem — so why did a shorter obstacle make the attack harder?" The hard-won victory naturally prompted Company Commander Qian to conduct a thorough after-action review. Recalling the details of the engagement, he quickly identified the reason: compared to previous exercises, their breaching time had indeed been reduced considerably, but after penetrating into depth, small Blue Force elements repeatedly harassed them from multiple directions, and fire strikes were far more precise than before, making it difficult for Red Force to control the tempo of the engagement throughout.

"In this exercise, we emplaced obstacles based on our actual troop strength. Although the obstacle distance was shortened, we consequently had more forces available to commit to subsequent phases of the engagement." When exchanging views with Blue Force commander Company Commander Yin, the latter's words confirmed Qian's hypothesis. Yin candidly acknowledged that this shift in thinking had originated from an earlier "victory."

In that adversarial training exercise, Blue Force had constructed defensive works in advance and emplaced an obstacle belt nearly 5 kilometers long across Red Force's axis of attack. The approach was "highly effective" — right up until the exercise ended, Red Force was still struggling with the breach and had no capacity to address subsequent tasks.

Yet this "victory" drew considerable skepticism at the after-action review: "On a real battlefield, would you actually have the conditions to construct obstacles on such a large scale?" "You can't ignore reality and go to extremes — setting difficulty for difficulty's sake." Staff Officer Yang, who had participated in exercise control on multiple occasions, cut straight to the heart of the matter: "The battlefield has no rules, but there are certain operational principles that cannot be violated."

He offered examples: the width of Red Force's attack frontage should be determined by Red Force's attacking strength — you would not normally use a company's worth of troops to attack a defensive frontage held by a battalion; likewise, Blue Force, constrained by troop strength, time, and terrain, must integrate obstacle emplacement tightly with fire support arrangements, troop dispositions, and terrain features to form an organic whole — it is absolutely not a matter of simply pursuing maximum obstacle length.

"The length of an obstacle requires weighing multiple factors. Blindly pursuing 'boundless' obstacles may appear to be 'training hard and training strictly' (从难从严), but in reality it already violates the requirements of actual combat." The words of the brigade's operations and training section chief left a lasting impression on Company Commander Yin.

"As a 'whetstone' (磨刀石), the prerequisite for setting difficulty is to simulate the enemy's real situation to the greatest extent possible. The purpose of setting difficulty is to help Red Force identify shortcomings and improve capabilities. If you ignore reality in pursuit of a so-called 'victory' and blindly create situations that rarely occur in real combat, you have lost sight of the fundamentals." Company Commander Yin acknowledged that the experience prompted them to reexamine their own responsibilities.

Since then, they have regularly organized tactical war-gaming and battle-case study sessions, strictly simulating based on actual troop strength, and have stopped setting "out-of-scope problems" (超纲题) that amount to "setting difficulty for difficulty's sake." They have adhered to the practice of working backward from Red Force capability shortfalls to derive training subjects, taking "train what Red Force lacks" as the starting point for adversarial design, and reducing ineffective difficulty that is divorced from actual combat. They have explored a "Red-Blue role reversal" training method, deepening understanding of the "whetstone" function through role switching, and cultivating a cohort of personnel who understand the adversary and understand how to fight.

"In this exercise, Red Force's breaching time was greatly reduced compared to before, but shortfalls in command and control, battlefield awareness, and tactical coordination were exposed far more fully." Reviewing the "engagement," Company Commander Yin's words carried a note of "honorable defeat" (虽败犹荣): "On the future battlefield, these could all be decisive factors. Helping Red Force identify problems in advance — that is our 'victory.'"

The brigade's leadership told reporters that in adversarial training, the question of how long an obstacle should be has no definitive answer — but more important than that answer is the reflection and discussion it has prompted among the brigade's officers and soldiers: how should the Blue Force "whetstone" sharpen Red Force combat power?

Commentary

"Training Hard and Strictly" Must Have a Basis

■ Yang Meng

In the past, some Blue Force commanders easily fell into the misconception that "the longer the obstacle, the better." In fact, obstacle length does not straightforwardly equate to adversarial difficulty — extending an obstacle is intended to delay the enemy's attack tempo, trading space for time; shortening an obstacle is intended to hold forces in reserve and lure the enemy in deep, wearing the enemy down through a contracted defensive line. Neither approach is absolutely superior to the other.

It must be clearly understood that the fundamental prerequisite for "training hard and strictly" (从难从严) in setting adversarial conditions is to benchmark against actual combat. "Training hard and strictly" must have a "basis" (据), and that "basis" is the "yardstick" (标尺) of actual combat. If, in order to "stump" Red Force, you create extreme situations that would be virtually impossible to encounter on a real battlefield, you have divorced yourself from actual combat and are being "difficult for difficulty's sake" (为难而难), deviating from the fundamental purpose of helping Red Force test operational methods and temper capabilities. The value of what a certain brigade of the 71st Group Army's Blue Force did in shortening the obstacle depth during training lies precisely in breaking the fixed mindset of "difficulty for difficulty's sake."

More important than answering "how long should the obstacle be" is translating actual-combat thinking into operational actions that can be known and felt. As the requirements for realistic training continue to rise, every echelon must at all times use the yardstick of actual combat to measure training details, and must genuinely recognize that winning or losing in training is not the end point — fighting a battle and advancing one step further is the ultimate objective.

Original Chinese
障碍设多长，实战说了算 ■解放军报特约记者 宋世杰 蒋雨铖 严子昌 谢 岩绘 “连长，打不打？” 盛夏时节，第71集团军某旅组织综合战术训练。开辟通路前，红方指挥员千连长看着侦察回传的情报有些犹豫——蓝方前沿障碍纵深距离与过往相比，缩短了近三分之一。 以往，为迟滞红方进攻，蓝方设置障碍时，大多“能设多长就设多长”，当下情况颇为反常，是“疏忽”还是“圈套”？ 思索片刻，千连长还是决定抓住战机：“按照预案快速破障，同时加强警戒，防止偷袭。” 因为前沿障碍纵深大幅缩短，红方工兵分队在火力掩护下，仅花费了一半的预定时间便成功开辟通路。 然而，他们刚刚完成破障，蓝方便一拥而上，实施打击。最终，红方凭借兵力优势艰难取胜，但进攻过程十分胶着，并且“伤亡”数量超出预期。 “每次进攻，破障都是个难题，怎么这次障碍变短了，进攻反而变难了？”胜利来之不易，千连长自然要好好复盘。回想战斗细节，他很快找到原因——相较以往，他们的破障时间确实缩短不少，可进入纵深后，蓝方的小股兵力从多个方向反复袭扰，火力打击也远比以往精准，导致整个进攻过程，他们一直难以掌握战斗节奏。 “此次对抗，我们根据实际兵力设置障碍。虽然障碍距离缩短，但也因此有了更多兵力投入到后续对抗之中。”与蓝方指挥员尹连长交流时，对方的话验证了千连长的猜想，尹连长坦言，这种观念的转变，源于此前的一场“胜仗”。 那次对抗训练，蓝方提前构筑防御工事，在红方进攻正面设置了近5公里长的障碍。这一做法“成效显著”，直到训练结束，红方还苦于破障，根本无暇顾及后续任务。 然而，这场“胜利”在复盘会上却引来颇多质疑：“真实战场上，真的有条件构筑这么大范围的障碍吗”“不能忽略实际走极端，为了设难而设难”……多次参加导调工作的杨参谋更是一针见血指出问题关键：“战场无规则，但有些作战原则是不能违背的。” 他举例说明，比如红方进攻正面距离应由红方进攻力量来决定，一般不会用一个连的兵力来进攻一个营的防守正面；再如蓝方受制于兵力、时间、地形等因素，障碍设置必须与火力配系、兵力部署、地形特点紧密结合，形成有机整体，绝非单一追求距离长短…… “障碍的长短需要综合考量多种因素，盲目追求‘漫无边际’，看似‘从难从严’，实则已经违背实战要求。”该旅作训科科长的话，让尹连长至今记忆犹新。 “作为‘磨刀石’，我们设难的前提，是要最大限度模拟‘敌军’真实情况。设难的目的，是帮助红方发现短板、提升能力。如果为了所谓的‘胜利’忽略实际，盲目设置一些真实作战中很少出现的情况，就成了舍本逐末。”尹连长坦言，那次经历让他们重新审视自身职责。 此后，他们定期组织战术推演和战例研讨，严格按照兵力实际进行模拟，不搞“为设难而设难”的“超纲题”；坚持从红方能力短板出发反推训练课题，把“红方缺什么就练什么”作为对抗设计出发点，减少脱离实战的无效难度；探索“红蓝互换”训练方式，在角色转换中深化对“磨刀石”职能的理解，培养一批懂对手、懂打仗的“明白人”。 “此次演练，红方破障时间较以往大大缩短，但在指挥控制、战场感知、战术协同等方面的短板暴露得更加充分。”回顾这场“战斗”，尹连长的言语中带着几分“虽败犹荣”，“未来战场上，这些都可能是决定胜负的关键。帮助红方提前发现问题，才是属于我们的‘胜利’。” 该旅领导告诉记者，对抗训练，障碍应该设置多长，这个问题没有明确答案，而比这个答案更加重要的，是该旅官兵由此引发的思考与讨论——蓝方这块“磨刀石”，该如何“磨”出红方战斗力？ 短评 从难从严需有“据” ■杨 猛 过往，部分蓝方指挥员很容易陷入“障碍越长越好”的误区。事实上，障碍长短并不完全等同于对抗难易——将障碍设长，是为了迟滞“敌”进攻节奏，以空间换时间；将障碍缩短，则是为了兵力后置、诱“敌”深入，通过收缩防线与“敌”展开周旋，两者没有绝对的优劣之分。 要清楚认识到，“从难从严”设置对抗条件的基本前提，是对标实战。从难从严需有“据”，这个“据”就是实战这把“标尺”。如果为了“难住”红方，设置出真实战场上几乎不可能出现的极端情况，便成了脱离实战、“为难而难”，偏离了帮助红方检验战法、锤炼能力的根本目的。第71集团军某旅蓝方在训练中缩短障碍纵深距离的可贵之处，正在于打破“为难而难”的思维定式。 比回答“障碍设多长”更重要的，是将实战思维转化为可知可感的作战行动。随着实战实训的要求越来越高，各级必须时刻用实战标尺衡量训练细节，切实认清训练胜负不是终点，打一仗进一步才是最终目的。
