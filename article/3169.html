Ten Thousand Mountains Cannot Stop the Resolute March — There Is a Force Called "Spurring the Horse Forward Without Dismounting"

千山万壑拦不住脚步铿锵，有一种力量，叫“快马加鞭未下鞍”
PLA Daily (解放军报) 12 August 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
A PLA political education essay published around Army Day (August 1) uses Long March mountain-crossing episodes — Loushan Pass, Jiajinshan, Liupanshan — and Mao's battlefield poetry to frame the current force's mission as an unbroken relay from 1927 to the present, closing with Xi Jinping's injunction that each generation must walk its own Long March. This is standard political work content oriented toward ideological consolidation; its value is as a record of how the institution links historical sacrifice to present-day 'strong military' (强军) obligations for junior officers and soldiers, not as evidence of a new policy line or operational development.
Translation

More than 90 years ago, in that desolate autumn, a lightly clad column marched into the vast and boundless mountains. Behind them were enemy forces in hot pursuit; beneath their feet were treacherous passes fit only for birds and goats; ahead were cliffs as sheer as blades. Silent natural barriers, by the arrival of this column, became the march route, became the battlefield, became the passes of life and death — and after the three armies had crossed, they were transformed into a spiritual benchmark.

From October 1934 to October 1936, the First, Second, and Fourth Front Armies of the Red Army and the 25th Army carried out the great Long March. They traversed more than ten provinces, fought more than 600 engagements and battles against the enemy, crossed nearly one hundred rivers, and scaled dozens of high and treacherous mountain peaks. The Central Red Army alone crossed more than 20 mountain ranges along the way. The Yuechengling, Tiannanshan, Fodingshan, Daloushan, Wumengshan, Jiajinshan, Mengbishan, Minshan, Liupanshan… mountain after mountain, danger after danger, stretching on without end, with no finish in sight.

Yet those cold, lifeless names on a map had warmth breathed into them by pairs of iron feet running with blood, were sublimated into spiritual totems one after another, and were strung together into a chain of signposts marking victory!

Guizhou's Daloushan range lies sprawled across the land like a crouching azure dragon, with layer upon layer of cliffs and peaks rising in sheer walls — it was here that the Battle of Loushan Pass was fought. "The west wind blows fierce; across the vast sky wild geese cry in the frosty morning moonlight." This was the first great victory on the Long March route; the Red Army actively waged a war of maneuver to seize local initiative, laying the foundation of confidence for the later strategic turning point. After storming Loushan Pass, Mao Zedong rode to the top of the pass, gazed out over the battlefield where the smoke of gunfire was slowly clearing, and with a sweep of his brush wrote that stirring ci-poem "Recalling Qin'e · Loushan Pass." Though his words depicted the perilousness of the mountains and the ferocity of the fighting, what surged in his chest was the heroic spirit of "the pass is iron-hard, yet today we stride across it anew" (雄关漫道真如铁，而今迈步从头越).

The trials posed by nature were no less severe than the brutal ferocity of combat against the enemy. Jiajinshan, at an elevation of more than 4,000 meters, is blanketed in snow year-round, and its weather is wildly unpredictable. "Jiajinshan, Jiajinshan — birds cannot fly over it, no mortal can climb it; to cross Jiajinshan, only an immortal could come down to earth" — this folk rhyme is a true portrait of its forbidding environment. Yet beneath the feet of the brave, a road to victory always unfolds, a road pounded firm by faith and fearlessness. Marching over the mountains, Red Army officers and soldiers trod on snow hard and cold as stone, silently bidding farewell to one comrade after another who fell by the roadside, while the bright battle flags remained raised high through howling blizzards. The warriors who fell in the bone-piercing cold wind had already become immortal "sculptures," signposts for those who came after to press forward one upon another. Here, there were no grand words — only silent, tragic heroism; no ornate epitaphs — only spines that would bend before they would break.

Gazing at these unbroken ranges of mountains that seemed as though they could never all be crossed, Mao Zedong, while on the march, wrote three stirring short verses (小令):

What stands three chi and three from heaven is the mountain — and even more, the upright, towering bodies of the Red Army officers and soldiers; what overturns seas and rivers is the mountain — and even more, the surging heroic spirit of the Red Army officers and soldiers locked in fierce battle; what pierces the blue sky is the mountain — and even more, the fighting will of the Red Army officers and soldiers, growing stronger with every setback.

Ten thousand ranges of mountains and passes — the higher the mountain, the greater the person who stands atop it. Though the mountains are treacherous, they cannot stop the resolute march; though the winds are fierce, they cannot scatter the resounding songs of triumph.

Liupanshan was the last high mountain crossed by the Central Red Army on the Long March. Standing atop Liupanshan, Mao Zedong gazed out to the farthest reaches of the undulating, layered mountains; wild geese flew south in formation; the blazing red flag "burned" at the mountain's summit, unfurling freely in the face of the west wind — victory lay just ahead! Not long after, he put brush to paper and wrote that widely recited ci-poem: "The sky is high, the clouds are pale; we watch the wild geese fly south to the horizon. If we fail to reach the Great Wall we are not true men — counting on our fingers, we have already marched twenty thousand li."

At that moment, the mountains were still those same mountains, but this column, having passed through a hundred battles and ten thousand li of marching dust, carried an even more resolute ideal and conviction, and was more invincible than ever. "Wind and rain soak our clothes and harden our bones; wild vegetables fill our stomachs and steel our will; officers and soldiers share hardship as one, and the revolutionary ideal soars higher than the sky." In the darkest hour of the Chinese nation, the Red Army officers and soldiers ground their flesh and blood into every inch of earth beneath their feet, carved their convictions into every mountain ridge, and in the moment when heaven itself seemed about to fall, grew into the pillar holding it up. What they crossed was not only the towering, overlapping ranges that spanned heaven and earth, but also the ten-thousand-fathom chasm of old China's deep poverty and weakness, and the humiliating pass of the Chinese nation's century of decline. Those thousands of mountains and valleys that were conquered became spiritual cornerstones inscribed into the memory of the nation. This column, which fought to the death for a revolutionary ideal higher than the sky, used bodies of flesh and blood to set the Chinese nation on the grand journey of great rejuvenation.

Today, the mountains remain as majestic as ever. Atop Liupanshan, the red flag rolls in the west wind; at the foot of Jiajinshan, visitors come in an endless stream. As we traverse the span of history and look back at the long road this column walked, what we see are the figures of generation after generation relaying the climb over the mountains.

On August 1, 1927, the crack of gunfire atop the walls of Nanchang announced the birth of the people's army. From that moment on, this column's march has never ceased. From the snow-capped mountains and grasslands of the Long March, to guerrilla warfare behind enemy lines beneath the Taihang Mountains; from the thousand-li leap forward into the Dabie Mountains, to the tunnel defense on Shangganling (Triangle Hill); from the thunderclap on the Gobi of Lop Nor, to the wall of flesh and blood against raging floods; from the deep-blue wake in the Gulf of Aden, to guarding the mountains and rivers of the Karakoram… This column did not stop at any single mountaintop, nor did it halt at any particular moment in history, but has always fixed its gaze on "the other side of the mountain." The mountains change, the enemy changes, the mission changes — but the posture of "spurring the horse forward without dismounting" (快马加鞭未下鞍) has never changed, and the spine that "pierces the blue sky with a blade still unbroken" (刺破青天锷未残) has never bent.

"Every generation has its own Long March road; every generation must walk its own Long March road well." The Commander-in-Chief's earnest exhortation, like the drumbeat of an advancing army, resounds in the heart of every officer and soldier of the new era.

The Long March of the new era is an undertaking of pioneering significance, formidable difficulty, and great complexity; surging waves and crashing breakers test every officer and soldier of this column. Only by channeling personal ideals into the great cause of building a strong nation and a strong military, by standing at the crest of the wave of transformation, and by remaining unyielding in a complex environment, can the red answer sheet of a century of marching be transformed into an enduring monument of the age that does not fail the people.

The wind rises; the red flag soars high. This heroic column, treading the land of the motherland and bearing the hopes of the nation, once again sets out in full readiness, once again advances into the mountains — toward the direction pointed by the Party flag, toward the place where the people's gaze is fixed, toward the goal of the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation —

Original Chinese
90多年前，那个萧瑟的秋天，一支衣衫单薄的队伍走进了茫茫群山。身后是围追堵截的敌军，脚下是鸟道羊肠的险隘，前方是陡峭如刃的绝壁。静默的天堑，因为这支队伍的到来，成了征途、成了战场、成了生死存亡的关隘，三军过后更化作了一种精神的标高。 1934年10月至1936年10月，红军第一、第二、第四方面军和第二十五军进行了伟大的长征。他们纵横10余个省份，同敌人进行了600余次战役战斗，跨越近百条江河，攀越数十座高山险峰。单是中央红军，一路翻越的山峰就有20余座。越城岭、天南山、佛顶山、大娄山、乌蒙山、夹金山、梦笔山、岷山、六盘山……山连着山，险接着险，绵延不断，看不到尽头。 然而，这些在地图上一个个冷冰冰的名字，却在一双双流淌着鲜血的铁脚板下注进了温度，升华为一座座精神的图腾，连成了一串串胜利的路标！ 贵州的大娄山，山势横亘如苍龙盘卧，重崖叠峰，峭壁绝立，当年娄山关战斗就在这里打响。“西风烈，长空雁叫霜晨月。”这是长征路上的首次大捷，红军积极实施运动战赢得局部主动，为后来的战略转折奠定了信心和基础。攻克娄山关后，毛泽东策马登上险隘，望着硝烟渐去的血火战场，挥毫写下了那首荡气回肠的《忆秦娥·娄山关》，笔下刻画的虽是山的险要、战斗的惨烈，胸中翻涌的却是“雄关漫道真如铁，而今迈步从头越”的豪迈。 比起与敌厮杀的残酷激烈，大自然的考验威胁毫不逊色。夹金山，海拔4000多米，终年积雪不化，气候变幻无常。“夹金山，夹金山，鸟儿飞不过，凡人不可攀。要想越过夹金山，除非神仙到人间”，这首民谣正是对其险恶环境的真实写照。然而，勇敢者的脚下永远铺展着一条通往胜利的路，这路是用信仰和无畏夯筑而成的。翻山行军中，红军将士踩着冷硬如石的积雪，默默送别路边一个个倒下的战友，鲜亮的战旗在狂风暴雪中始终高扬。在刺骨寒风中倒下的一位位勇士，已然化作了不朽的“雕塑”，成为后来者前仆后继的路标。在这里，没有豪言壮语，只有无声的悲壮；没有华丽的墓志铭，只有宁折不弯的脊梁骨。 望着这些连绵不断、似乎永远也翻不完的山岭，毛泽东在行军中曾写下了三首壮怀激烈的小令： 离天三尺三的是山，更是红军将士昂然屹立的身躯；倒海翻江的是山，更是红军将士激战尤酣的豪情；刺破青天的是山，更是红军将士愈挫愈勇的斗志。 关山万千重，山高人为峰。山虽险，拦不住脚步铿锵；风虽恶，吹不散凯歌嘹亮。 六盘山是中央红军长征途中翻越的最后一座高山。站在六盘山顶，毛泽东极目远山叠嶂起伏，大雁成行南飞，夺目的红旗“燃烧”在高山之巅，迎着西风肆意舒卷，胜利就在前方！不久后，他挥笔写下了那首广为传诵的词作：“天高云淡，望断南飞雁。不到长城非好汉，屈指行程二万。” 此刻，山还是那群山，这支队伍却在历经百战余烬、万里征尘后，有了更为坚定的理想和信念，更加不可战胜。“风雨浸衣骨更硬，野菜充饥志越坚；官兵一致同甘苦，革命理想高于天。”在中华民族的至暗时刻，红军将士把血肉碾进脚下的每一寸泥土，把信念刻进每一道山脊，在天欲堕之时成长为赖以拄其间的砥柱。他们跨越的，不仅是横亘天地的重峦叠嶂，更是旧中国积贫积弱的万仞绝谷和中华民族百年沉沦的屈辱关口。那些被征服的千山万壑，化作一块块载入民族记忆的精神基石。这支为高于天的革命理想殊死战斗的队伍，用血肉之躯推动中华民族走上了伟大复兴的恢宏征途。 今天，群山依旧巍峨。六盘山巅，红旗漫卷西风；夹金山下，游人络绎不绝。我们穿越历史时空，回望这支队伍走过的漫漫长路，看到的是一代又一代人接力翻越的身影。 1927年8月1日，南昌城头的那声枪响，宣告了人民军队的诞生。从那时起，这支队伍的脚步就从未停歇。从长征路上的雪山草地，到太行山下的敌后游击；从大别山的千里跃进，到上甘岭的坑道坚守；从罗布泊的戈壁惊雷，到洪涛恶浪的血肉长城；从亚丁湾的深蓝航迹，到喀喇昆仑的护卫山河……这支队伍没有停在某座山顶，也没有止于某段历史，而是始终将目光定格在“山的那一边”。山在变，敌在变，使命在变，但他们“快马加鞭未下鞍”的姿态从未改变，“刺破青天锷未残”的脊梁从未弯曲。 “每一代人有每一代人的长征路，每一代人都要走好自己的长征路。”统帅的殷切嘱托，如催征的鼓点，激荡在新时代的每一名官兵心中。 新时代的长征，是具有开创性、艰巨性、复杂性的事业，惊涛骇浪考验着这支队伍的每一名将士。唯有将个人的理想汇入强国强军伟业，在变革的浪潮中勇立潮头，在复杂的环境中坚韧不拔，才能把百年行军的红色答卷化成不负人民的时代丰碑。 风起了，红旗高扬，这支英雄的队伍脚踏着祖国的大地，背负着民族的希望，又一次整装出发，又一次挺进群山，向着党旗所指的方向，向着人民凝望的地方，向着中华民族伟大复兴的目标——
