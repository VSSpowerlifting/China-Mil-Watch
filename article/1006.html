About this analysis
Summary
Four soldiers from a brigade under the 72nd Group Army — Staff Sergeant Lan Sixing, Sergeant Zhu Qingxi, Sergeant Li Kaiyan, and Staff Sergeant Li Jun — simultaneously passed the military physical fitness 特一级 (Special Grade One) evaluation under a newly revised Military Physical Fitness Training Syllabus (军事体育训练大纲) that caps basic fitness scores at 100 points per event and adds combat physical fitness (战斗体能) and practical skills (实用技能) as mandatory components. The article documents how the revised syllabus exposed a structural flaw in the old system: soldiers could earn Special Grade One by accumulating bonus points in a single strong event, masking deficits in endurance, load-bearing transitions, and close-quarters skills that the new continuous-operations format now tests directly. The framing points to a deliberate institutional effort to discredit 'grinding individual events' as a training philosophy and replace it with weakness-targeted, feedback-driven preparation — though whether this company's approach has been adopted at scale across the 72nd Group Army or beyond remains unaddressed.

Four Comrades from the Same Company Pass "Special Grade One"

■ Guo Enjian, PLA Daily Reporter Xiang Liming

In the mountainous region of central Zhejiang, the morning mist had yet to clear when the sound of hurried footsteps was already echoing across the training ground of a brigade under the 72nd Group Army.

Staff Sergeant (Second Class) Lan Sixing gasped as he crossed the finish line of the track, with Sergeant Zhu Qingxi close on his heels. The two immediately moved without pause to the strength training area. Kettlebell deadlifts, medicine ball forward pushes, weighted lunge walks — these were all supplementary exercises they had added to address weaknesses in their physical conditioning.

"Before, we only focused on the 3,000-meter run and pull-ups. Now we have to bring up our overall capabilities." At last month's practical skills assessment, the two had paid a price — their core strength gave out, and their pace dropped noticeably in the second half.

Not far away, the muffled thud of grappling came from the mats. Sergeant Li Kaiyan was repeatedly practicing prisoner-capture techniques with Staff Sergeant (Second Class) Li Jun. Throat lock, force application, controlled takedown — by the end of one sequence, both men were breathing hard.

"Stop — your hip drive is still a little off." Li Jun jabbed a finger into Li Kaiyan's side. "When you locked me just now, your core wasn't tight, so I broke free right away." Li Kaiyan nodded and readjusted his stance. Prisoner capture has never been a showy technique; what matters is the fraction of a second in close-quarters engagement when one person gains control over the other.

Not long ago, Lan Sixing, Zhu Qingxi, Li Kaiyan, and Li Jun — four comrades from the same company — simultaneously passed the physical fitness "Special Grade One" (特一级) at a superior-level military physical fitness special-grade evaluation.

"The certificate is in my pocket, but training has to go on." As Lan Sixing spoke, freshly torn calluses on his palms were still seeping blood. "'Special Grade One' is a mirror — it reflects the true distance between training and actual combat."

"After the new edition of the Military Physical Fitness Training Syllabus (军事体育训练大纲) took effect, the standards for special-grade evaluation were given new specifications, with increased weighting for combat physical fitness (战斗体能) and practical skills (实用技能)," said Li Kaiyan. In other words, simply running fast or doing the most pull-ups is now outdated. Whether one can continuously complete multiple events under combat conditions, and whether one can convert physical fitness into skills that are useful on the battlefield — that is what the new measuring standard is truly designed to gauge.

Behind the four of them, more and more figures are joining this training ground. On the track and through the obstacle course, training changes benchmarked against the new Syllabus and oriented toward actual combat are quietly taking place with each passing training day.

Military Physical Fitness "Special Grade One" Has a New Measuring Standard

■ Guo Enjian, Lu Dongyu, Liu Hongjie, PLA Daily Reporter Xiang Liming

A reconnaissance element of a brigade under the 72nd Group Army conducts simulated helicopter-borne landing training. Photo by Zhong Zhihao.

**The era of "grinding" high scores on individual events to earn "Special Grade One" is over**

"Still a few seconds short — looks like this training method is wrong." Staring at the freshly posted results of a simulated combat physical fitness assessment on the electronic screen, a breathless Li Jun tore the bib number from his chest.

Although he already holds "Special Grade One," Li Jun knows clearly that his current scores still fall short of actual combat requirements.

Back in the barracks, Li Jun retrieved the new Military Physical Fitness Training Syllabus and confirmed the new standards once more: the basic physical fitness score, previously uncapped, has been adjusted to a ceiling of 100 points per individual event, and two new components — combat physical fitness and practical skills — have been added to the special-grade evaluation criteria, placing greater emphasis on comprehensive physical conditioning. "The new standard is a new measuring standard. What it measures is not the threshold for 'Special Grade One,' but the 'Special Grade One' capability to take the field in actual combat," said Li Jun.

Under the old system for earning "Special Grade One," every pull-up beyond the full-score threshold earned an additional point; Li Jun alone had "ground out" 34 extra points on that single event. Now, after carefully studying the new Syllabus, he understands that the era of earning "Special Grade One" by grinding high scores on individual events is over.

When preparing for the old assessment, Li Jun's logic was simple: set "extreme targets" for himself — daily check-ins of 200 pull-ups, armed 6-kilometer runs, and other above-standard training, not even sparing rest time, all in order to lock in the bonus-point "dividends" from his strongest events.

After the new Syllabus requirements were released, what gave Li Jun the most headaches was the 300-meter position-capability combination exercise (300米岗位能力组合练习) — which integrates physical fitness, skills, and tactical movement. It demands both speed and strength, and in several simulated assessments he lost pace in the final 50-meter shuttle run due to exhaustion.

Change was painful. Li Jun scrapped his training plan and started over, breaking the "combination exercise" down into component movements such as barbell hang cleans and weighted carries, and weaving them into his daily training. While others stretched after their runs, he practiced shuttle runs carrying ammunition boxes; while others rested, he studied how to correct his force-generation posture. After several months, the "final 50 meters" that had once cost him pace became his "golden 50 meters" for gaining time.

"From 'Special Grade One' to actual combat, I still have to keep 'grinding' — grinding new methods, grinding new standards," said Li Jun.

In the company, many others are experiencing the same "harshness" of battlefield requirements as Li Jun. Sergeant Wang Guanjun used to coast through the entire company on the strength of his single pull-up specialty; now, faced with the continuous operations of combat physical fitness in the new syllabus, his problem of poor physical resource allocation is fully exposed. Sergeant Xu Jinyang can run the 3,000 meters comfortably under 10 minutes, but repeatedly falls behind in the second half of armed cross-country runs.

The company quickly introduced a new intensive training program: instead of a one-size-fits-all collective run, it established a closed-loop training record for each officer and soldier built around "weak-event identification + intensive methods + phased assessment."

"The military physical fitness training in the company now has a much stronger combat flavor — genuinely raising overall quality to actual combat standards." Li Jun, having just finished a cooperative carry exercise, lay on the ground gasping for breath, reflecting on the current state of "pain and joy."

After the new Syllabus took effect, new requirements — including a maximum threshold for basic physical fitness scores and increased weighting for combat physical fitness and practical skills — struck directly at Li Jun's "pain points" as a "long-distance running ace."

"Even though the assessment is harder, I feel like I've become more well-rounded!" said Li Jun. Now that the new standards are right in front of him, he has no choice but to confront his weaknesses head-on and hurry to "make up his homework." Watching the scores for events that once "dragged him down" climb one by one into the excellent range, he trains with even greater drive.

"In the past, building high scores by grinding numbers was, at its core, a disconnect between individual effort and the realities of troop training," said Political Instructor Wang Kunyang. Today, what officers and soldiers compete over on the training ground is no longer whose individual event looks "better," but whose comprehensive capability is more "outstanding."

**"Measuring" the gap between capability and actual combat; "cutting away" the empty formalism of hollow drills**

Staff Sergeant (Second Class) Lan Sixing is the most senior soldier among the four who passed "Special Grade One" this time. When preparing for the assessment, faced with the question "You've got 12 years in — why are you still pushing this hard?", the introverted soldier found himself momentarily at a loss for words.

The release of the new Syllabus made his previous "substitute running for all training" approach "out of step with the times." For example, he can run the armed 5-kilometer in under 22 minutes, but his 30-meter serpentine run has hovered at the "good" level due to unstable center-of-gravity control; he can knock out 50 pull-ups with ease, but once he enters the continuous operations of combat physical fitness, his body's transition speed cannot keep up with the pace.

"Just run first — once you're moving, the wind picks up, and maybe it'll blow the answer your way." During that period, Lan Sixing trained daily on events including the armed 5-kilometer cross-country, variable-pace runs, vaulting horse exercises, and pull-up combination exercises. But this traditional "brute-force, cast-a-wide-net" approach to training brought him only frequent injuries and stagnant scores.

Eventually, the company commander identified the problem. After several discussions and experiments, the company commander worked with Lan Sixing to develop a personalized plan of "targeted weakness remediation and scientific training (靶向补弱、科学训练)."

To improve the continuity of load-bearing operations in combat physical fitness, Lan Sixing broke his training down into a combination package of "25-meter shuttle run + ammunition box carry + in-place strength training," repeatedly refining the transition segments. To correct a single movement, he set up equipment to record himself and compared the footage frame by frame against instructional videos, continuously improving his takeoff posture. One month later, his combat physical fitness event scores reached the excellent level.

"Soldiers sprinting toward 'Special Grade One' are benchmarking against actual combat, closing in on actual combat, and integrating into actual combat under the requirements of the new Syllabus." Seeing Lan Sixing's progress, the company commander realized that each soldier's strengths are different, and a one-size-fits-all training model cannot effectively raise collective performance. The company therefore combined the new Syllabus standards with individualized training methods, tailoring a training plan for each officer and soldier — "measuring" the gap between capability and actual combat, and "cutting away" the empty formalism of hollow drills.

Private First Class Zheng Jiawei is tall with long arms but has weak core strength; in pull-up assessments he always "swings without rising." The plan the company designed for him: reduce the number of pure pull-up repetitions and increase core training such as planks and hanging leg raises. Three weeks later, not only did his pull-up scores meet the standard, but his parallel-bar dips also broke his personal record. "I never expected that training volume going down would actually make my scores go up!" said Zheng Jiawei, delighted.

Sergeant Zhu Qingxi also benefited. He has good explosive power but poor endurance, and repeatedly failed to reach the excellent level in the second half of the full-kit 200-meter obstacle course due to exhaustion. The full-kit 200-meter obstacle course is an event unique to reconnaissance soldier assessments; compared to the 400-meter obstacle course, it eliminates the 200-meter shuttle run but requires participants to wear full combat dress. Although he increased the time and repetitions devoted to specialized training such as pull-ups and variable-pace runs, the results were minimal and improvement was limited.

Chen Wenjie, the company's military physical fitness backbone (军体骨干) with experience from military academy study, quickly identified the flaw in Zhu Qingxi's training method: the full-kit 200-meter obstacle course requires not just physical fitness, but the comprehensive combat capability to "pass various obstacles precisely, rapidly, and safely even under extreme fatigue." After adopting a targeted "embedded" (嵌合式) training approach, Zhu Qingxi continued to complete obstacle crossing, climbing, and other strength training while in the fatigued state induced by variable-pace runs, and his physical conditioning improved substantially.

**Only when everyone trains according to the logic of the battlefield will the value of "Special Grade One" be elevated**

Private First Class Cai Jianhao still remembers the scene of the company lining up to welcome back the squad leaders who had passed "Special Grade One" — among the four, his squad leader Li Jun and deputy squad leader Lan Sixing were both included.

When Cai Jianhao first joined the company as a new soldier, his training scores were not outstanding — he barely passed the 3,000-meter run and could only manage a few pull-ups. In weighted training especially, he would always lose pace midway through carrying a dummy.

Li Jun walked him through the instructional video segment by segment: on screen, the demonstration soldier carried the dummy in a sprint — turning, switching hands, charging — every movement clean and crisp. "On the battlefield, no one waits for you to adjust your center of gravity," Li Jun said, pointing at the screen. "Your problem isn't that you lack strength — it's that your center of gravity shifts during the turn, so you can only gut it out in the second half."

Li Jun broke down the "three-phase force generation method (三段发力法)" he had developed while preparing for the special-grade assessment: legs for the start, hips for the middle, arm swing for the sprint. Then he had Cai Jianhao follow along — once, twice, three times — until Cai Jianhao completed a full run without losing pace.

This kind of "one-on-one," or even "one-on-many," mentoring has become increasingly common in the company.

Zhu Qingxi leads a group of new soldiers in strength training, sharing not only his own training journal but also drafting individual training plans for them. Lan Sixing leads several new soldiers with weaker endurance or coordination in specialized long-distance running and obstacle training, often using his own rest time to answer their questions.

Leading others forward means staying out in front yourself. Lan Sixing is always the first to arrive at the training ground each day, pushing every set to failure, and when leading his squad and platoon in climbing drills, he climbs through the course himself several times first.

The company's officers noticed this change. The political instructor said at a meeting: "In the past when we talked about the 'lead goose effect' (头雁带飞), we always thought it meant having the top performers lead a few more drills and do a few more demonstrations. Now look at these four — what they're leading is not just technique, but standards. Holding a special grade and still pushing yourself hard in training — that does more than any lecture ever could."

The company quickly institutionalized this "lead goose effect." Starting from the four "Special Grade One" holders, they gradually established a mentoring chain of "top performers lead backbone personnel, backbone personnel lead all members." The training ground no longer follows the old model of "one instructor calls out, the whole company trains together," but is instead dispersed into a number of small groups, each with its own training focus — the pace and atmosphere are tighter and more substantive than before.

Growth and progress are visible to the naked eye. In a subsequent comprehensive physical fitness general assessment, the overall excellent-and-good rate rose by nearly ten percentage points compared to the previous one. The more obvious change is on the training ground itself — in the past, when training time came, the duty officer blew the whistle to assemble, gave unified commands, and everyone did the same movements: what to train and how much was the same for the whole company. Now it is different: some are doing shuttle runs on the track with water bags, some are doing step-ups, hexagonal jumps, and other supplementary training, some are repeatedly working through the details of the serpentine run around the poles.

"From 'I know' to 'I can do it,' there is a long road in between. These four are walking out in front, and the comrades behind them, watching their backs, know how to walk it," the company commander reflected. "In the past, the company drove training with the duty officer's whistle and the backbone personnel's voices. Now it runs on 'lead figures' — you don't need to call out; they're already out there training, and the comrades alongside them naturally follow."

The value of the "lead goose" is not to run alone out front, but to engrave the actual-combat measuring standard into every individual's training. Only when everyone trains according to the logic of the battlefield will the value of "Special Grade One" be elevated.

**"Training harder" is no match for training smarter**

■ Zhang Wenjie

When Lan Sixing and the other three signed up to participate in the superior-level military physical fitness grade evaluation assessment, I happened to be assigned to the same training group as them. At first, I assumed that all four of them had solid foundations and that passing the special grade was a foregone conclusion. But after training alongside them for a few days, I found that their training methods were completely different from before — no grinding for volume, no piling on hours; every day they were benchmarking against the new standards to find weaknesses and refine details.

What left the deepest impression on me was a moment of being "stuck" during one training session.

That day the company organized full-kit 200-meter obstacle training. When I reached the high wall, I fell back into my old problem — I cleared it but landed unsteady, pitching forward. This had troubled me for a long time; in every assessment it was where I lost time. During the break, Li Kaiyan walked over: "When you go over the obstacle, your core isn't tight and your legs drive too early."

He had me stand next to the high wall and broke the movement down for me: "After you jump, first tuck your abdomen and press against the wall; wait until your center of gravity is stable, then push off and flip over. You were doing it exactly backwards just now." I followed his instructions for a few repetitions, and sure enough it felt much cleaner.

"Just that one movement — I drilled it hundreds of times while preparing for the special grade," said Li Kaiyan, wiping sweat. "I used to think you could just power through on fitness alone, but once the new Syllabus tested continuous operations, every weakness was fully exposed." That day when training ended, I didn't rush back to the barracks — instead I stayed on the training ground and ran through the obstacle movements a few more times.

Later, when the company established the "weak-event identification + intensive methods + phased assessment" training program, I was the first to sign up. Pull-ups were weak, so I trained hanging leg raises the way Zhu Qingxi taught me to build core strength. I was losing pace in the second half of the armed 5-kilometer, so I followed Lan Sixing's approach of breaking training into a "combination package" of speed plus strength.

The results showed quickly. One month later in an obstacle assessment, my completion time was nearly 10 seconds faster than before. Even more valuable was a shift in mindset: when I encounter a new problem, I no longer panic. In the past, my first reaction to a problem was "I haven't trained hard enough" — add volume, add repetitions, train to failure. Now I carefully analyze: Where is the problem? What does the syllabus require? What specialized training exists for the weak point? As they say, "Think it through before you act, and you'll get twice the result with half the effort."

"Training harder" is no match for training smarter. I watched, learned from, and adjusted alongside the training methods these four used while preparing for the assessment, and in the end I tasted the rewards myself. The certificates are in their hands, but I "brought back" something too: a more scientific training habit.

**Measure against the new standard; answer the new examination paper well**

■ Han Chengzhu

Four soldiers from the same company simultaneously passing the military physical fitness "Special Grade One." What makes this noteworthy is not only that the achievement itself was hard-won, but also that it functions like a mirror, reflecting the deeper direction of military physical fitness training reform in the new era: how exactly does physical fitness connect to the battlefield? What is it that truly bridges training and actual combat?

Since the newly revised Military Physical Fitness Training Syllabus was implemented, the threshold for "special grade" evaluation has risen substantially. A perfect score in basic physical fitness is no longer the finish line — it is the starting line. Combat physical fitness, practical skills, and continuous operation capability have become the new hard requirements. This means the old path of "excelling in individual events to win on total score" no longer works, and the era of simply coasting on accumulated experience and natural talent is passing. That a single company could achieve multiple simultaneous breakthroughs under such stringent standards carries significance far beyond the realm of physical fitness — it reflects a collective turn in training philosophy, and a grassroots unit's conscious response to actual-combat requirements.

In the past, the same logic prevailed across many domains — physical fitness, marksmanship, tactics, communications, driving: train the individual events you are good at to the extreme, and use one or two strong suits to mask overall weaknesses. In physical fitness, be satisfied with "running fast"; in marksmanship, be satisfied with "shooting accurately"; in specialty skills, be satisfied with "knowing how to operate." This way of thinking creates an illusion: that the battlefield will allow you to do only what you are good at. The new Syllabus does not accept that illusion. The change in special-grade evaluation criteria is also a microcosm of military training standards being pried open — shifting every training domain from "grinding data" to "building real capability (练实功)," from "testing individual events" to "testing continuity," from "I know I can" to "I don't know what's coming next, but I can handle it."

How is the wall between training and actual combat actually torn down? Many people's first instinct is to add events, add difficulty, add scenario context. But the real answer may be more straightforward — make training itself unpredictable. When a training plan is no longer a fixed schedule of "what we train this week, what we train next week," but is instead continuously adjusted based on each person's weaknesses, each week's feedback, and phased changes; when assessments are no longer back-to-back, sequential, and prepared-for, but are instead continuous, cross-cutting, and subject to sudden rule changes — then training is truly prepared for combat. The bridge connecting training to actual combat is not built by adding a few "actual-combat events"; it is built by making every training plan "come alive" — with feedback, with correction, with unpredictable variables.

This is why this company's "Special Grade One" is worth examining: because they inadvertently demonstrated something. When a unit begins to take seriously the logic of the new syllabus — not applying a one-size-fits-all approach, not pursuing individual-event extremes, not avoiding weaknesses — what it reaps is not merely a few training standouts, but an upgrade of an entire training ecosystem. Physical fitness is only the first stone to surface above the water; beneath the surface, the entire riverbed of military training is shifting.

How to connect? How to bridge? The answer is not in documents, and not in slogans. It will only emerge, slowly, in the actions of those who dare to break with inertia and make training "come alive."

“现在连队的军事体育训练战味更浓了，实打实按照实战要求提升全面素质。”刚完成协作搬运的李俊，趴在地上大口喘着粗气，感叹着当下的“痛并快乐”。 新《大纲》施行后，基础体能设定最高阈值、战斗体能和实用技能权重增加等新要求，直击“长跑高手”李俊的“痛点”。 “虽然考核难度增大了，但感觉自己变得更全面了！”李俊说，现在新标准摆在眼前，自己不得不直视短板，抓紧“补作业”。看着电子屏上那些曾经“拖后腿”的课目成绩一点点跳到了优秀，他练得更起劲了。 “以往靠刷数据来塑造高分，本质上就是个人努力与练兵实际的脱节。”指导员王坤阳说，如今，官兵们在训练场上比拼的不再是谁的单项更“好看”，而是谁的综合能力更“突出”。 “量”出能力与实战的差距，“裁”掉务虚不求实的假把式 二级上士蓝思星是这次考取“特一级”的4名士兵中兵龄最长的一位。备考时，面对“兵龄都12年了，还这么拼干啥”的疑问，内向的他一时竟不知怎么回答。 新《大纲》的出台，让之前“以跑代练”的笨办法变得“不合时宜”——比如，他武装5公里能跑进22分钟，但30米蛇形跑因为重心控制不稳，始终在良好水平徘徊；他单杠能轻轻松松上50个，可一旦进入战斗体能的连贯作业，身体转换速度就跟不上节奏。 “先跑，跑起来就有风了，或许就把答案吹来了。”那段时间，蓝思星每天要练习武装5公里越野、变速跑、木马练习、单杠组合练习等课目。但这种“蛮干硬练、广撒网式”的传统训练方式，给他带来的是频繁伤痛和毫无起色的成绩。 后来，连长看出了问题。几经商议和试验，连长带着蓝思星共同制订了一份“靶向补弱、科学训练”的个性化计划。 为了提升战斗体能中负重作业的连贯性，蓝思星把训练拆解为“25米折返跑+弹药箱搬运+原地力量训练”的组合套餐，反复打磨转换环节；为了纠正一个动作，他架起设备记录并和教学视频逐帧对比，不断改善起跳姿态。一个月后，他的战斗体能课目成绩达到了优秀。 “战士们向‘特一级’冲刺，就是在新《大纲》要求下对标实战，靠近实战，融入实战。”看到蓝思星的进步，连长意识到，每名战士的优长是不一样的，“一刀切”的训练模式并不能有效提高集体成绩。于是，连队结合新《大纲》标准，采取个性化训练方式，为每一名官兵量身定制训练计划，“量”出能力与实战的差距，“裁”掉务虚不求实的假把式。 上等兵郑嘉威，身高臂长但核心力量弱，单杠考核中总是“晃而不起”。连队为他设计的方案是：减少单纯拉杠的次数，增加平板支撑、悬垂举腿等核心训练。3周后，他不仅单杠成绩达标，就连双杠臂屈伸也突破了个人记录。“没想到训练量‘下去’了，成绩反而提高了！”郑嘉威开心地说。 从中受益的还有中士朱庆曦。他爆发力好但耐力差，实装200米障碍后半段多次因为体力不支没有达优。实装200米障碍是侦察兵考核特有的课目，与400米障碍相比，少了200米的折返跑，但考核人员需着战斗着装参加考核。尽管他增加了引体向上、变速跑等专项训练的时间和次数，还是收效甚微，提升有限。 连队军体骨干陈文杰凭借院校学习的经验，很快指出了朱庆曦训练方法的漏洞：实装200米障碍需要的不仅仅是体能，更是“在极度疲劳下，依然能精准、快速、安全通过各种障碍”的综合战斗能力。针对性采取“嵌合式”的训练方式后，朱庆曦在变速跑带来的疲劳状态下继续完成跨越障碍、攀爬等力量训练，他的体能得到了大幅度提升。 当每个人都按照战场的逻辑去练，才会提升“特一级”的含金量 上等兵蔡剑豪至今记得连队夹道欢迎考取“特一级”的班长们归来的场景——4个人里，他的班长李俊和副班长蓝思星都在里面。 新兵下连时，蔡剑豪的训练成绩并不突出——3000米跑勉强及格，单杠也拉不了几个。特别是负重训练，他搬运假人时总会中途掉速。 李俊带他一段段分析教学视频：画面里，示范兵扛着假人跃进，转身、换手、冲刺，每个动作干净利落。“战场上没人等你调整重心”，李俊指着屏幕说，“你的问题不是力气不够，是转身那一下重心偏了，后半程只能硬撑。” 李俊把自己备战特级时总结的“三段发力法”拆给他看：起步用腿、中段靠腰、冲刺接摆臂。然后让他跟着做，一遍、两遍、三遍……直到蔡剑豪完整跑完一趟没有掉速。 这种“一带一”，甚至“一带多”的场景，在连队里越来越常见。 朱庆曦带着一部分新兵进行力量训练，他不仅把自己的训练日志分享给新兵，还为他们制订个人训练计划；蓝思星带领几名耐力或协调性较弱的新兵进行长跑和障碍训练专项强化，常常利用自己的休息时间为他们答疑解难。 带着别人进步，自己更要冲在最前面。蓝思星每天总是第一个到达训练场，每组动作做到力竭，带着班排练攀登的时候，自己先爬个好几遍。 连队干部注意到了这个变化。指导员在会上说：“过去我们讲‘头雁带飞’，总觉得是让尖子多带几次操，多做几次示范就行了。现在看看他们4个，带的不只是动作，更是标准。拿了特级还坚持训练上跟自己较劲，这个比任何说教都管用。” 连队很快就把这种“头雁效应”变成了制度。他们从4个“特一级”开始，逐步建立了“尖子带骨干，骨干带全员”的帮带链条。训练场上不再是“一个教练喊、全连一起练”的老模式，而是分散成若干个小组，每个小组都有自己的训练重点，节奏和氛围比过去更紧更实。 成长进步肉眼可见。在之后的一次全面体能普考，整体优良率比上一次提高了将近一成。更明显的变化在训练场上——以前到了训练时间，值班员吹哨集合、统一下口令、统一做动作，练什么、练多少全连一个样。现在不一样了，有人扛着水袋在跑道上来回折返，有人在做踏阶、六边形跳跃等辅助训练，有人围着蛇形跑杆反复抠细节。 “从‘我知道’到‘我做到’，这中间隔着一条很长的路，他们4个走在前面，后面的同志看着背影，就知道怎么走了。”连长感慨道，连队以前抓训练，靠的是值班员的哨子和骨干的嗓门，现在靠的是“领头”——你不需要喊，他们自己在那里练，旁边的同志自然就跟上了。 “头雁”的价值，不是独自领跑，而是把实战化的标尺刻进每个人的训练里。当每个人都按照战场的逻辑去练，才会提升“特一级”的含金量。 “练得狠”不如练法新 ■张雯杰 蓝思星他们4人报名参加上级的军事体育等级评定考核时，我正好跟他们分在同一个训练小组。起初，我认为他们4个人底子都好，考取特级是板上钉钉的事。可跟训几天后，我发现他们的练法跟以前大不一样——不拼数量、不堆时间，天天对着新标准找短板、抠细节。 让我印象最深刻的，是一次训练中的“卡壳”。 那天连队组织实装200米障碍训练，我跑到高墙时犯了老毛病——翻过去落地不稳，身体往前栽。这问题困扰了我很久，每次考核都是在这里耽误了时间。休息时，李凯炎走过来：“你过障碍时核心没收紧，腿发力太早。” 他让我站到高墙边，把动作拆开给我看：“起跳后先收腹、贴墙，等重心稳了再蹬腿翻。你刚才正好反了。”我照着做了几遍，果然利索了很多。 “就这一个动作，我备考时练了上百遍。”李凯炎擦着汗说，“以前也觉得靠体能硬冲就行，但新《大纲》一考连贯作业，短板就全暴露了。”那天训练结束，我没急着回宿舍，而是留在训练场把障碍的动作又过了几遍。 后来连队建立“短板课目+强化手段+阶段验收”的训练方案，我第一个报了名。引体向上不行，就按朱庆曦教的办法练悬垂举腿补核心；武装5公里后半程掉速，就学蓝思星把训练拆成速度加力量的“组合套餐”。 效果很快显现。一个月后障碍考核中，我通过的时间比之前快了将近10秒。更让我受益的是一种心态上的转变，遇到新难题我不慌了。以前遇到难题，第一反应是“练得不够狠”，加量加次数，练到力竭为止。现在我会仔细分析：问题出在哪？大纲是怎么要求的？针对弱项的专项训练有哪些？就像他们说的“想清楚再干，事半功倍”。 “练得狠”不如练法新。他们4人备考的训练方法，我跟着看、跟着学、跟着改，最后自己也尝到了甜头。证书在他们手里，但我也“揣回”了一样东西：一种更科学的训练习惯。 对照新标准，答好新考卷 ■韩成珠 一个连队4人同时考取军事体育“特一级”。这件事情之所以引人注目，不仅在于成绩本身来之不易，更在于它如同一面镜子，映照出新时代军事体育训练改革的深层指向：体能与战场，究竟如何连接？训练与实战，到底靠什么打通？ 新修订的《军事体育训练大纲》实施以来，“特级”评定的门槛大幅提高。基础体能满分不再是终点，而是起点；战斗体能、实用技能、连贯作业能力成为新的硬杠杠。这意味着，过去那种“单项冒尖、总分取胜”的老路走不通了，单纯靠吃老本、拼天赋的时代正在过去。一个连队能在如此严苛的标准下实现多人同时突破，其意义远远超出了体能范畴——它折射的是一种训练理念的集体转身，是一支基层部队对实战化要求的自觉回应。 过去，体能、射击、战术、通信、驾驶等很多领域都曾流行过同一种逻辑——擅长的单课目练到极致，用一两个强项掩盖整体短板。体能上满足于“跑得快”，射击上满足于“打得准”，专业上满足于“会操作”。这种思维会让人产生一种错觉：战场会允许你只做擅长的事。新《大纲》不认这个错觉。特级考评标准发生变化，同时也是军事训练标准被撬动的缩影——让每一个训练领域都从“刷数据”转向“练实功”，从“考单项”转向“考连续”，从“我知道我能”转向“我不知道接下来会发生什么，但我能应对”。 训练与实战之间的墙，到底怎么推倒？很多人第一反应就是加课目、加难度、加背景。但真正的答案可能更朴素——让训练本身失去可预测性。当一份训练计划不再是“这周练什么、下周练什么”的固定表格，而是根据每个人的短板、每周的反馈、阶段的变化不断调整的时候；当考核不再是背对背、按顺序、有准备，而是连贯、交叉、冷不丁换规则的时候，训练就是真正为作战而准备。打通训练与实战的那座桥，不是靠几个“实战化课目”搭起来的，而是靠让每一份训练计划都“活”起来，有反馈、有修正、有不可预测的变量。 所以，这个连队的“特一级”之所以值得琢磨，是因为他们无意中示范了一件事：一个单位开始认真对待新大纲的逻辑，不搞一刀切、不追求单课目极限、不回避短板，收获的就不会只是几个训练尖子，而是一整套训练生态的升级。体能只是最先浮出水面的那块石头，水面之下，整个军事训练的河床都在变动。 怎样连接？怎样打通？答案不在文件里，也不在标语里，它只会在那些敢于打破惯性、让训练“活”过来的行动中，慢慢浮现。
