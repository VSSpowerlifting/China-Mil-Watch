Over 70 Years of Time, He Gave All the Light and Heat of His Life to the Aviation Cause

About this analysis
Summary
Yao Zhicheng, a 96-year-old metallographic analysis expert at AVIC Shenyang Aircraft Corporation (the former State-Owned Factory No. 112), has worked the same inspection post for more than 70 years and continues to report daily despite three bone fractures and three decades past mandatory retirement age. His career documents two specific cases where his metallographic judgments — on J-6 primary load-bearing components in 1971 and on main-beam defects in Hubei-supplied steel — overturned proposals to ground or scrap large batches of fighter aircraft, with the 1971 decision reaching Premier Zhou Enlai for approval. This is a political-work profile timed to his nomination as a National Outstanding Communist Party Member candidate; its value is as a record of how AVIC and PLA-adjacent defense industry media frame individual technical dedication and institutional loyalty, not as evidence of a capability development or policy shift.

A Profile of Yao Zhicheng, Metallographic Analysis Expert at AVIC Shenyang Aircraft Corporation——

Seventy-Plus Years of Microscopic Exploration: Where There Is Will, There Is a Way

■ Tian Bo

Recent photograph of Yao Zhicheng. Photo by Chen Yang

On a recently publicized list of candidates for commendation as National Outstanding Communist Party Members, there is one veteran Party member who has spent a lifetime living out his founding aspirations — Yao Zhicheng, a metallographic analysis expert at Shenyang Aircraft Corporation Co., Ltd. (hereinafter referred to as AVIC Shenyang Aircraft, or Shenyang Aircraft), aged 96. He has been rooted in the metallographic inspection post for more than 70 years and continues to serve "beyond retirement age."

Metallographic analysis is the first checkpoint for aviation metallic materials. Through the small field of a microscope, Yao Zhicheng leaves no grain pattern, hairline crack, or internal defect in metal with anywhere to hide. With rigorous judgments that tolerate not the slightest error, he has quietly stood guard over the safety of fighter jets soaring through the blue sky.

"Where there is will, there is a way (有志者事竟成)." Perhaps because the characters "Zhicheng" appear in his own name, this saying has become his personal motto. Seventy-plus years of microscopic exploration are precisely its embodiment.

Choosing this work meant being destined for obscurity

At three-thirty in the morning, the stars above Shenyang have not yet faded and the night is still deep. In an old residential building near the AVIC Shenyang Aircraft factory grounds, a light comes on. Ninety-six-year-old Yao Zhicheng carefully straightens his blue work uniform, washed to a faded white, and steps out through the door into the cool morning mist. He has walked this road connecting home and work unit for 71 springs and autumns. His steps have grown increasingly unsteady, yet the direction in his heart has never wavered.

Born in 1930 into a farming family in Shanghai, Yao Zhicheng was bright and studious as a child, but with the nation in crisis, his peaceful years of schooling were shattered by the flames of war. When the Battle of Shanghai broke out, the young boy witnessed with his own eyes Japanese warplanes bombing and strafing indiscriminately, and was filled with grief and indignation: "Our great China — why do we suffer such humiliation? Why is the sky filled with nothing but enemy aircraft?"

A burning seed was planted then and there: build China's own fighter jets, and defend the nation's boundless sky!

Because of his family's poverty, Yao Zhicheng entered a grain shop as an apprentice at an early age. The heavy labor did not extinguish his patriotic ambitions. In 1953, Yao Zhicheng fulfilled his wish and was admitted to the Department of Mechanical Engineering at Zhejiang University. Back in school, he cherished this hard-won opportunity all the more. His foundational knowledge was weak, so he compensated with diligence, rising early and staying late, immersing himself in the library and classrooms. During his two years of study, Yao Zhicheng was single-minded and did not return home even once.

In that era, How the Steel Was Tempered was widely read among young people, and the story of Pavel Korchagin devoting his life to his ideals shook him deeply. Yao Zhicheng quietly resolved that after completing his studies, he would dedicate every technique he had learned entirely to the motherland.

In 1955, heeding the nation's call, he bid farewell to his homeland in the Jiangnan region and traveled north to Shenyang, becoming a technician in the metallurgy specialty at State-Owned Factory No. 112 (the predecessor of AVIC Shenyang Aircraft), embarking from that point on a long journey of serving the nation through aviation.

When he first entered the metallographic analysis laboratory, the conditions were far more rudimentary than Yao Zhicheng had expected — a cramped office, a second-hand Soviet-supplied microscope, three or four staff members, and a scant collection of foreign-language materials: that was the sum total of their resources.

Some have called the somewhat "niche" profession of metallographic inspection engineer the "internist" of aviation metallic materials. Metal alloys of every variety were the "patients" Yao Zhicheng faced day in and day out. Whenever a "difficult case" arose, he had to act like an internist performing a diagnosis — through meticulous observation and extensive statistical analysis, he would produce a precise "diagnostic report."

Spending year after year in this small laboratory was tedious and monotonous. Choosing to do this work meant being destined for obscurity. At the time, all technical documentation for fighter aircraft was in Russian. In order to read professional literature and thoroughly master technical standards, Yao Zhicheng set out on a grueling path of self-study.

After finishing his work in the workshop and laboratory, Yao Zhicheng would often walk several kilometers to a nearby university to study Russian. His meager wages were divided into three portions: one sent to his wife in Shanghai, one for his own living expenses, and the remainder spent on books. Whenever new foreign-language books arrived at the bookstore, he would buy them at the first opportunity. According to colleagues, Yao Zhicheng would often make do with two steamed buns and a little salted vegetable for dinner, reciting vocabulary words as he walked. Through summer heat and winter cold, through wind and rain without exception, he gnawed through the "hard bone" of a foreign language.

The industry had long observed a division of labor: in the laboratory, technicians were responsible for drawing analytical conclusions while workers handled the experimental operations. Yao Zhicheng broke with this convention, insisting on personally performing operations and recording every set of data himself. He believed that only by handling materials with his own hands and observing them closely could he truly understand the "temperament" of metals. Years of bending over to observe specimens gradually curved his spine; daily handling of tweezers and grinding of test samples permanently deformed the joints of his right hand, which remains bent in the posture of work at all times.

He fought one such hard battle after another

In 1971, a major test arrived without warning — the J-6 aircraft's primary load-bearing components were using domestically produced new materials for the first time, and the factory discovered during inspection that the materials exhibited a tendency toward low-temperature cold brittleness. Some opinions held that all components of that batch of aircraft that had already been installed should be removed and replaced entirely.

But if carried out, this would not only cause enormous economic losses; the just-emerging development of domestically produced new materials would also face a halt.

At this critical juncture, Yao Zhicheng moved his bedding into the laboratory and worked day and night, repeatedly comparing metallographic charts, poring over vast quantities of literature, and conducting low-temperature simulation tests. Through meticulous analysis and judgment, he reached a conclusion: although the material had cold-brittle characteristics, it would not affect flight safety, and wholesale replacement was unnecessary. This report, which embodied painstaking effort and a sense of responsibility, was submitted up through the chain of command and ultimately received the approval of Premier Zhou Enlai. More than 90 fighter aircraft were able to enter normal service, and the path to applying and promoting domestically produced new materials was thereby opened.

He fought one such hard battle after another. On one occasion, the main beam of a fighter aircraft supplied by a steel plant in Hubei showed suspected defects — "bright spots and bright lines" — during sample testing, and hundreds of aircraft faced the risk of being grounded. Some believed this was hydrogen embrittlement caused by hydrogen in the material, endangering flight safety; Yao Zhicheng firmly held that it was a material segregation problem and that flight safety was not at issue.

Practice is the sole criterion for testing truth. Yao Zhicheng immediately led a team to Hubei, where they stayed at the plant for several months conducting in-depth investigation, confirming his judgment. The alarm was lifted, and the fighter jets that had briefly been "grounded" once again ruled the skies.

Experiments in the aviation field routinely cost millions. Over decades of work on the front lines of aviation industry production, Yao Zhicheng used his solid professional foundation and rigorous work style to overcome key technical difficulties in more than one hundred major aviation scientific research and engineering projects, saving the state enormous experimental costs. In 1992, he was awarded a State Council Special Government Allowance.

At work, Yao Zhicheng was slow to speak but quick to act. He had a habit he maintained for many years: writing notes on slips of paper. Scrap draft paper, medicine package inserts, the backs of receipts — all could serve as his "notepad," used to record operational key points, experimental techniques, or items requiring attention.

Dai Pengfei, a young technician in the metallographic laboratory, recalled the first time he independently carried out an assignment. He had been busy until noon and had just made a simple cup of instant noodles to eat when he noticed a neatly written slip of paper pressed under the bowl, annotating item by item the details of sample grinding, etching, and observation.

For a junior colleague, this brief note was the most considerate of guides. This tradition of "brief notes, deep feeling (纸短情长)" has been passed down from generation to generation in the metallographic laboratory.

In teaching and nurturing others, Yao Zhicheng was patient and thorough. When a junior colleague's thinking went astray, he never directly negated them; instead he guided them gently, inspired their thinking, and shared reference materials and classic texts. When major presentations arose, he willingly stayed in the background and gave younger colleagues the opportunity to present. Under his careful cultivation, a cohort of technical backbone personnel born in the 1980s and 1990s rapidly grew into the core of the metallographic inspection team.

Over the decades, the more than three million characters' worth of technical materials that Yao Zhicheng compiled in his spare time have become a shared asset for the industry. Colleagues say with heartfelt admiration: "Teacher Yao is a 'living textbook' of the metallographic field!"

As long as I don't collapse, I will keep coming to work

Yao Zhicheng is indifferent to fame and material gain. As various honors arrived one after another, and most colleagues who joined at the same time moved into management positions, promotion opportunities were placed before Yao Zhicheng on several occasions — each time, he declined: "Compared to an office, I cannot do without my microscope and laboratory bench."

The old man has lived a plain and low-key life throughout. To this day, he still lives with his second daughter in an old-style apartment of 80 square meters, wears the work uniform issued by his work unit year-round, and cannot bring himself to replace a pair of shoes even after they have been mended multiple times. Because he has spent years rooted in the laboratory, leaving early and returning late, neighbors who have lived in the same building for more than twenty years have mostly heard his name but rarely seen the man. "Dad has been plain and simple his whole life — stubborn as an ox. Tell him not to come to work and he won't listen. Offer him a bigger apartment and he says he doesn't pursue such things, that as long as he has a place to live it's fine," said his second daughter, Yao Jinglian. "In his whole life, Dad has only made two requests of his work unit: one, that he be allowed to continue working after retirement; and two, that he be given a set of blue work uniforms."

In 1990, Yao Zhicheng retired. When the news spread, several companies came to his door with generous salary offers — all of which he flatly refused. "Shenyang Aircraft is my home; the metallographic laboratory is my post. I still want to keep working." He voluntarily applied to return to his post without pay. Later, when the work unit grew concerned about his advanced age and declining health, the old man stubbornly wrote out a personal pledge in his own hand: he voluntarily chose to work on the job; personal safety was his own responsibility and had nothing to do with the work unit.

The sunset clouds still fill the sky (莫道桑榆晚，为霞尚满天). For more than 30 years since retirement, his daily routine has never changed: rising punctually at three-thirty every morning, eating a simple breakfast, walking two kilometers to the factory grounds, bringing his own packed lunch and dinner, and not returning home until evening — never missing a day for wind or rain. Family members have repeatedly urged him to stop; he still sets out on time. In 2013, 2019, and 2021, he suffered accidental bone fractures on three separate occasions, each time regarded by doctors as a serious case given his advanced age — yet each time, he stood back up through sheer force of will and returned to the laboratory.

Wholly absorbed in his work, he is filled with guilt toward his family. In his early years, he went north to Shenyang and was separated from his family. His young daughter traveled a thousand li to find her father, and when they met she was too shy and uncertain to recognize him. His childhood sweetheart and wife, Guo Cuigen, managed the household affairs her entire life; in her later years she fell gravely ill, and he was often unable to be by her side at all times because of work — he even missed her funeral. His eldest daughter died young from overwork; the immense grief was buried in his heart and transformed into the drive to keep going. Fortunately, his family has always understood his choices and has supported his work without wavering.

Past ninety years of age, his learning and research have not stopped. This "post-90s" individual considers himself still young. In recent years, he has undertaken the translation of the American Metals Handbook; when his eyesight fails him he moves closer to the light, and when his energy is limited he advances section by section. He says that while he can still hold a pen, leaving behind more materials means being able to help young people more. On holidays and even on New Year's Eve, the light in the metallographic laboratory is often on. While others gather with their families, he is still bent over his desk, organizing materials and analyzing test samples.

Over more than 70 years, from a spirited young man to an elder of advanced age (鲐背之年), Yao Zhicheng, like Pavel Korchagin, has given all the light and heat of his life to the magnificent aviation cause. On the writing desk in his home sits a copy of Readings in the Chinese Spirit (《中国精神读本》). He often tells his family: "We who work in aviation must have a spirit and drive (精气神). As long as I don't collapse, I will keep coming to work."

记航空工业沈飞金相分析专家姚志成—— 七十余载微观探秘：有志者事竟成 ■田 博 姚志成近影。陈 阳摄 在近期公示的全国优秀共产党员拟表彰名单中，有一位用一生践行初心的老党员——沈阳飞机工业集团有限公司（以下简称航空工业沈飞）的金相分析专家、96岁的姚志成。他扎根金相检测岗位70余载，退休后仍在“超龄服役”。 金相分析是航空金属材料的首道关口。透过方寸显微镜，姚志成让金属的纹路、细微裂纹、内在缺陷无所遁形。他用毫厘不差的严谨研判，默默守护着战鹰翱翔蓝天的安全。 “有志者事竟成。”或许他名字中有“志成”二字，这句话也成了他的座右铭。70余载微观探秘，这句话恰是写照。 从事这项工作，注定默默无闻 凌晨三点半，沈阳星斗未落，夜色正浓。航空工业沈飞厂区附近的一栋旧居民楼里，一盏灯开始亮起。96岁的姚志成仔细整理好洗得泛白的蓝色工装，推门踏入微凉晨雾。这条连接家与单位的路，他已走了71个春秋。步履日渐蹒跚，心中的方向却从未偏移。 1930年生于上海农家的姚志成，少时聪颖好学，然国难当头，安稳的读书时光终被战火打破。淞沪会战爆发时，年幼的他亲眼目睹日寇战机狂轰滥炸，悲愤不已：“泱泱中华，为何任人欺凌？为何天空尽是敌机？” 一颗滚烫的种子就此深埋：造出中国自己的战机，守护祖国的万里苍穹！ 因家境贫寒，姚志成早早进入粮店做学徒，繁重劳作并未磨灭报国之志。1953年，姚志成如愿考入浙江大学机械系。重回校园，他格外珍惜这来之不易的机会。基础薄弱，他便以勤补拙，夙兴夜寐泡在图书馆与教室。两年求学时光里，姚志成心无旁骛，没有回过一次家。 在那个年代，《钢铁是怎样炼成的》在青年中广为流传，保尔·柯察金为理想奉献一生的故事深深震撼了他。姚志成暗下决心，学成之后，定要将所学技术全部献给祖国。 1955年，他响应国家号召，辞别江南故土，北上沈阳，成为国营第112厂（航空工业沈飞前身）冶金专业的一名技术员，从此踏上航空报国的漫长征途。 初入金相分室，条件之简陋出乎姚志成的意料——一间狭小的办公室，一台苏联援助的二手显微镜，三四名工作人员，寥寥可数的外文资料，便是全部家当。 有人把金相检测工程师这个略显“冷门”的职业称作航空金属材料的“内科医生”。形色各异的金属合金，就是姚志成时时面对的“病人”。每遇“疑难杂症”，他便要像内科医生那般“望闻问切”，通过细致的观察和大量统计分析，拿出精准的“诊断报告”。 常年守在这方寸实验室里，枯燥、乏味，选择从事这项工作，注定默默无闻。当时，战机技术资料均为俄文。为读懂专业文献、吃透工艺标准，姚志成开启了苦学之路。 结束车间与实验室的工作，姚志成常徒步数公里赶往附近的大学学习俄语。微薄工资分成3份：一份寄给上海的妻子，一份用于自己的生活花销，剩下一份则用来买书。书店里每有新的外文书到货，他都会第一时间买回来。据同事讲述，姚志成常常两个窝头配一点咸菜凑合着就是晚饭，边走边背诵单词。寒来暑往，风雨无阻，他硬生生啃下了外语这块“硬骨头”。 行业里向来有分工，实验室里技术员负责研判结论，实验操作则交由工人完成，而姚志成打破了这一惯例，坚持亲手操作、记录每一组数据。他认为只有亲手触摸、细致观察，才能摸清金属的“脾气”。长年俯身观测，致其脊背渐渐弯曲；日日握持镊子、打磨试样，令其右手关节永久变形，无论何时，都是劳作时的弯曲模样。 这样的硬仗，他打了一场又一场 1971年，一场重大考验不期而至——歼-6飞机主要受力零件首次采用国产新材料，工厂在检测中发现，材料存在低温冷脆倾向。部分意见主张：将该批次战机已装机部件全部拆除更换。 可一旦执行，不仅会造成巨额经济损失，刚刚起步的国产新材料研发也面临停滞。 危急关头，姚志成把铺盖搬进实验室，夜以继日，反复比对金相图谱，翻阅海量文献，开展低温模拟测试。经缜密分析研判，他得出结论：该材料虽有冷脆特性，但不会影响飞行安全，无需整体拆换。这份凝聚心血与担当的报告层层上报，最终获得周恩来总理的认可。90余架战机得以正常服役，国产新材料应用推广之路也由此畅通。 这样的硬仗，他打了一场又一场。一次，湖北某钢厂供应的战机主梁在取样实验中，出现“亮点、亮线”的疑似缺陷，数百架战机面临停飞风险。有人认为属于氢元素引起的材料氢脆，危及安全飞行；姚志成则坚定认为是材质偏析问题，飞行安全没有问题。 实践是检验真理的唯一标准。姚志成即刻率队远赴湖北，驻厂数月深入调研，证实了自己的判断，警报解除，一度“蒙尘”的战鹰重新叱咤云海。 航空领域的实验，耗资动辄数百万。姚志成奋战在航空工业生产一线数十载，用扎实的专业功底和严谨作风，攻克上百项重大航空科研工程关键技术难题，为国家节省大量实验成本。1992年，他获评国务院政府特殊津贴。 工作中，姚志成讷于言却敏于行，他有一个坚持多年的习惯：写纸条。废旧稿纸、药品说明书、单据背面，皆可成为其“便签本”，用于记录操作要点、实验技巧或注意事项等。 金相室年轻的技术员代朋飞忆起自己首次独立开展任务，忙碌到正午，简单泡了一桶泡面正准备充饥，忽见碗下压着一张字迹工整的纸条，逐条标注着试样磨制、腐蚀、观测的细节。 对后辈，这一纸短言，便是最贴心的指引。这份“纸短情长”，在金相室代代流传。 授艺育人，姚志成耐心细致。遇后辈思路偏差，他从不直接否定，而是循循善诱，启发思考，并分享典籍资料；遇重大汇报，他甘居幕后，将展示机会让予青年。在他的悉心培养下，一批80后、90后技术骨干迅速成长为金相检测中坚。 数十年间，姚志成利用业余时间整理的300余万字的技术资料，成为行业的共享财富。同事们由衷赞叹：“姚老师就是金相领域一部‘活着的教科书’！” 只要不倒下，我就坚持上班 姚志成对名利淡泊如水。各类荣誉纷至沓来，同期入职的同事大多走上管理岗位，数次升迁机会摆在姚志成面前，他都一一婉拒：“比起办公室，我更离不开显微镜和实验台。” 老人一生朴素低调，至今，他仍与二女儿住在80平方米的老式住宅里，常年穿着单位发放的工装，一双鞋子缝补多次仍舍不得更换。因为常年扎根实验室早出晚归，同楼二十余载的老邻居，多闻其名而少见其人。“爸爸一生朴素，人倔得像头牛，不让他上班他不听；要给他换个大房子，他说不追求这些，能住就行。”二女儿姚静莲说，“爸爸这辈子只对单位提过两个要求：一是退休后让他继续上班，二是给他一套蓝色工装。” 1990年，姚志成退休。消息传开，多家企业开出优厚薪资登门邀约，都被他断然拒绝。“沈飞是我的家，金相室是我的阵地，我还想继续干活。”他主动申请无偿返岗。后来，单位忧其年高体弱，老人固执地亲笔写下承诺书：自愿在岗工作，人身安全自行承担，与单位无关。 莫道桑榆晚，为霞尚满天。退休30余年，他的作息从未改变：每天凌晨三点半准时起床，简单吃过早饭，步行两公里前往厂区，午晚都自带盒饭，直到晚上才回家，风雨不误。家人反复劝阻，他依旧按时上路。2013年、2019年、2021年，他曾3次意外骨折，次次被医生视作高龄重症，可每一次，他都凭着顽强的意志重新站起来，重返实验室。 一心扑在工作上，他对家人满是愧疚。早年北上沈阳，与家人两地分隔。年幼的女儿千里寻父，相见时怯生生不敢相认；青梅竹马的妻子郭翠根一辈子操持家事，晚年身患重病，他也常常因工作无法时刻陪伴，甚至缺席了妻子的葬礼；大女儿积劳早逝，巨大的悲痛被他藏在心底，化作继续坚守的动力。幸运的是，家人始终理解他的选择，一如既往地支持他的工作。 年逾九旬，学习与钻研仍未停下，这位“90后”认为自己还年轻。近几年，他着手翻译《美国金属材料手册》，眼神不济就凑近灯光，精力有限就分段推进。他说，趁着还能执笔，多留下一些资料，就能多帮到年轻人。节假日甚至是除夕夜，金相室的灯光常常亮起。别人阖家团圆之时，他依旧伏案整理资料、研判试样。 70余载光阴流转，从意气风发的青年，到鲐背之年的老者，姚志成像保尔·柯察金一般，把生命中所有光和热，都献给了壮丽的航空事业。在他家里的书桌上，摆放着一本《中国精神读本》，他常常告诉家人：“我们做航空事业的人，必须要有一股精气神，只要不倒下，我就坚持上班。” 制图：杨 磊
