A Regiment of the Central Theater Command Air Force Aviation Corps: Forging the "Spiritual Cutting Edge"

About this analysis
Summary
A regiment of the Central Theater Command Air Force Aviation Corps conducted a series of high-difficulty training events — including a long-range cross-regional mobility exercise flown on a previously untested route with a compressed timeline, inserted enemy intercepts, and fuel-margin strikes — and has since converted to a new aircraft type, during which it built a digitized special-situation response database that raised pilots' simulated emergency response success rate by 30 percent. The article documents the regiment's institutional answer to a specific readiness problem: how a unit flying older equipment sustains combat relevance against better-equipped sister units, with the framing centering on manual-calculation fallback skills and tactical innovation as force multipliers. The conversion narrative extends a recognizable PLA Air Force pattern of using political-work media to record how units manage the cognitive and doctrinal demands of new-aircraft transition, here adding the specific claim that a safety early-warning system with dozens of monitoring indicators was developed in-house during the conversion period — details that provide a baseline for tracking this regiment's readiness trajectory.

Forging the "Spiritual Cutting Edge"

— An Investigative Report from a Regiment of the Central Theater Command Air Force Aviation Corps

■ Ren Zhaoyi, Air Force News Reporter Fang Leilei

During high-altitude garrison training, a combat aircraft takes off beside a snow-capped mountain (archival photo). Photo by Ren Zhaoyi.

Can we really fly like this?

Night had fallen, and a thin mist hung in the air. At the airfield of a regiment of the Central Theater Command Air Force Aviation Corps, aircraft stood in formation, engines roaring and shattering the silence, as a long-range cross-regional mobility training exercise was poised to begin.

Though a routine subject, it was full of "challenges" — the mission timeline was compressed by nearly half, the route traversed a new airspace, multiple "enemy situations" were inserted en route, emergency situations were introduced on the fly under low-visibility meteorological conditions, and flight crew members did not receive their strike coordinates until moments before the canopy closed.

Difficulty surged; risk increased. Yet the flight crews' eyes were resolute: this is exactly how we must fly.

The formation lifted off in the dead of night and immediately encountered multiple interceptions. After breaking through in fierce combat, several aircraft arrived at the target airspace with fuel levels approaching their limits. With no room to retreat, the crews forced their way through on the margins of limiting conditions and precisely "destroyed" the simulated targets. After fighting through an entire day and night, they returned under the stars and moon, flight crew members soaked through their flight suits, the taut nerves of the command personnel only slightly eased.

"Organizing training this way carries high risk and enormous pressure — one slip and things go wrong. But as long as combat demands it, no matter how high the risk, we must try." Reflecting on this "extraordinary episode" from not long ago, the regiment commander stated plainly: leading troops in war requires the courage to challenge risk.

Courage is the cutting edge of a soldier's spirit. Behind the character "brave" (勇) lies the intense sense of crisis these officers and soldiers maintain — the constant feeling that their skills are not yet sufficient. Behind the character "dare" (敢) lies their mission-driven consciousness (使命自觉) to refine their martial skills and strengthen their capabilities without pause.

Tempering through storm after storm forges hearts of courage, one by one.

In aviation units, the pressure of safety is very real.

For this regiment, this long-range cross-regional mobility subject was anything but ordinary — long flight times, multiple takeoff and landing airfields, complex meteorological conditions, and along the way, a "hard fight" to be waged alongside units flying dissimilar aircraft.

At the pre-training planning meeting, the staff section reported, as was customary, a classic route that had been flown for many years. Faced with familiar airspace, the regiment Party committee leadership raised the question: could a new route be opened?

Some expressed reservations: "If we switch to a new route and additional safety risks arise, who bears responsibility?"

"Fear of bearing risk stems from the conservative mentality of 'no accidents equals achievement.'" Amid the skepticism, the regiment Party committee leadership reached consensus: "If you tense up — or even become so afraid that you want to hide — the moment you see risk, how will you ever take the battlefield? It is precisely because there is risk that responsibility is required."

They then decisively scrapped the original plan, substituted a route that had never been flown before, and raised the difficulty to the upper limit.

Embracing the entirely new challenge, morale across the regiment ran high. They studied carefully, prepared thoroughly, and ultimately completed the mission beyond expectations. Yet alongside the scorecard was a full page of problems urgently requiring resolution.

"If we hadn't tried it, how would we have known we still had these shortcomings?" said one veteran aviator with deep feeling. Problems encountered in training are not a nuisance — they are the "stepping stones" to improving capability and making progress. Having flown for a full day and night, he stepped off the aircraft and immediately began an enthusiastic after-action review.

Thereafter, the regiment Party committee and staff kept their eyes fixed on actual combat, continuously generating new and difficult problems, keeping all officers and soldiers in a state of urgency and crisis, striving to think what others had not thought of and to do what others dared not do.

After several training sessions, flight crew members broadly reported: change the direction, change the weather, introduce an unfamiliar emergency situation — some signature skills stopped working, but new killing moves also emerged under the pressure of extreme conditions.

During one night long-range strike training, the commander suddenly introduced a new emergency situation: the alternate airfield had been closed due to heavy fog. This "interlude" meant that every subsequent operation had to be executed flawlessly — otherwise, not only would the mission be impossible to complete, there would be nowhere for the aircraft to land.

In the vast darkness, the crew coordinated closely, calculated every link precisely, and controlled the aircraft steadily. They ultimately located the target in unfamiliar airspace, completed the simulated strike within the prescribed time, and returned safely — bringing back precious data and invaluable experience gathered under extreme conditions.

"Putting pressure on ourselves, setting our own difficult problems, and deliberately seeking hardship — all of it is to gain the initiative on the battlefield." A weapons control officer (武器控制师) in the regiment said: the more dangerous roads you travel, the more confidence you naturally accumulate.

During one major mission, flight crew members from the regiment flew their aircraft directly toward the target airspace. Unexpectedly, the weather changed suddenly, and the data prepared beforehand deviated from actual conditions. Facing this impromptu test, the weapons control officer remained calm and immediately recalculated. The crew then operated according to the revised data and struck the target in a single blow.

This engagement confirmed: tempering through storm after storm forges not only emergency response techniques, but hearts of courage, one by one.

Trial and error is also advancing toward the correct direction by a roundabout route.

The audacity to fight and the will to press forward are genes etched into the bones of this unit. Previously, the unit's equipment was relatively dated. Watching sister units successively re-equip with new aircraft, the regiment's officers and soldiers were consumed with anxiety.

"It wasn't that we were anxious to re-equip — it was that everyone was worried about whether we still had the fighting power to win a battle." The regiment leadership stated plainly: re-equipping happens in sequence, but there is no sequence when it comes to training hard to win. For a soldier, the true crisis is not inferior equipment but insufficient capability.

Confronting the reality of the gap, they proactively raised the slogans: "Equipment can be old, but thinking must not be outdated" and "Equipment upgrades follow orders; mental upgrades depend on self-discipline." They organized officers and soldiers to conduct in-depth study and training across five areas — theoretical innovation, target research, tactical innovation, equipment innovation, and technical support — and jointly tackled more than ten research subjects, striving to earn the "pass" for the future battlefield.

The road ahead was fraught with risk, time was extremely tight, and "defeating the strong with the weak" (以弱胜强) was anything but easy. They kept their heads down and pressed forward, their steps resolute.

During one exercise mission, the regiment boldly volunteered for the fight, competing on the same stage as units equipped with far more advanced aircraft in live-munitions strike capability. At first, few paid attention to them. After several rounds, their bombing results ranked near the top, in no way inferior to the results achieved by the advanced aircraft. Sister units could not help but praise them: "You achieved the effect of precision-guided munitions using conventional ordnance."

On another occasion, the regiment was mixed into a formation with sister units for a "combat" exercise, and many assumed they would play a supporting role. But after fierce opposition, the only aircraft to successfully complete the mission and "survive" belonged to this unit. The flight crew members of the sister units who had fought alongside them cast admiring glances: they won because they had studied deeply and their skills were strong.

This was no exaggeration. The old equipment produced outstanding results because they relied on an indomitable fighting spirit and relentless day-and-night training. They understood clearly: no matter how advanced the weapon, basic skills must be trained to a high level; no matter how equipment iterates, fallback methods must never be abandoned.

During one confrontation exercise, a crew from the regiment was ordered into action. In the heat of battle, they were suddenly subjected to intense enemy electromagnetic jamming, and the aircraft's electronic equipment nearly failed entirely. At the critical moment, following tactics they had previously studied, supported by pre-prepared data, they used manual methods to precisely calculate the route and delivered a decisive blow before the adversary could react.

In the post-battle review, the regiment's penetration and strike results were marginally better than those of the more capable aircraft. "This victory was won precisely by a skill that might seem outdated — manual calculation." One flight crew member reflected that the battlefield is full of uncertainty, and there is no "completed" state in refining tactics. When applied correctly, old methods can still find new uses and may even generate greater power.

Based on this understanding, the regiment's officers and soldiers developed new lines of thinking: How can old skills be trained into new trump cards? How can tactical research and application achieve innovation while discarding the outdated? How can penetration and strike operations exploit the adversary's weaknesses while leveraging one's own strengths?

The path of exploration was not smooth — they stumbled, experienced setbacks, produced erroneous data, and even took "detours" and "dead ends."

"Some of the tactics validated on paper proved ineffective; some of the ingenious ideas conceived through hard thinking also had little practical effect." One flight crew member said that at first, everyone was somewhat discouraged, feeling that many days of hard work had been wasted. But on reflection, such effort was equally valuable — at minimum, it proved that "this road leads nowhere." Trial and error is also advancing toward the correct direction by a roundabout route. On the road of striving forward, one must hold firmly to the objective and keep one's mind fixed on war — one cannot look ahead and behind, wavering and hesitating.

The true charge is to strive to run ahead of the times.

In earlier years, the regiment's officers and soldiers held firmly to one conviction: only by training to the limit can one reach the peak of strength. Behind nearly every record they broke in training was a story of blood running hot.

But in recent years, their understanding has quietly shifted: in an era of striving, nearly everyone is accelerating, everyone has a few tricks up their sleeve, and to win on the battlefield, fighting with all one's might is far from enough.

New equipment and new tactics struck them with particular force. On one occasion, after witnessing a new-model aircraft deliver a thunderous strike, a pilot from the regiment who had previously won the "Golden Dart" award could not help but exclaim: "If I hadn't seen it with my own eyes, it would have been hard to imagine — a battle can be fought like this!"

"We can defeat a superior adversary with inferior means, but this is absolutely not the inevitable law of winning wars." The regiment leadership said with deep meaning: the true charge is to strive to run ahead of the times.

Even while flying older aircraft types, they consciously expanded their thinking, explored new knowledge, and mastered new skills, routinely "arm-wrestling" with units equipped with new aircraft, keeping their finger on the pulse of the times and anticipating battlefield developments.

After a period of time, they reaped considerable rewards — not only had their skills advanced, but they had also arrived at new insights: in a certain sense, an upgrade in thinking can allow equipment to achieve iteration beyond its physical parameters.

After re-equipping with new aircraft, when sister units generously shared their experience, the regiment did not adopt it wholesale. Instead, they studied the techniques, updated their concepts, and upgraded their thinking with a "critical mindset." "Look — we condensed a thick stack of experience compilations into a slim conversion manual," said one young flight crew member excitedly. The process of condensing knowledge was also a process of simplifying complexity, grasping the underlying principles, and accelerating advancement.

Faced with the high degree of informatization of the new aircraft, they actively improved their training model. Establishing a "special situation response database" (特情处置数据库) is a prime example. Flight crew members digitally recorded the process of handling each special situation, and through analysis and study of the data, summarized the optimal response procedures in a format immediately comprehensible to all. Statistics show that through this method alone, pilots' simulated emergency response success rate improved by 30 percent.

Moreover, during the conversion period, the regiment's safety inspection team developed a special situation assessment safety early-warning system. They set dozens of safety monitoring indicators for various special situation assessments; once an anomaly appears, the system immediately issues a warning. In describing this innovation, a technician's eyes revealed a sense of pride.

"During the conversion process, everyone truly understood what 'knowledge overload' means, and genuinely grasped that in the information age, one must think even more." The regiment leadership explained that today, officers and soldiers actively recognize change, adapt to change, and seek change, striving to develop more real-combat "interfaces" (实战"接口") for the new equipment.

On one occasion, upon incidentally hearing that a particular tactic required rolling revision, the regiment leadership's eyes immediately lit up. In their view, was this not a precious opportunity for growth? By participating in this mission, officers and soldiers could cultivate systems-warfare thinking (体系作战思维) during the conversion phase, achieving close integration between conversion training and front-line combat readiness.

"The only fear is that the mission goes to someone else." They immediately volunteered to their superiors. Subsequently, with support at all levels, they simultaneously studied the characteristics of the new aircraft type and researched systems-warfare tactics, deepening their actual-combat awareness through mutual learning and exchange, and generating numerous new insights.

Pressure is both motivation and propulsion. In this new aircraft conversion, the regiment's performance across multiple training indicators set new records for conversion training among units of the same type. Faced with these new results, the officers and soldiers had no time to celebrate — they turned immediately to new missions and new challenges.

茫茫夜色中，机组成员密切配合，精算每个环节，稳稳操控战机，最终在陌生空域发现目标，在规定时间内完成模拟打击，随后安全返场，并带回了极限条件下的一个个珍贵数据和一条条宝贵经验。 “自我加压、自设难题、自讨苦吃，都是为了赢得战场先机。”该团一名武器控制师说，险路走多了，底气自然就足了。 敢打敢拼，是刻进这支部队骨子里的基因。此前，团队装备相对老旧。目睹兄弟单位陆续换装新机，该团官兵心急如焚。 “倒不是着急换装，而是大家担心，还有没有一战之力。”该团领导坦言，换装有先后之分，但苦练打赢没有先后之别。对于军人，真正的危机，不是装备不好，而是本领不强。 正视现实差距，他们主动叫响“装备可以老旧，理念不能陈旧”“装备更新听命令，头脑更新靠自觉”的口号，组织官兵围绕理论创新、目标研究、战法创新、装备革新、技术支援5个方面深研细训，携手攻坚10余个课题，努力考取走上未来战场的“通行证”。 前路风险重重，时间异常紧迫，“以弱胜强”谈何容易！他们埋头赶路，步履铿锵。 一次演习任务，团队果断请战，与众多装备先进战机的部队同台比拼实弹打击能力。起初，鲜有人关注他们。几轮较量后，他们的投弹成绩位居前列，与先进战机打出的成绩相比毫不逊色。一时间，兄弟单位不禁称赞：“你们用常规弹药打出了精确制导的效果。” 又一次，该团与兄弟单位混编“作战”，不少人以为他们是“配角”。谁知，一番激烈对抗过后，顺利完成任务、唯一“存活”下来的竟是这支部队的战机。对此，并肩作战的兄弟单位飞行人员投来赞许的目光：他们能赢，是因为研究深、本领强。 此言不虚。老装备打出“满堂彩”，他们凭的是不服输的血性，靠的是日夜苦练。因为他们深知，武器再先进，也得练强基本功；不管装备如何迭代，保底手段都不能丢。 一次对抗演练，该团一个机组奉命出战。战斗正酣之际，突遭“敌”强电磁干扰，机上电子设备几乎失效。关键时刻，他们按照之前研究的战术，以预先准备数据为支撑，通过人工作业方式，精准测算航线，在对方未来得及反应之时便“一剑封喉”。 战后复盘，该团突防突击成绩比性能先进的战机略胜一筹。“这次获胜，靠的正是看似过时的看家本领——人工计算。”一名飞行人员感到，战场充满不确定性，打磨战法也没有“完成时”，只要运用得当，老办法也能派上新用场，甚至焕发更大威力。 基于这种认识，该团官兵产生新的思考：如何把老手艺练成新绝招？战法研究和运用上如何做到推陈出新？突防突击中如何抓住对方短板，发挥自身优势？ 探索的路上，并非一帆风顺，他们有过磕磕绊绊，也经历过挫折，得出过错误数据，甚至走过“弯路”“回头路”。 “图上论证的战法，有一些证明无效；冥思苦想出的奇招，也有一些起不了多大作用。”一名飞行人员说，一开始，大家有些沮丧，觉得多日辛苦白费了。后来一琢磨，这样的努力同样有价值，至少证明“此路不通”。试错，也是在向着正确的方向迂回前进。奋进路上，需要目标坚定、一心向战，不能瞻前顾后、患得患失。 真正的冲锋，是努力跑在时代前边 前些年，该团官兵坚信一个理念：只有训到极限，才能强到巅峰。他们每一次刷新训练纪录的背后，几乎都有热血沸腾的故事。 可是这几年，他们的认知悄然转变：置身奋斗的时代，几乎人人都在加速跑，个个都有两把刷子，想要制胜疆场，靠拼命远远不够！ 新装备、新战法对他们的冲击尤为强烈。一次，目睹新型战机雷霆一击，该团一名曾斩获“金飞镖”的飞行员不由感叹：“若非亲眼所见，很难想象，仗还能这么打！” “我们可以以劣胜优，但这绝不是赢得战争胜利的必然规律。”该团领导意味深长地说，真正的冲锋，是努力跑在时代前边。 飞老机型时，他们就有意识地拓展新思维，涉猎新知识，掌握新技能，并常态与列装新装备的单位“掰手腕”，把握时代脉搏，前瞻战场动态。 一段时间后，他们收获颇丰，不仅本领精进了，而且有了新的感悟：某种意义上讲，思维的升级，可以让装备实现物理意义外的迭代。 换装新机后，面对兄弟单位倾囊相授的经验，他们并未全盘照搬，而是带着“批判思维”学习技术、更新观念、升级思维。“瞧，我们把厚厚一摞经验集，浓缩成薄薄一本改装手册。”一名年轻飞行人员兴奋地说，浓缩知识的过程，也是化繁为简、掌握规律、加速进阶的过程。 面对信息化程度高的新型战机，他们积极改进训练模式。建立“特情处置数据库”便是典型一例。飞行人员将每个特情处置的过程进行数字化记录，并通过分析研究数据，总结出最优处置流程，让大家一看就懂。据统计，仅通过这一方法，飞行员模拟应急处置成功率就提升了30%。 不仅如此，改装期间，该团安全监察组研究出一套特情考核安全预警系统。他们为各类特情考核设置数十项安全监控指标，一旦出现异常，系统会立马预警。说起这一创新，一名技术人员眼神中流露出自豪之情。 “改装过程中，大家才真正理解什么是‘知识过载’，也真切明白，身处信息时代更要多动脑。”该团领导介绍，如今，官兵积极识变、应变、求变，努力为新装备拓展更多实战“接口”。 一次，偶然听到一项战法需要滚动修订时，该团领导的眼睛瞬间亮了。在他们眼里，这何尝不是一次宝贵的成长机遇！通过参与此次任务，官兵在改装阶段就能培塑体系作战思维，实现改装训练与一线备战的紧密衔接。 “就怕任务分给了别人。”他们第一时间向上级请战。随后，在各级支持下，他们一边了解新机型特点，一边研究体系作战打法，在互学互鉴中深化实战意识，催生诸多新认知。 压力，是动力亦是推力。此次新机改装，该团多项训练指标，创下同类型部队改装训练新高。面对新成绩，官兵无暇庆贺，转身投入新的任务，迎接新的挑战。
