From "Barren Wasteland" to "Island Garden": They Planted a Devoted Defense of the Motherland with Their Own Hands

从"不毛之地"到"海岛花园",他们用双手种下对祖国深情的守护
PLA Daily (解放军报) 8 June 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
PLA Daily ran a three-part feature on an unnamed company of the People's Armed Police Zhoushan Detachment, Zhejiang General Corps, garrisoning a remote East China Sea island, profiling individual soldiers—Corporal Luo Wuzeren, Warrant Officer Third Class Chen Gen, Platoon Leader Peng Cheng, and others—and the unit's self-built vegetable garden, radio station, and ecological cultivation base fashioned from discarded ammunition crates and tires. The piece documents the PAP's standard political-work genre of 艰苦奋斗 (hardship-and-struggle) narrative, using first-person soldier testimony to frame retention, morale, and identity formation on isolated maritime outposts as a deliberate institutional product rather than incidental hardship. The framing—'cultivating the monotony of day after day into fertile ground for combat effectiveness (战斗力)'—points to a conscious effort to recast garrison tedium as mission-relevant conditioning, which is the specific problem this kind of content is designed to address for units where attrition and low morale on remote postings are chronic pressures.

A Vegetable Plot Is Also a Spiritual Position (精神阵地)

■ Zeng Xin, PLA Daily Special Correspondent Wang Da

The May sea breeze, laden with a salty, damp smell, beats again and again against the reef. Geologists once declared that on this small island, "growing a tree to maturity is harder than raising a child." Yet today, in a corner of the garrison of a certain company of the People's Armed Police Zhoushan Detachment, Zhejiang General Corps, cucumbers hang thick and green from their vines, and bright-red tomatoes gleam like jewels in the sunlight.

"Aside from the reef and a little salt-tolerant wild grass, there was almost no trace of green." Staff Sergeant Second Class Wang Peng still remembers what the island looked like more than ten years ago. "Fresh water came by resupply, vegetables came by transport ship, and a single strong gust could wipe out every seedling we had worked so hard to bring ashore."

"I refused to accept that." Wang Peng and his comrades began their "contest" with nature. They hauled bags of planting soil from the mainland, collected rainwater, conserved every drop of fresh water, used old planks and discarded ammunition crates to build windbreaks, and ran repeated trials to find crop varieties suited to island conditions.

"The casuarina trees beside the training ground have grown to above head height, forming a green windbreak; when the climate is favorable, vegetables and fruit are basically self-sufficient, and in peak season we can sun-dry vegetables and pickle condiments to supplement the table through winter and spring." After several years, the garrison's appearance was completely transformed. Even more remarkable, the officers and soldiers applied their creativity to build a distinctive "Island Garden" ecological cultivation base using discarded ammunition crates, old tires, and collected shells. Loofah vines climb the railings beneath the observation post; pumpkins set fruit in "flowerpots" fashioned from old tires; discarded plastic bottles have been converted into automatic drip-irrigation devices.

Staff Sergeant Second Class Pan Dong has served on the island for eight years. Last year, his wife visited the island for the first time. Seeing her husband busy at work in a garrison full of lush greenery, she said with reddened eyes: "He has made this place his home — now I can rest easy." Those words expressed the shared feeling of many island-garrison families: "That patch of green is a symbol of home, a place where longing can be set to rest."

"At first it was about improving our lives, but later I realized that growing vegetables had become a kind of ritual for putting down roots on the island," said Pan Dong. Every seedling that survived, every harvest's joy, reinforced the officers' and soldiers' sense of belonging — of "taking the island as home." Sowing, watering, fertilizing, harvesting: the process not only provided fresh vegetables but became a unique way for the troops to regulate their mental state and cultivate patience in a harsh environment.

"What we have planted in this soil is not only vegetables, but our youth and our faith. Every tree that has survived, every season's harvest of vegetables, is a witness to our steadfast fulfillment of our mission," said Political Instructor Huang.

[Caption: Party members of a certain company of the People's Armed Police Zhoushan Detachment, Zhejiang General Corps, reaffirm their oath of Party membership. Photo by Xu Shaodi.]

As the sun sets, soldiers who have finished training hum songs as they walk into the vegetable garden to water, weed, and pick ingredients for dinner.

From "barren wasteland" to "island garden," what has changed is more than the landscape. This patch of green bears witness to a unit's creativity and resilience, and tells the ordinary stories of officers and soldiers keeping watch on the island. On this small island far from the mainland, they sow vegetables with their own hands — and sow, too, a devoted defense of the Motherland.

Passionate Steadfastness at the East China Sea Outpost (东海前哨)

■ Yue Chang, Zeng Xin, PLA Daily Special Correspondent Wang Da

Scattered across the Motherland's ten-thousand-li maritime frontier lie island after island. Generation after generation of island-garrison officers and soldiers have written, in the blood of their youth, stories of steadfastness unknown to the outside world, and have completed a tempering of life (生命淬炼) in the dimensions of dream, value, and belonging.

When initial bewilderment is replaced by resolute strides; when individual worth is redefined through fighting shoulder-to-shoulder with comrades; when the feeling of being on an "isolated island" is warmed by deep bonds of comradeship — these young island sentinels complete the transformation of identity from "outsider" to "guardian." This is not only a contest between people and their environment; it is also a story of growth in which personal ideals are forged into a sense of mission and responsibility (使命担当).

[Caption: Reef stones painted with designs at the garrison gate. Photo by Xu Shaodi.]

Guarding the Sea

"Carve responsibility into your bones, and you won't be afraid"

At 4,000 meters above sea level on the snow-covered plateau, horses gallop and prayer flags flutter in the wind — that is the hometown of Corporal Luo Wuzeren, a Tibetan soldier of a certain company of the People's Armed Police Zhoushan Detachment, Zhejiang General Corps. The year he enlisted, he crossed nearly the entire map of China to arrive at the East China Sea outpost.

He had expected to find a youth more magnificent than the Tibetan saffron flower amid the great sea, but reality dealt him a heavy blow. "The sea is so vast it makes your heart race," he wrote to his father.

Not long after joining his company, on a typhoon night with wind slamming against the windows, he curled up in his bunk thinking of the blizzards on the plateau — at least there you could see the direction of the snow peaks. But here he was lost — there was no cleaving through waves as he had imagined, only day after day of duty and training.

The change came during a five-kilometer armed cross-country run. As a plateau soldier, his cardiopulmonary capacity and endurance exploded at low altitude. As he crossed the finish line, he heard his comrades cheering. For the first time, he felt that this island had a track that belonged to him.

But what truly made him understand where that track led came later.

For many new soldiers in the company, physical fitness was a weak point. After Luo Wuzeren placed first, the squad leader sought him out: "Zeren, you have a strong foundation — help bring the others along."

So more and more comrades began training with him. There were always some who fell behind; Luo Wuzeren would turn back, grab an arm, push a back, and bring the stragglers to the finish line one step at a time.

One evening, a fellow soldier from the same enlistment year, Liu Zhiping, pressed a jar of yak-meat paste sent from home into his hands: "Zeren, thank you. Now when I patrol with my rifle, my legs don't go weak — I can guard this sea alongside you."

Three months later, the company entered the detachment's mass military skills competition (群众性比武竞赛). In the collective five-kilometer armed cross-country event, they placed first.

"The plateau gave me the foundation for running; the island gave me the reason for running." Standing on the awards platform that time, Luo Wuzeren understood — he wanted to run ahead of the wind and waves together with his comrades, and guard this sea.

This awakening was not Luo Wuzeren's alone.

In the sea breeze, a landing craft rises and falls with the waves. Warrant Officer Third Class Chen Gen stands in the hold overhauling machinery; the inspection proceeds in one fluid motion, as though the wind and waves have no effect on his work at all. Yet who could have imagined that this tough man who works with steel machines had been so seasick when he first arrived on the island that he "wanted to jump into the sea"? That churning, heaving sensation had shattered every romantic image of the ocean he had ever held.

When Chen Gen arrived as a new soldier, he vomited until he was exhausted. His squad leader patted him on the shoulder and handed him a cup of warm water: "Keep vomiting, and eventually you'll vomit your way into becoming master of the sea."

That was his third month on the island. A typhoon was approaching, and heavy swells had already risen on the surface. On the detachment's most remote island, a comrade had fallen unconscious from a head injury and had to be evacuated to the mainland for treatment before the typhoon sealed off the sea lanes.

The squad leader took Chen Gen out to sea; Chen Gen was responsible for caring for the casualty. As the swells pounded the hull, Chen Gen crouched in a corner of the hold and vomited until the world went dark. He shouted with his eyes shut: "Squad leader, I can't take it anymore…"

The squad leader held the helm steady, didn't turn his head, and roared back: "If we go down, this brother is dead!"

Those words hit Chen Gen like a slap across the face. Wave after wave crashed in; salt-reeking seawater flooded into the hold. Chen Gen gripped the railing with all his strength, forced his eyes open, and lifted his head.

By the time they reached the transfer point, it was already dark. The wind was fierce and the waves high; the mooring lines were taut as bowstrings. The squad leader jumped off the craft, and together with Chen Gen carried the unconscious comrade onto a stretcher.

Later, after emergency treatment, that comrade finally regained consciousness. The military doctor told Chen Gen and the squad leader that had they been half an hour later, the patient would have been in mortal danger.

From that day on, Chen Gen never again uttered the words "I can't." Twenty-two years of guarding the island — the sea wind has weathered him dark and rough, and his large hands are covered in calluses. But twenty-two years of steadfastness have also made him an outstanding mechanical and electrical specialist (机电骨干). Now Chen Gen can calmly repair the main engine in Force 8 winds, eyes steady, hands unwavering. When comrades ask his secret, he grins: "Once you've vomited your guts out, you get used to it." Then he always adds: "Carve responsibility into your bones, and you won't be afraid."

At Peace

"Wherever the Motherland needs you to be, that is where you grow"

The sacrifices of the officers and soldiers are rarely seen by the outside world, but every island-garrison soldier is "recorded" by this island, by the comrades around them, and by the sea they guard, in a unique and profound way.

At first, Corporal Su Chenghao felt the island's reef stones were "dead" — until he used colored stones to mosaic his first "red sailboat" on a rock. A few days later, a "seagull" quietly "grew" beside the red sailboat. A few days after that, a five-starred red flag appeared in the scene as well…

A silent dialogue had begun on the reef stones.

Later, Su Chenghao used his rest time to take new soldiers around collecting rocks, carrying them back to the garrison, washing them clean, and painting them with colors. He told the new soldiers: "Come on — let me take you to bring the island's colors back with us." Gradually, the entrance to the company was filled with reef stones painted with all manner of designs — warships, lighthouses, the mountains and rivers of home, family members far away.

Someone asked Su Chenghao: "You paint these stones — does anyone look at them?"

"The squad leader has seen them. The new soldiers have seen them. Every time they pass these stones, they stop, they smile, they remember that they too are in the picture." Su Chenghao crouched down, touched the lighthouse painted on a reef stone, and said.

When a person has spent a long time on this island, they begin to notice the other things that are also trying hard to survive.

Corporal Wang Dexian's "treasure" is a thick volume he calls the "Island Book of Life" (《海岛生命册》), which records 47 species of plants he has discovered on the island.

"This is the octopus bush (抗风桐) — knocked down by a typhoon, it sprouts new shoots the following year; battered by the waves, it stands upright again a few days later…" Wang Dexian pointed to a leaf pressed in the book. "Soldiering is the same: wherever the Motherland needs you to be, that is where you grow."

Forty-seven plants, forty-seven stories. Today, the book has become an "ecological map" for new soldiers learning about the island, and a "temporal index" for veterans savoring the years gone by.

"Sound can open the warmest channel through the deepest loneliness." Early this year, new soldier Cen Ze took up the microphone of the company's "Island Voices" (海岛之声) radio station. At that point, the station had been idle for two years.

He launched the first program, called "Hello, New Soldier."

The first thing new soldiers did upon arriving on the island was to be brought to the radio station to record a brief self-introduction.

Each recording was a few dozen seconds long. Cen Ze edited them into a program and broadcast it during dinner.

Mandarin spoken with hometown accents was heard across the whole island over the airwaves. Veterans murmured at the dinner table: "That new soldier from Guangdong — his voice sounds so earnest." "The one from Nanjing, Jiangsu — what a nice name…"

"Today the whole island knows who I am, even though they haven't seen my face yet," one new soldier wrote in his diary.

"Today is my birthday, and no one knows…" Officers and soldiers would write what was on their minds on sticky notes; the radio station's "Waves of the Heart" (浪花心语) segment would broadcast them at random. One day, an anonymous note contained a few lonely words. Cen Ze spoke into the microphone: "Comrade, the whole island knows now. Happy birthday!"

"Thank you." The next day, that soldier slipped back a note. The handwriting was identical to the birthday note.

Cen Ze posted the two notes side by side on the wall of the radio station. Later, the wall was covered with notes…

Taking Root

"Waves cannot wash away the roots; wind cannot drive away the people"

"I am an isolated island — everywhere is the same; I was always alone." Private Zhang Yulong is an orphan who never had a home. On his first night on the island, he lay in his bunk and said this to himself.

"Eat up — longevity noodles." On his birthday, his squad leader, Staff Sergeant Second Class Zhang Guohou, brought out a bowl of noodles. "The noodles are made from field rations, with an egg on top."

"No one has ever celebrated my birthday, my whole life," said Zhang Yulong.

Later, Zhang Yulong fell ill and was sent to a hospital off the island for treatment. Lying in the hospital bed, he was alone again.

The following afternoon, Company Commander Zhou Wenli arrived: "Every comrade in the company asked me to tell you to take good care of yourself and get well."

From the company to the hospital — crossing the sea — the journey takes nearly three hours. Every two or three days, officers and soldiers from the company took turns coming to visit him.

"I used to think I was a rootless duckweed. Now I have grown roots. The people on this island are my roots." On the day he was discharged and returned to the island, Zhang Yulong wept.

Zhang Yulong is not the only one to have found "roots" on the island.

Years ago, new soldier Peng Cheng arrived at the company. Two years later, he was admitted to the People's Armed Police Officer Academy. When assignments were handed out after graduation, classmates urged him to choose a major city. He shook his head and resolutely returned to the small island.

"When I stood where you are standing now, I vomited until the world went dark and cried in secret at night. It was this island that taught me to be a soldier; it was the wind and waves here that turned me from a coward into a platoon leader." At a new-soldier training session, Peng Cheng — now a platoon leader — stood before the new soldiers and said.

Outside the garrison wall of the company, there is a ring of camphor trees. Each tree has words carved into it: the year of enlistment, training reflections, messages left by veterans upon departing the island.

"This was planted by my squad leader — he guarded this island for twelve years," said Peng Cheng, pointing to one tree. Carved into it were the words: "Waves cannot wash away the roots; wind cannot drive away the people."

That was Peng Cheng's second year on the island, when a typhoon struck. His squad leader took him up onto the barracks roof to reinforce the antenna. The wind was so strong a person could barely stand; Peng Cheng's foot slipped and his body tilted toward the edge of the building. The squad leader instantly drew out a safety rope, looped one end around him and tied the other end to his own waist.

"Don't let go!" the squad leader roared. The knot of the safety rope dug into his waist, and he cried out in pain. Two more veterans rushed over; the three of them together finally pulled Peng Cheng back.

On the eve of his graduation, Peng Cheng sat alone for a long time, thinking back on the rope marks left on his squad leader's waist after the typhoon…

Now Peng Cheng has become one of the people on the island least afraid of wind and waves. On typhoon days, he is the first to rush out and secure the mooring lines.

In four years on the island, he has come to know every inch of its terrain and every shift of its winds. He has given a name to every reef on the island, and takes soldiers out to learn directions and memorize the tides.

"Knowing them is knowing our position (阵地), knowing the place we are defending," Peng Cheng tells his soldiers.

An Eyewitness Account

The Sound of the Waves Remains; the Years Are Like a Song

■ Gao Weigang

[Caption: At sunset, officers and soldiers of a certain company of the People's Armed Police Zhoushan Detachment, Zhejiang General Corps, stand guard and conduct patrols. Photo by Xu Shaodi.]

The waves beat against the shore like the island's eternal heartbeat. The experience of first landing on a certain island for research is branded in my memory: transferring to a landing craft, then to a ferry vehicle, nearly three hours of jolting travel.

The moment I stepped off, sea wind carrying salt and brine hit me full in the face. Looking around, I saw young faces on which the sea wind had already carved its marks.

What moved me most during the research was the resilience of officers and soldiers who "endure hardship without speaking of hardship" (吃苦不言苦). In the past, the island's supply ships were often delayed by wind and waves, and fresh vegetables became a "luxury." The officers and soldiers had not a word of complaint. Cook squad leader, Staff Sergeant Second Class Pan Dong, worked out 13 methods for preparing canned food dishes and compiled them into a "Island Cooking Manual" (《海岛烹饪手册》) for distribution across the island. Mechanical and electrical squad leader, Warrant Officer Third Class Chen Gen, drew by hand a schematic chart of all navigational markers in the island's waters, wrote detailed maintenance manuals, and marked key points in different colors with meticulous clarity…

Most unforgettable was a late-night round of sentry checks. "The Big Dipper has turned back to that position again," a sentry said, looking up at the stars and telling me their stories. At the Spring Festival gala, officers and soldiers sang together "The Soldier's Second Hometown" (《战士的第二故乡》); as veterans departed the island upon discharge, they solemnly pressed their dog-eared, annotation-filled notebooks into the hands of new comrades…

During the research period, it happened that family members were visiting the unit. The journey for families to reach the island was equally arduous: high-speed rail to bus, then ferry; if a typhoon struck, they could only wait on shore. But once on the island, the military wives rolled up their sleeves and watered and fertilized alongside the soldiers. In the vegetable garden, a soldier from Sichuan grew chili peppers; a soldier from Guangdong cultivated bok choy; a soldier from Gansu planted salt-tolerant potatoes in the cracks between reef stones. I remember one military wife who, just before leaving, scattered a handful of cilantro seeds from her hometown and said softly: "Let them keep him company in my place." That small patch of green had become a tender footnote to an iron fortress.

On the day of departure, the first light of dawn was just touching the surface of the sea. The formation of officers and soldiers doing morning exercises ran along the reef path; the resounding rhythm of their footsteps joined in chorus with the surging sound of the waves. Looking back at this island where once "growing a tree to maturity was harder than raising a child," I saw that casuarina trees now form a forest, and the vegetable garden stays green year-round.

The sound of the waves remains; the years are like a song. Generation after generation of island-garrison officers and soldiers have tempered what others see as loneliness into wordless steadfastness, and have cultivated the monotony of day after day into fertile ground for combat effectiveness (战斗力). Ground down by raging seas and towering waves, watching over the island through the rising of the sun and the setting of the moon, they have forged themselves into an unshakable bedrock (磐石) on the Motherland's maritime frontier.

对中队不少新兵而言，体能是短板。罗伍泽仁拿了第一之后，班长找到他：“泽仁，你底子好，带带大家。” 于是，跟着他练习的战友越来越多，总有人掉队，罗伍泽仁便折返回去，拽胳膊，推后背，把掉队的战友一步一步带到终点。 一天晚上，同年兵刘志平塞给他一罐从老家寄来的牦牛肉酱：“泽仁，谢谢你。我现在巡逻背枪腿不软了，能跟你一起守好这片海了。” 3个月后，中队参加支队群众性比武竞赛，集体五公里武装越野课目，得了第一。 “高原给了我奔跑的底子，海岛给了我奔跑的理由。”这一次，罗伍泽仁站在领奖台上想明白了——他要和战友一起跑赢风浪，守好这片海。 这种觉醒，并非罗伍泽仁独有。 海风中，登陆艇随着海浪上下起伏，三级警士长陈根站在舱底检修机械，检修过程一气呵成，风浪似乎没有对他的操作带来任何影响。可谁又能想到，这个与钢铁机器打交道的硬汉，初上岛时竟晕船晕到“想跳海”？那翻江倒海的滋味，曾将他所有关于大海的浪漫想象击得粉碎。 新兵登岛时，陈根吐到虚脱，班长拍着他的肩膀，递上一杯温水：“吐着吐着，就吐成大海的主人了。” 那是他上岛的第3个月，台风临近，海面已经涌起大浪。支队最偏远的海岛上，一名战友因头部受创昏迷不醒，必须赶在台风封锁航道前，转移到陆地接受治疗。 班长带着陈根出海，陈根负责照料伤员。浪涌拍打着艇身，陈根蹲在船舱一角吐得昏天黑地。他闭着眼喊：“班长，我不行了……” 班长稳稳把着舵，头也没回，吼了一句：“我们要是趴下了，这兄弟就没命了！” 那句话像一巴掌扇在陈根脸上。巨浪一波接着一波，咸腥的海水灌进船舱。陈根死死攥住栏杆，强迫自己睁开眼、抬起头。 抵达转送点时，天已经黑了。风大浪急，缆绳绷得像琴弦。班长跳下艇，和陈根一起把昏迷的战友抬上担架。 后来，经过抢救，那名战友终于醒了过来。军医告诉陈根和班长，再晚半小时，病人就危险了。 从那以后，陈根再没喊过一句“不行”。守岛22年，海风把他吹得黝黑粗糙，一双大手满是老茧。但22年的坚守，也让他成长为一名优秀的机电骨干。如今，陈根能在八级风浪里稳稳当当修主机，眼不花、手不抖。有战友问他秘诀，他咧嘴一笑：“把胆吐没了，就习惯了。”说完，他还会补充一句：“把责任刻进骨头里，就不怕了。” 官兵组织战术训练。万林广摄 安 心 “祖国需要你在哪，你就在哪生长” 官兵们的付出，很少被外界看见，但每一个守岛官兵，都被这座岛、被身边的战友、被他们守护的这片海，以独特而深刻的方式“记录”着。 起初，下士苏程浩觉得岛上的礁石是“死”的，直到他用彩石在石头上拼贴出第一艘“红帆船”。几天后，“红帆船”旁边竟悄然“长”出了一只“海鸥”。又过几天，画面上又多了一面五星红旗…… 一场无声的对话，在礁石上展开。 后来，苏程浩利用休息时间带着新兵们到处捡石头，搬回营区，清洗干净，涂上颜料。他对新兵说：“走，带你们去把岛上的颜色搬回来。”渐渐地，中队门口摆满了画着各种图案的礁石——有军舰、有灯塔、有家乡的山水、有远方的家人。 有人问苏程浩：“你画这些石头，有人看吗？” “班长看见了，新兵看见了。他们每次路过这些石头，会停下来，会笑，会想起自己也在画里。”苏程浩蹲下身，摸了摸礁石上画的灯塔说道。 一个人在这个岛上待久了，就会开始注意那些同样也在努力活下去的东西。 中士王德贤的“珍宝”，是一本厚重的《海岛生命册》，里面记录着他在岛上发现的47种植物。 “这是抗风桐，被台风吹倒，来年又冒出新芽，被浪打过，过几天又挺立起来……”王德贤指着册子里的一片叶子说，“当兵也是一样，祖国需要你在哪，你就在哪生长。” 47种植物，47个故事。如今，这本册子成了新兵了解海岛的“生态地图”，也成了老兵回味岁月的“时光索引”。 “声音，能在最深的孤独中开辟出最温暖的航线。”今年初，新兵岑泽接过中队“海岛之声”广播站的话筒。彼时，广播站已经闲置了两年。 他办了第一个栏目，叫“新兵你好”。 新兵上岛第一件事，就是被拉到广播站录一段自我介绍。 每段录音几十秒，岑泽把它们编成节目，在晚饭时间播出。 带着乡音的普通话，通过电波被全岛听见了。老兵们在餐桌上小声议论：“那个广东的新兵，声音挺憨的”“江苏南京那个，名字好听”…… “今天全岛都认识我了，虽然他们还没见过我的脸。”一个新兵在日记里这样写道。 “今天是我生日，没人知道……”官兵们会把自己的心里话写在便利贴上，广播站的“浪花心语”栏目会随机播报，有一天，一张匿名纸条上写着一段孤独的话语。岑泽对着话筒说：“这位战友，全岛现在都知道了。生日快乐！” “谢谢。”第二天，那名战友塞回一张纸条。笔迹和那张生日纸条，一模一样。 岑泽把两张纸条并排贴在广播站的墙上。后来，墙上贴满了纸条…… 扎 根 “浪打不掉根，风刮不走人” “我是一座孤岛，哪儿都一样，我本来就是一个人。”上等兵张雨龙是一名孤儿，从小没有家，上岛第一晚，他躺在铺上跟自己说道。 “吃吧，长寿面。”生日那天，班长、二级上士张国后端出一碗面，“面条是用野战食品煮的，上面加了个鸡蛋。” “我从小到大，没人给我过过生日。”张雨龙说。 后来张雨龙不幸患病，被送到岛外医院治疗。躺在病床上，他又变成了一个人。 第二天下午，中队长周文立来了：“全中队战友让我叮嘱你好好接受治疗。” 从中队到医院，漂洋过海，路程近3小时。每隔两三天，中队官兵就会轮流来看望他。 “我以前觉得我是个没根的浮萍。现在我长出了根。这岛上的人，就是我的根。”出院回岛那天，张雨龙泪如雨下。 像这样在岛上找到“根”的，不止张雨龙一个。 当年，新兵彭程万里下连，来到这里。2年后，他考上武警警官学院。毕业分配时，同学劝他选择大城市，他摇摇头，坚定地返回小岛。 “当年，我站在你们这个位置，吐得昏天黑地，夜里偷偷哭。是这座岛教会我当兵，是这里的风浪把我从一个怂包练成了排长。”一次新兵集训，已是排长的彭程万里站在新兵面前说。 该中队营区围墙外，有一圈樟树。每棵树上都刻着字，有入伍年份、有训练心得、有老兵退伍时的留言。 “这是我的班长种的，他在这守了12年。”彭程万里指着一棵树说，树上刻着：“浪打不掉根，风刮不走人。” 那是彭程万里上岛第2年，台风来袭。班长带着他上营房顶加固天线，风吹得人站不稳，彭程万里脚下一滑，身体往楼边倒。班长瞬间抽出保险绳，一头套住他，一头拴在自己腰上。 “别松！”班长吼道。保险绳的绳结勒进腰，疼得他大叫。又有两名老兵扑过来，3人合力，终于将彭程万里拉了回来。 毕业前一夜，彭程万里独自坐了很久，回想起台风后班长腰上的勒痕…… 现在，彭程万里成了岛上最不怕风浪的人之一，台风天，他第一个冲出去固定缆绳。 在岛上4年，他熟悉这里的每一寸土地、了解每一阵风的变化。他把岛上每一块礁石都起了名字，带着战士们认方向、记潮汐。 “记住它们，就是记住我们的阵地，记住我们守护的地方。”彭程万里对战士们说。 亲历者说 涛声依旧 岁月如歌 ■高炜罡 夕阳下，武警浙江总队舟山支队某中队官兵正在站岗巡逻。许少迪摄 浪涛拍岸，犹如海岛恒久的心跳。初登某岛调研的经历，烙印于心：换乘登陆艇、转乘摆渡车，颠簸了近3小时。 刚下车，海风裹着咸腥扑面而来。环顾四周，一张张青涩的脸上，镌刻着海风雕琢的痕迹。 调研中，最触动我的是官兵们“吃苦不言苦”的韧劲。在以前，岛上给养船常因风浪延误，新鲜蔬菜成了“奢侈品”，官兵们毫无怨言。炊事班长、二级上士潘东琢磨出13种罐头菜的烹饪方法，并将其整理成《海岛烹饪手册》在全岛推广；机电班长、三级警士长陈根一笔一画地记录下全岛海域航标示意图，详细撰写维修手册，各方面要点用不同颜色标注得清清楚楚…… 最难忘的是一次深夜随哨兵查哨。“北斗七星又转到那个位置了。”哨兵望向星空向我讲述他们的故事。春节晚会上，官兵们齐唱《战士的第二故乡》；老兵退伍离岛之际，郑重地将卷着边、写满心得的笔记塞到新战友手中…… 调研期间，恰逢家属来队。家属上岛之路同样充满艰辛：高铁转汽车，再换乘轮渡，遇上台风天，只能在岸上苦等。可一旦上岛，军嫂们就撸起袖子跟战士一起浇菜施肥。菜园里，四川籍战士种辣椒，广东籍战士育小白菜，甘肃籍战士在礁石缝里种耐盐土豆。记得一位军嫂临走前撒下一把家乡的香菜种子，轻声说：“让它们替我陪着他。”这方小小的绿色天地，成为钢铁堡垒温情的注脚。 返程日，曙光初染海面，出操的官兵队伍跑过礁石路，铿锵的脚步声与澎湃的涛声合奏。回望这座曾经“种活一棵树比养大一个娃还难”的海岛，如今木麻黄成林，菜园常绿。 涛声依旧，岁月如歌。一代代守岛官兵，将常人眼中的孤独，淬炼成无言的坚守；将日复一日的单调，深耕为战斗力的沃土。他们在惊涛骇浪的磨砺中，在日升月落的守望里，把自己锻造成祖国海疆坚固的磐石。
