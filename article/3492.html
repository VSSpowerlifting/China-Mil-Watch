About this analysis
Summary
A unit under the Xinjiang Military District uses its military history museum display of 2,930 silver dollars — preserved intact since a 1932 Yanchuan guerrilla action through more than ten garrison relocations — as the centerpiece of a political education campaign on frugality and combat-readiness resource discipline. The article pairs the artifact's history with two contemporary anecdotes: a logistics cadre resisting pressure to divert plateau field-repair construction materials to living quarters, and a company that kept new-model optical sights locked away unused until a nighttime shooting assessment exposed the resulting proficiency gap. This is standard political work content (以史育人, education through history); its value is as a record of how a Xinjiang Military District unit frames resource discipline and combat-readiness stewardship for junior officers and NCOs, not as evidence of a new policy line or changed posture.
Translation

2,930 Silver Dollars: "Red Family Fortune" Reveals Original Aspiration

■ Zeng Xianglong

Red artifacts on display at the military history museum of a unit under the Xinjiang Military District. Photo by Liu Shuai

In the military history museum of a unit under the Xinjiang Military District, 2,930 silver dollars are on display — a "red family fortune" (红色家底) that every member of the unit holds in memory. Weathered by decades of time, the silver dollars have lost their original luster, yet they carry within them the original aspiration (初心) of generation after generation of officers and soldiers to prepare for and fight in war.

This weighty family fortune traces back to Yanchuan, northern Shaanxi, in 1932. The unit's predecessor was a guerrilla force active in the Yanchuan area. After a victorious battle, they seized a quantity of materiel, including silver dollars. Considering that liaison missions, procurement, and similar tasks required occasional small expenditures, the guerrillas distributed one silver dollar to each cadre to be kept for emergencies.

Before long, the guerrilla force was caught in a fierce battle, suffered heavy casualties, and urgently needed to purchase large quantities of medicine to treat the wounded. Upon learning of the situation, the cadres each produced their silver dollar and turned them all in. It turned out that no one had been willing to spend their coin — each had been saving it for the moment it was truly needed. The silver dollars remaining after the medicine was purchased were kept in reserve.

The Battle of Shenjialing in Lanzhou was the hardest-fought and most glorious battle in the unit's history. At the time, the 4th Company of a certain regiment had fought down to only four survivors. After the battle, the regimental Party committee allocated 100 silver dollars for the company's reconstitution. The then-company commander Wang Ligong and his soldiers revisited the story of the Yanchuan guerrillas donating their silver dollars to save the wounded, and the entire company resolved: guard the "red family fortune" well, and save it for when it is needed most! The officers and soldiers maintained a style of plain living and frugality, unwilling to waste a single fen, and kept a considerable number of silver dollars in reserve.

For a period, the 4th Company was assigned as the vanguard, suffering enormous combat attrition. Sister companies voluntarily contributed their saved silver dollars to the 4th Company. Beyond covering basic expenditures, Wang Ligong solemnly handed the remaining silver dollars over to the higher-level organization. The finance department took this especially seriously, storing these silver dollars separately and keeping them in careful custody.

Later, the unit received orders to carry out a mission in a harsh and remote area. The environment was brutal; hunger, wounds, and illness were constant companions, and the unit fell into serious difficulty for a time. To ensure the force maintained sufficient combat effectiveness, the finance department drew on a portion of the reserve silver dollars to purchase supplies. Once again the unit relied on this "red family fortune" to pull through the crisis, and as conditions improved slightly, the soldiers voluntarily turned over the remaining silver dollars to the finance department.

As the years passed, the unit underwent more than ten garrison rotations. Successive Party committee leaderships strictly enforced management regulations, carefully preserving all 2,930 silver dollars in total, with not a single person taking any for personal use.

As the economy developed, silver dollars passed out of circulation. With approval from higher authorities, this collection of silver dollars was retained by the unit as red artifacts. In 1979, during the unit's final long-distance garrison relocation, logistics cadre Guo Shengliang and his comrades escorted the coins throughout the entire journey, standing watch in shifts, and safely delivered all 2,930 silver dollars to the new station after a lengthy transfer.

Thereafter, every successive Party committee handover has been accompanied by a solemn ceremony for the transfer of red artifacts. What has been passed down alongside them is a plain and pure set of values — the silver dollars have a monetary worth, but the officers' and soldiers' fine conduct of hard struggle and selfless dedication to duty (克己奉公) is a priceless treasure that no sum of money can measure.

"Save It for When It Is Needed Most"

■ Mu Yulong, Cadre of a Unit under the Xinjiang Military District

A few days ago, I walked into the military history museum and stood for a long time before the display case holding the silver dollars. These coins, which circulated during the Land Revolution period and the War of Resistance Against Japan, would have represented no small sum of wealth at any time. Looking at them, I could picture in my mind the scene of revolutionary forebears carefully managing their way through hard times, and I was also reminded of an experience during a plateau field training exercise.

On that occasion, the unit was conducting a medium repair (中修) task on a certain type of equipment in a plateau environment. Because a considerable amount of equipment was required, the higher command decided to procure a batch of construction materials to build a field repair workshop.

When the materials arrived, someone suggested: since the construction materials were fairly plentiful, could a portion be set aside to expand the living quarters, which would make for more comfortable accommodations?

Honestly, in that moment my mind wavered. I thought: plateau field training means heavy wind and sand, and living conditions are simple — diverting a little material for the sake of the officers and soldiers wouldn't really count as misappropriating public resources for private use. But then I turned my thoughts around, and those 2,930 silver dollars appeared in my mind. In those days, the forebears guarded their life-saving savings, willing to go hungry and endure the cold rather than casually divert them. Now, here were dedicated materials right in front of me, and I was already thinking about improving living conditions. Comparing the two, I felt my face flush with heat.

In the end, the cadres and backbone personnel unified their thinking: all construction materials must go into combat readiness facility construction, in service of combat effectiveness. Before long, a fully functional field repair workshop rose from the plateau.

This incident sounded a warning bell for me. Compared with the revolutionary war era, material support today is abundant — but the conduct of frugality and plain living must not be abandoned. If combat readiness materiel is diverted for living support, what is being undermined is the bottom line of maintaining discipline and fighting to win.

Of course, not abandoning the character of hard struggle does not mean pressing materiel and equipment to the bottom of a storage chest. At times, hoarding for its own sake also betrays the original intent and creates another form of "waste."

A few years ago, a certain company was issued a batch of new-model optical sights. Everyone in the company treated them as treasures, afraid that bumps and knocks during training would be hard to account for, so they kept them locked in a cabinet and rarely used them, only occasionally taking them out to "see the light." During one higher-level nighttime shooting assessment, participants were required on short notice to use the new-model sights. The company hurriedly organized study sessions, but because everyone had used them so rarely, they were still unfamiliar with them on the range, and the final shooting results were unsatisfactory.

From this, I truly came to understand what the forebears meant by "save it for when it is needed most." It does not mean disposing of things arbitrarily, nor does it mean locking them away on a shelf. It means adhering to the principle of "everything for the sake of guaranteeing victory in war, everything for the sake of improving combat effectiveness" — not spending a single fen that should not be spent, and not sparing a single fen that must be spent, putting every accumulated resource to use where it counts most, helping the force overcome one difficulty after another and achieve one victory after another.

In recent years, the unit has significantly reduced administrative expenditures and concentrated financial resources to build new training facilities including a comprehensive training ground for reconnaissance soldiers and a light weapons shooting range. Before one long-distance field training deployment, the officers and soldiers of the maintenance company of the service support battalion volunteered to weld and repair hundreds of vehicle-mounted equipment items themselves, saving a considerable sum of funds. I believe the unit's officers and soldiers will not forget where those 2,930 silver dollars came from. Our "red family fortune" will remain substantial forever.

(Compiled by PLA Daily special correspondent Cui Yaqing)

Original Chinese
2930枚银元：“红色家底”见初心 ■曾祥龙 新疆军区某部军史馆收藏的红色文物。刘帅 摄 在新疆军区某部军史馆，陈列着2930枚银元，这是该部人人铭记的“红色家底”。历经几十载岁月洗礼，银元褪去了当年的光亮，却藏着一代代官兵向战为战的初心。 这笔沉甸甸的家底，最早可追溯到1932年的陕北延川。该部前身是活跃在延川地区的一支游击部队。一次战斗胜利后，他们缴获了包括银元在内的一些物资。考虑到执行联络、采买等任务有零星开支需求，游击队便给每名干部分发一枚银元，以备不时之需。 不久，游击队遭遇一场恶战，伤亡惨重，急需购置大量药品救治伤员。得知情况，干部们纷纷掏出银元悉数上交。原来，这些银元大家都没有舍得花，就是为了在关键时刻派上用场。购买药品后剩下的银元，被留存下来。 兰州沈家岭战斗，是该部历史上最过硬、最光辉的一战。当时，某团四连浴血拼杀至仅剩4人，战后，团党委划拨100枚银元用于连队重建。时任连长王立功和官兵们重温延川游击队捐银元救伤员的往事，全连官兵决定：守护好“红色家底”，留到最需要的时候用！官兵们保持艰苦朴素、勤俭节约的作风，不肯浪费一分一厘，将不少银元留了下来。 有段时间，四连担负先锋任务，战损消耗巨大，兄弟连队主动将节省下来的银元送给四连。除了保证基本开支之外，王立功郑重地将剩余的银元交给上级组织。对此，财务部门格外重视，将这笔银元单独存放、妥善保管。 后来，部队奉命到某艰苦地区执行任务。环境恶劣，饥饿、伤病如影随形，部队一度陷入困境。为了保证队伍战斗力充足，财务部门拿出一部分储备银元购买物资，该部再次靠着这笔“红色家底”渡过了难关，情况稍有好转后又自发把留存的银元上交财务部门。 岁月流转，该部先后历经10余次换防，历届党委班子严格落实管理规定，将总共2930枚银元悉心保存，没有一人撷取私用。 随着经济发展，银元退出流通领域。经上级批准，这批银元作为红色文物被该部妥善留存。1979年，该部最后一次千里移防，后勤干部郭生亮和战友全程押运、分段值守，历经长途转运，将2930枚银元安全送抵新驻地。 此后每一届党委班子换届，都会举行庄重的红色文物交接仪式。同样传承下来的，还有朴素纯粹的价值观——银元有价，而官兵艰苦奋斗、克己奉公的优良作风，是任何金钱都无法衡量的无价之宝。 “留到最需要的时候用” ■新疆军区某部干部 母玉龙 前几天，我走进军史馆，在存放银元的展柜前驻足良久。这批土地革命时期和抗日战争时期流通的货币，无论何时都算一笔不小的财富。看着它们，我眼前浮现出革命前辈精打细算过“苦日子”的场景，也想起了一次高原驻训的经历。 那次，部队在高原环境下开展某型装备中修任务。由于要动用的设备较多，上级决定采购一批建材，搭建野战修理工间。 当物资到货时，有人提议：既然建材比较充裕，能不能分出一部分来扩建生活区，这样住着也舒服些。 老实说，那一刻我心里动了念头，想着高原驻训风沙大、住宿条件简陋，匀一点材料也是为了官兵，不算公器私用。可一转头，脑海里浮现出那2930枚银元。当年先辈守着救命积蓄，宁肯自己挨饿受冻，也不轻易挪用。如今专项物资摆在眼前，可我竟惦记起改善生活条件。两相一对比，我脸颊不禁发烫。 后来，干部骨干统一思想，所有建材必须投入战备设施建设，服务于战斗力。没过多久，一座功能完备的野战修理工间在高原拔地而起。 这件事给我敲响了一记警钟。相比于革命战争年代，今天物质保障充足，但勤俭节约的作风本色不能丢。倘若挪用战备物资用于生活保障，动摇的就是守纪律、为打赢的底线。 当然，不丢掉艰苦奋斗的本色，也不意味着把物资、装备压在箱底。有时，一味珍藏也会背离初衷，造成另一种形式的“浪费”。 几年前，某连配发了一批新式瞄准镜，连队上下都当宝贝，怕训练中磕了碰了不好交代，平时锁在柜子里不舍得用，偶尔才拿出来“见见光”。一次上级组织夜间射击考核，临时要求全员使用新式瞄准镜参训。连队赶紧组织学习，可大家平时用得少，到了靶场上依然不熟悉，最终射击成绩不尽如人意。 由此，我真正读懂了先辈们口中“留到最需要的时候用”，那不是随意处置，也不是束之高阁，而是秉持着“一切为了保障打赢，一切为了提升战斗力”的原则，不该花的一分不乱花，该花的一分不能省，将积攒的每一份资源都用在刀刃上，助力队伍克服一道道难关、取得一次次胜利。 近几年，单位大幅缩减行政开支，集中财力建成侦察兵综合训练场、轻武器射击场等新型训练场地。一次远程驻训前，勤务保障营修理连官兵主动请缨，自己动手焊接维修数百件随车器材，节省了一笔可观的经费。我相信，部队官兵不会忘记那2930枚银元的来处，我们的“红色家底”会永远厚实。 （解放军报特约记者崔雅庆整理）
