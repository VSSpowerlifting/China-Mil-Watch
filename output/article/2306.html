About this analysis
Summary
PLA Daily's 'Strong-Army Style' mass cultural activities program published four first-person narrative scripts from the 'Strivers Speak' story-session series, featuring a PAP Hunan Corps training instructor, a Joint Logistic Support Force 960th Hospital head nurse with three UN peacekeeping tours, a Navy submarine helmsman-signalman, and an Army target-drone operator holding six national patents. This is standard political-work content; its value is as a record of how the institution frames individual sacrifice, technical mastery, and mission identity for junior and mid-grade enlisted personnel — not as evidence of new policy or operational posture. The framing across all four scripts consistently ties personal physical hardship to unit effectiveness, a pattern that documents the current campaign's messaging priorities rather than any change in training standards or force structure.

Listen — the Sound of Struggle from the Front Lines

— Selected Scripts from the "Strivers Speak" (奋斗者说) Strong-Army Story Sessions, Part of the "Strong-Army Style" (强军风采) Mass Cultural Activities

Stories are the vessel of the spirit. The march toward a strong army is inscribed with the footprints of struggle and filled with vivid stories that strike straight to the heart and spur people forward. Since the beginning of this year, units across the entire military have carefully organized "Strivers Speak" strong-army story sessions as part of the "Strong-Army Style" mass cultural activities, using the inspiring stories of exemplary figures close at hand to rally hearts and minds. The blazing practice of building a strong army has provided rich material for these story sessions. We will select and publish the best scripts to lead our readers deep into the front-line posts and feel the new spirit and new bearing of officers and soldiers striving to build a strong army. Units are warmly invited to submit contributions.

— The Editors

Always Be a "Steel Nail"

■ Sun Jiangtao

Profile: Sun Jiangtao, instructor at the Teaching and Research Section of the Training Base of the People's Armed Police (PAP) Hunan Corps, First-Class Staff Sergeant; named one of the 23rd "Top Ten Loyal Guards of the Chinese People's Armed Police"; awarded first-class merit citation once, second-class merit citation once, and third-class merit citation twice; recipient of the China Youth May Fourth Medal in 2026.

Sun Jiangtao

In the dead of night, I sometimes run my fingers over the scar on my wrist. It lies against my skin like a centipede — hard to the touch, with a steel nail beneath it.

During the corps special operations intensive training in 2014, I fell from a four-meter-high rope ladder and fractured my left hand when it hit the ground. The doctor said the bone had been set five times without success, and in the end the only option was to put in a steel nail. From that point on, my wrist joint had an uninvited guest.

Late at night, unable to sleep in the darkened ward, I suddenly recalled my years of martial arts training at the Shaolin Martial Arts Research Institute as a boy. Back then, we would hold a stance for half a shi (半个时辰) at a stretch. The master held a bamboo switch in his hand — if anyone's knees bent, it came down with a crack. Beads of sweat dripped from my chin to the floor, pooling into a small puddle.

"Only those who can stand firm can develop real skill!" Those words from my master entered my bones even earlier than the steel nail in my wrist.

Not long after I was discharged from the hospital, I heard that the corps special operations cadre and backbone intensive training was about to begin, and I insisted on taking part. When shooting, the rifle stock pressed against my shoulder socket and my left hand supported the handguard; every shot felt like someone striking my wrist with a hammer, the steel nail vibrating inside the bone. On the obstacle course, scaling the high wall required pushing off with both hands. I didn't dare use my left hand, and forced myself over using only my right hand and core strength.

First in tactics, first in combat, first in individual all-around… The day the results were posted, I stood before the notice board and smiled with satisfaction. My comrades called me the "Iron Man." I waved it off — I thought the assessment was too high. I am not made of iron; the pain in my wrist was real. I simply did not want a single injury to put me out of the ranks.

Standing firm takes strength, and it takes willpower.

When I first enlisted in 2011, my martial arts background let me stand out from the very start of basic training — I ran 3,000 meters in under eleven minutes on my first attempt, and picked up equipment training immediately. But afterward I was assigned to a garrison company with heavy sentry duties, and I let my training standards slip. In one assessment my results were unsatisfactory; I couldn't even keep up with the veterans on the run, and my fighting spirit collapsed. The squad leader pulled me aside and said: "You're already deflated after just a few days? Maybe you should just serve your two years and get out."

The squad leader had read the stubborn, competitive streak in my bones and deliberately threw cold water on me — a few words were enough to bring me back to my senses. I had practiced martial arts for so many years without giving up, and I had enlisted determined to be a good soldier. How could I just walk away?

In winter, after collective training ended, I would still hang from the pull-up bar, puffs of white breath rising from my mouth. I practiced low crawling in the snow until my hands swelled from the cold, but I didn't feel it as hardship.

In the next assessment, I again ranked near the top. The squad leader slapped my shoulder and called out: "Well done!" I have remembered that praise to this day. It was also from that time that I began to slowly understand — a good soldier has two sides: the sharp edge that charges forward to win, and the resilience that does not break under setbacks; even when knocked down, one always grits one's teeth and stands back up.

The New Year's Eve of my second year gave me an even deeper understanding of what it means to be a good soldier. At Mao Zedong Square in Shaoshan, we carried out security duties in full combat gear. As the midnight bell rang in the new year, fireworks burst overhead and ten thousand lights glittered before us. Cold wind poured down my collar; the white breath I exhaled frosted on my face mask. In the distance, people were counting down, people were cheering. In that moment, my heart was unusually calm, and for the first time I felt so clearly the weight of this uniform. Afterward, I often told new soldiers: What does loyalty look like? Sometimes, it looks like a soldier standing at his post — like a steel nail, driven into his battle station.

In 2015, I went abroad for the first time, traveling to Jordan to serve as an instructor. After passing through multiple rounds of selection, I represented the PAP in the coaching team, serving as a combat instructor.

During a training break, a foreign student came up to challenge me. He was half a head taller than me and had studied striking arts and judo overseas. He wore a smile at the corner of his mouth, his eyes full of defiance. His whip kick swept toward me with a rush of air. I spotted the opening, grabbed his right elbow, clamped my left leg around his neck in a cross-body armlock, and locked him down tight. He gritted his teeth and struggled a few times, his face flushing red, and finally tapped the mat — he conceded. As the applause broke out, I said to myself: I haven't brought shame to this uniform.

In 2017, my old wrist injury flared up repeatedly. The doctor looked at the scans, was silent for a moment, then said: "If this keeps up, the hand will be finished…" That day, walking out of the hospital, I stood at the entrance for a long time; the steel nail in my wrist felt heavier than it ever had. I fell into confusion: what is left for a special operations soldier who cannot put force through his hand?

At that moment, my old squad leader's words woke me up: "If you can't be a special operations soldier, you can become an instructor and teach your skills to others."

A nail can hold a broken bone together, but it cannot hold me up when I am sinking. I had to hold myself up. If I could not be the "sharp blade" charging into battle, then I would be the "iron hammer" that forges the sharp blade. In September 2017, after completing my overseas instruction assignment and returning home, I transferred to the PAP Hunan Corps Training Base and took up a post as an instructor.

When I first started leading a squad, I trained hard myself, but the squad's results were a mess. One soldier had poor physical fitness and failed the five-kilometer armed cross-country run. Some said his foundation was too weak to work with. I refused to believe there was a soldier who couldn't be brought along. I carefully drew up a training plan for him and trained alongside him every day. When we ran, if he wanted to stop, I pushed him forward. After countless five-kilometer runs — I lost count of how many — on the day of the final assessment, he finally achieved an "excellent" rating. After crossing the finish line, he crouched on the ground, weeping with joy, and said to me: "Squad leader, I didn't let you down!"

In that moment, my eyes grew hot too. I said nothing — I just patted his shoulder, the same way my squad leader had once patted mine.

Occasionally a new soldier asks me how I got through all these years. I hold out my left hand for them to see and tell them: when a bone breaks, a nail can hold it together; as long as a person has that drive inside, there is no obstacle that cannot be overcome!

(Compiled by Deng Xin and Song Hengye)

My "Blue Helmet" Journey

■ Zhou Juan

Profile: Zhou Juan, head nurse of the Special Services Section at the 960th Hospital of the Joint Logistic Support Force; delegate to the 13th National Congress of Chinese Women; awarded the United Nations "Medal of Honor for Peace" three times; awarded third-class merit citation three times.

Zhou Juan (first from right)

When people speak of female peacekeeping soldiers, they often bestow the flattering titles of "angel" or "rose," as if beneath that blue helmet there glows a layer of romantic soft light. Over ten years, I have worn the blue helmet three times to carry out peacekeeping missions. For me, that shade of blue is more like the deep sea — the more still it is, the more awe-inspiring.

In July 2013, I went abroad for the first time, accompanying a peacekeeping medical team to the South Sudan mission area. On the very first night, no sooner had the lights-out call sounded than dense gunfire and artillery erupted in the distance. In that moment, I truly felt that I was in a real battlefield and sensed the unknown dangers lurking there.

"Beep —" The first time I received a gunshot-wound patient, the shrill alarm of the monitor suddenly sounded, and the patient's pupils were slowly dilating. I drew a deep breath, pressed down the fear and revulsion surging up inside me, and had only one thought in my mind: faster, faster. My comrades and I worked continuously for eight hours, moving back and forth through the ward, making emergency interventions. When the patient's vital signs finally stabilized and our taut nerves suddenly relaxed, I realized my legs had gone weak and my feet felt as though they were treading on cotton.

Through one increasingly dangerous and urgent rescue mission after another, I gradually came to understand: within this military uniform and white coat there is a kind of strength that can press down all the fear in one's heart and make us act without regard for anything else.

When I returned from South Sudan, I thought that experience would slowly fade, but instead it took root in my heart, often waking me in the night, making me hear the moans of patients across the artillery fire. So when the second peacekeeping mission came, I volunteered to go to the more arduous Mali. Some people didn't understand: "Haven't you already gone once?" But I knew that precisely because I had been through the baptism of war, I better understood the value of a veteran in the field.

When I set foot on Malian soil, I planted a small, unremarkable seedling with my own hands in a corner of the camp. I never expected that when I set foot on Malian soil a second time, it had withstood the raging sandstorms of the Sahara and, in the gaps between the artillery fire, had put out branches and leaves, growing into a "tree of peace" bearing witness to Sino-African friendship. Every time I gaze at it, I think of those days of wresting lives from war and disease.

In November 2023, a pre-dawn race against death. A civilian peacekeeper with the United Nations Multidimensional Integrated Stabilization Mission in Mali (MINUSMA) suddenly developed severe chest pain and was rushed to our hospital. He had been in continuous pain for nearly an hour, drenched in sweat, breathing rapidly; the vessel had to be opened as quickly as possible. But the hospital lacked the conditions for interventional surgery, and transferring the patient to the capital Bamako would take more than two hours at the fastest — there simply was not enough time.

The patient's condition was the order! While reporting to our superiors, we immediately began thrombolytic therapy. Oxygen, cardiac monitoring, intravenous infusion… One hour passed with no sign of improvement. A look of despair gradually spread across the patient's face; trembling, he picked up his phone and began recording a video while weeping. I could not understand what he was saying, but I understood — he was leaving a final message for his family far away.

Every tiny fluctuation on the monitor gripped our hearts. Fortunately, the thrombolytic drugs finally took effect. The patient's chest pain gradually eased, and the electrocardiogram curve became strong and smooth again — the vessel was open. I wept with joy. A few days later, the MINUSMA Senior Medical Officer sent a special email highly praising the Chinese hospital's professional competence and humanitarian spirit, and conveying the patient's sincere gratitude.

Three peacekeeping tours, from South Sudan to Mali, from my thirties to my forties. Every time I went on a peacekeeping mission, my family was always very worried, yet they still gave me great support. On that unfamiliar land, I often missed my family, but once I was busy, all those longings were pressed down in my heart. I never dared to relax for a single moment — afraid that one instant of slackness might let a life that should have been saved slip through my hands; afraid that the glory of China's peacekeeping forces might be tarnished by a single mistake of mine…

Over 480 peacekeeping days and nights, the supplies and data I handled numbered in the tens of millions; every item of consumables handed over, every one of the more than 3,000 bilingual forms I filled out — not a single error. When MINUSMA officials came to offer condolences, they saw the supplies I had organized and arranged, and gave me a thumbs-up. Later, MINUSMA produced and broadcast a special video showing a day in my working life at the hospital, which received widespread praise from viewers online. China's Ambassador to the United Nations commented at a meeting: this video fully promoted the positive image of United Nations peacekeeping operations. This was not a showcase of me alone — I was only one representative among many. To be able to strive together with a group of like-minded comrades in a foreign land on behalf of the nation, to show the international community "Chinese standards" and display the image of the Chinese soldier — I feel boundlessly fortunate.

Over the years I have received some honors, but what I treasure most is not the flowers and applause. It is the sense of solid satisfaction that wells up in my heart each time a patient gives a thumbs-up and says "China is great" after a successful treatment.

Now I have come to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau to carry out medical assistance work in Tibet. The cold winds of the plateau are utterly unlike the blazing sun of Africa, but the tempering that comes from a harsh environment is no lighter. Yet wherever I am, my reverence for life and my fulfillment of my mission have never changed. I treat every medical patrol as a sortie, checking the first-aid kit again and again before setting out. From African soil to the snow-covered plateau, what changes is the latitude and longitude; what does not change is my original aspiration to "don white and take up arms, and march toward battle" (白衣执甲、向战而行).

Whenever the order comes, I will still shoulder my pack and walk into any battlefield that needs me, striving to be that battle-field rose that blooms quietly and stands firm against the wind.

(Compiled by Xu Jianfu and Liu Bin)

Precision in the Confined Space, the Course Stretches Far

■ Wang Qilin

Profile: Wang Qilin, helmsman-signalman technician aboard a Navy submarine, First-Class Master Sergeant; awarded second-class merit citation once and third-class merit citation twice.

Wang Qilin

In most people's eyes, the deep sea is a mysterious and romantic world. But for a submarine helmsman-signalman, beneath that dark blue there is no scenery — only the numbers jumping on the instrument panel and the heavy responsibility on one's shoulders. This is the battle station that controls the submarine's course and depth, and it is the bond that ties me tightly to the deep sea of the motherland.

It was a long-distance navigation exercise many years ago. That night the wind and waves were fierce; not long after the submarine left port, it ran into an underwater current. The submarine rocked from side to side, and a wave of dizziness hit instantly. I clenched my teeth, concentrated completely, and held the submarine on the prescribed heading. Just as the submarine was about to clear the current zone, the helmsman beside me noticed something wrong: "The compass needle has failed — the log reading is dropping fast!"

I tried to put the helm hard over to come around, but the rudder angle did not change at all. At that moment, a thought that made me tense surfaced in my mind: the submarine had been caught in a fishing net!

A fishing net in the deep sea is an "invisible killer" that submarines dread encountering. Once entangled, at best it damages the propeller; at worst it leaves the submarine without power, trapped on the seabed.

"Captain, report! The stern propeller is suspected of being fouled in a fishing net!" I drew a deep breath and pressed down the thunderclap in my chest. I knew full well that every second at that moment bore on the safety of the entire boat, and any panic would be fatal.

"Flood the buoyancy trim tanks!" "Ahead full, right full rudder!" Orders rang out one after another in the compartment. I gripped the helm wheel tightly with both hands, mentally retracing the submarine's track and the direction of the current to judge where the net was fouled, while at the same time executing the captain's orders — trying to use the force of the current, combined with the boat's turning, to tear an opening in the net.

As the helm wheel turned, a sharp snapping sound came from beneath the hull. My eyes lit up — that was the sound of the net breaking! I seized that fleeting opportunity without hesitation and rapidly adjusted the submarine's attitude. The submarine slid smoothly forward, like a deep-sea leviathan breaking free of its bonds.

"Heading restored to normal!" The young helmsman's voice was full of excitement. Only then did my taut nerves finally relax, and I realized my back was already soaked through with sweat.

That heart-stopping escape once again confirmed the saying: in the deep sea, details are the boundary between life and death.

Over the years, young helmsmen have often asked me: "Why can your hands stay so steady even in a crisis?" I tell them: "The submarine is our home. You have to get to know every valve and pipe, and understand its 'temperament' (脾气) in different sea states." This is pressed out second by second over time. Behind every precise dive and ascent, every effort to maintain "zero depth deviation" (深度差零) in surging swells, lie tens of thousands of tedious and rigorous training repetitions.

On the eve of a major mission, an expert proposed that, to raise the mission standard, the submarine's depth deviation should be controlled within 0.5 meters. Anyone familiar with submarines knows that the underwater environment is complex and ever-changing; controlling a behemoth of several thousand tons with such precision is no easy matter.

"We can try!" Facing the challenge, I did not hesitate. In the sea training that followed, I always brought a video camera specifically to record my own operating movements. After returning to port, I played the footage frame by frame and studied it repeatedly. "This rudder angle was too small," "This time the rudder correction was too late"… I recorded every detail in my notebook and engraved it in my mind.

On the day of the mission, I locked my eyes on the depth gauge, gripped the helm wheel with both hands, and reported immediately upon reaching the prescribed depth, successfully completing the mission: I not only held the depth deviation within 0.1 meters, but also reduced the heading deviation to nearly half the target figure that had been set in advance.

Precision in the confined space, the course stretches far. Thirty years have flowed by in silence, like deep-sea currents. From the breath-holding concentration of my early days facing complex sea states, to the calm composure of today, knowing the submarine's every temperament intimately — my hands have long since become one with this confined helm wheel. This silent track is the heartfelt declaration I have written to the deep blue ocean and to the great motherland.

(Compiled by Guo Zhengyang)

Releasing "Eagles" on the Grassland

■ Zhang Huru

Profile: Zhang Huru, Third-Class Master Sergeant in an Army unit; awarded third-class merit citation for national defense science and technology once; holder of six national patents.

Zhang Huru

The wind of the grassland is the true master here. It is nothing like the gentle, soft breezes of ordinary waterways and forests. This wind is hard and stubborn, blowing tirelessly year-round, carrying the scent of grass clippings and sand. Our small position, like a solitary stone on an endless green carpet, is often set slightly trembling by the wind here.

My work, like this wind, has a hard, unyielding quality. Others say we are "eagle handlers" — responsible for launching target drones, providing aerial targets specifically for air defense weapons and equipment trials.

My "eagle" lies quietly on the tow vehicle right now. It has none of the wild spirit and agility of a grassland eagle; it is gray-white all over, its contours clean and clear, looking more like a stone polished by wind and sand, or an unsharpened blunt sword. Its mission, from the moment of its creation, is to be "destroyed."

I walk a circle around it, fingers brushing the cold fuselage, checking every screw. Connecting cables, calibrating — every action has been repeated thousands of times, as familiar as breathing. Finally, I pat its "shoulder." That cold metal shell seems to be bidding me a silent farewell.

"Partner, today it's all up to you."

The launch order is given. The booster suddenly spews a blazing white flame, and the tremendous roar drowns out the wind. It breaks free of the earth, trailing a plume of exhaust as it surges toward the blue sky. Soon it becomes a small black dot, and under my control it begins to weave and soar across that blue backdrop along the carefully designed flight path.

An aerial contest begins. I stare intently at the screen, where its altitude, speed, and bearing flash and jump. My fingers move across the buttons of the remote controller, sending it flight commands, making it simulate the flight attitude of an enemy aircraft. It is like a solitary dancer on a vast stage, performing a story of tragic grandeur.

"Prepare to fire!" Hearing the sound from the intercom, my heart tightens slightly. On the distant horizon, a pinpoint of light suddenly blazes, then stretches into a white arc, and with a coldness of absolute precision, it drives toward my target drone.

This is a contest measured in seconds. I issue rapid commands, immediately releasing all the decoy flares in an attempt to create interference. In that moment, I feel as if I myself am sitting in that target drone, enduring enormous g-forces, the air squeezed violently from my lungs, watching that white light draw closer and closer, closer and closer…

A thunderous boom echoes across the sky, and the point of light on the screen vanishes in an instant. Outside the window, a small puff of smoke hangs against the deep blue backdrop — the trace left by the "eagle" I released. I let go of the control stick and look at that patch of sky — it was just there, burning fiercely, and then it scattered cleanly and completely.

The mission over, I slowly uncurl my fingers, which have gone white from gripping, and exhale a long breath. Countless days and nights of painstaking research, thousands of rehearsals of flight paths and parameters — all for this moment of real-world testing of weapons and equipment combat capability.

Walking out of the shelter, the long wind of the grassland instantly envelops me, still that fierce, hard, tenacious gust. In the distance, the horizon is soaked in the last light of the setting sun, spreading a warm, deep copper-red. Several comrades bend their heads to sort out equipment, talking about the scene of the missile hitting the target drone, their voices calm and relaxed, carrying the quiet ease that comes after a mission is complete.

Day after day the north wind sweeps tirelessly across the grassland; so too are we — rooted in this open wilderness, cycling back and forth between the workshop and the test range, content to be a silent whetstone, so that the sharp sword guarding ten thousand li of sky may be honed ever keener.

Night gradually closes in, and scattered lights flicker to life in the distance. Tomorrow, new "eagles" will be sent skyward by us, and new "eagle hunters" will come slicing through the air. We brushed the dust from our clothes and walked toward those lights.

(Compiled by Zhao Xian)

班长摸透了我骨子里不服输的劲儿，故意给我浇了一盆冷水，寥寥几句便让我清醒过来。习武那么多年都没放弃，入伍时决心想当个好兵，怎么能说走就走？ 冬季，集体训练结束后，我还吊在单杠上面，嘴里呵出的白气一团一团的。在雪地里练匍匐，手冻得发肿，但我没觉得苦。 后来考核，我再次名列前茅。班长拍着我的肩膀喊了一声：“好样的！”那声夸赞，我一直记到现在。也是从那时候起，我开始慢慢懂了——一个好兵有两个面，一面是冲锋夺胜的锐气，一面是受挫不折的韧劲，纵使摔倒，也总能咬牙站起。 第二年的除夕夜，让我对一个好兵的理解更加深刻。在韶山毛泽东广场，我们全副武装执行警戒任务。跨年的钟声敲响，烟花在头顶炸开，眼前是万家灯火。寒风灌进领口，呼出的白气在面罩上结霜。远处有人在倒数，有人在欢呼。那一刻，我心里反而特别平静，第一次那么清楚地感觉到这身军装的重量。后来，我常跟新兵讲：忠诚是什么样？有时候，它就是一个兵站在哨位上的样子——像一枚钢钉，钉在战位上。 2015年，我第一次走出国门，远赴约旦执教。经过层层遴选，我代表武警部队进入教练小组，担任搏击教练员。 训练间隙，一名外军学员上来挑战。他比我高半个头，曾在海外学过技击和柔道。他的嘴角带着笑，眼神里写满了不服气。他鞭腿扫过来，带起风声。我看准破绽，一把抓住他的右肘，左腿夹其脖颈形成十字固，死死锁住。他咬着牙挣扎了几下，脸涨得通红，最后拍了拍垫子——认输了。掌声响起来的时候，我在心里跟自己说：没给这身军装丢脸。 2017年，我的手腕旧伤反复。医生看了片子，沉默了一会儿后说道：“再这样下去，手就废了……”那天走出医院，我在门口站了很久，手腕里的钢钉仿佛比任何时候都沉。我陷入了迷茫：一个特战队员，手不能发力，还剩什么？ 此时，老班长的一句话点醒了我：“当不了特战队员，你可以去当教员，把你的本事教给别人。” 钉子撑住了一根断骨，可它撑不住我往下坠。我得靠自己撑住，当不了冲锋陷阵的“尖刀”，那就做锻造尖刀的“铁锤”。2017年9月，我完成出国执教任务回国后，调入武警湖南总队训练基地，走上教员岗位。 刚开始带班，我自己练得刻苦，全班成绩却是一团糟。有一个战士，体能差，5公里武装越野成绩不及格。有人说他底子太薄，不好带。我不信有带不好的兵。我为他精心制订训练计划，每天陪他一起训练。练跑步时，他想停，我就推着他跑。不知道跑了多少个5公里后，在结业考核那天，他终于达标“优秀”。冲过终点后，他蹲在地上，喜极而泣，对我说：“班长，我没给你丢人！” 那一刻，我的眼睛也热了。我什么都没说，只是拍了拍他的肩膀，就和当年班长拍我肩膀一样。 偶尔有新兵问我，这些年是怎么扛过来的。我就把左手伸给他们看，跟他们说：骨头断了，打钉子能撑起来；人只要心里有那股劲儿，就没有过不去的坎！ （邓鑫、宋恒烨整理） 我的“蓝盔”征途 ■周 娟 人物名片：周娟，联勤保障部队第960医院特勤科护士长，中国妇女第十三次全国代表大会代表，3次被授予联合国“和平荣誉勋章”，荣立三等功3次。 周娟（右一） 提起维和女军人，人们常冠以“天使”“玫瑰”的美称，仿佛那顶蓝盔之下自带一层浪漫柔光。10年间，我三度头戴蓝盔，执行维和任务。对我而言，那抹蓝色更像是深海——越是沉静，越让人敬畏。 2013年7月，我第一次走出国门，跟随维和医疗队远赴南苏丹任务区。第一天晚上，熄灯号刚刚响过，远处便传来密集的枪炮声。那一刻，我才真切体会到自己置身于真实的战场，感受到那里潜藏的未知与风险。 “嘀——”第一次收治枪伤伤员，监护仪刺耳的警报骤然响起，伤员的瞳孔在一点点涣散。我深吸一口气，将翻涌而上的恐惧和不适压在心底，脑中只剩一个念头：快一点，再快一点。我和战友们连续救治8个小时，在病房里不断穿梭、紧急处置。待到伤员的生命体征稳定下来，紧绷的神经骤然松弛，我才发觉双腿发软，脚下像踩着一团棉花。 往后一次次愈发凶险紧急的救援任务里，我渐渐懂得：这身戎装与白衣里藏着一种力量，可以压下心底所有惶恐，可以让我们不顾一切。 从南苏丹回来，我以为这段经历会慢慢淡去，可它反而在心里扎下了根，时常在夜里把我叫醒，让我听见那些隔着炮火的伤员呻吟声。因此，当第二次维和任务来临，我主动申请去更艰苦的马里。有人不解：“不是去过一次了吗？”可我知道，正是因为经历过战火的洗礼，我才更懂得一个老队员在战场上的价值。 踏上马里的土地时，我在营区一角亲手种下一株不起眼的小苗。没想到，当我第二次踏上马里的土地，它竟扛住了撒哈拉肆虐的风沙，在炮火的缝隙间抽枝展叶，长成了一棵见证中非友谊的“和平树”。每次凝望它，我都会想起那些与战火、与疫病争夺生命的日子。 2023年11月，一个与生命赛跑的凌晨。联合国马里多层面综合稳定特派团（以下简称“联马团”）一名民事维和人员突发剧烈胸痛，被紧急送至我们的医院。他已持续疼痛近1个小时，大汗淋漓，呼吸急促，必须尽快打通血管。可医院不具备介入手术条件，将伤员转运到首都巴马科最快也需要两个多小时，根本来不及。 伤情就是命令！我们一边向上级报告，一边立即为他进行溶栓治疗。吸氧、心电监护、静脉输液……1个小时过去，病情仍不见缓解。伤员脸上渐渐露出绝望的神情，颤抖着拿起手机，边流泪边录视频。我听不懂他说的话，但我明白，他是在给远方的家人留下遗言。 监护仪上每一个细微的波动，都牵动着我们的心。幸运的是，溶栓药物终于起效了。伤员的胸痛渐渐缓解，心电图曲线重新变得有力而舒展——血管疏通了。我喜极而泣。几天后，联马团高级医疗官专门发来邮件，高度赞扬了中国医院的专业素养和人道主义精神，并转达了患者诚挚的谢意。 3次维和，从南苏丹到马里，从而立之年到不惑之年。每次我去执行维和任务时，家人虽然总是很担忧，但仍然给予我很大支持。我在那片陌生的土地，也时常思念家人，但忙碌起来所有牵挂便暂且压在心里。我始终不敢有丝毫松懈，怕自己一瞬间的懈怠，让一条本该救回来的生命在手中滑落；怕中国维和部队的荣光，因为我的一个差错而蒙尘…… 480个维和日夜，我经手物资、数据数以千万计，交接的每份耗材、填写的3000多张双语报表，无一差错。联马团官员慰问时，看到我整理摆放的物资，对我竖起了大拇指。后来，联马团专门制播了一期展示我在医院一天工作生活情况的视频，获得网友好评。中国驻联合国大使在一次会议上评价：这个视频充分宣传了联合国维和行动的正面形象。这不是我一个人的形象展示，我只是其中一个代表而已。能和一群志同道合的战友，代表国家奋斗在异国他乡，向国际社会展示“中国标准”、展现中国军人形象，我感到无比的幸福。 这些年，我获得了一些荣誉，但我最珍视的不是鲜花与掌声，而是每次救治成功后，患者竖起大拇指说“China is great”时，心底涌起的那份踏实。 如今，我踏上青藏高原，开展援藏医疗帮扶。高原的冷风虽与非洲的烈日截然不同，但恶劣环境带来的磨砺却难分轻重。可无论在哪里，我对生命的敬畏、对使命的践行，从未改变。我把每一次巡诊当作一次出征，出发前把急救箱检查一遍又一遍。从非洲土地到雪域高原，变换的是经纬度，不变的是我“白衣执甲、向战而行”的初心。 只要一声令下，我仍会背起行囊，走进任何一片需要我的战场，努力成为那朵静静绽放、迎风挺立的战地玫瑰。 （徐建甫、刘宾整理） 方寸有度，航迹自远 ■王启林 人物名片：王启林，海军某潜艇舵信技师、一级军士长，荣立二等功1次、三等功2次。 王启林 在多数人眼里，深海是神秘而浪漫的世界。但对于一名潜艇舵信兵来说，这片幽蓝之下没有风景，只有仪表盘上跳动的数字和肩上沉甸甸的责任。这里是控制潜艇航线和深度的战位，也是我与祖国深海紧紧相连的纽带。 那是一次多年前的远航训练。当天夜里风浪很大，潜艇驶出港口没多久，便遭遇了海底暗流。潜艇左右摇晃，眩晕感瞬间袭来。我咬紧牙关、全神贯注，将潜艇控制在规定航向。就在潜艇即将驶离暗流区时，身边的舵手发现了异常：“罗经盘指针失灵，计程仪读数迅速下降！” 我试图打满舵回旋，但舵角没有丝毫变化。此刻，一个令我紧张的念头浮现在脑海：潜艇被渔网困住了！ 深海中的渔网，是潜艇不愿遇到的“隐形杀手”。一旦被缠住，轻则损伤螺旋桨，重则让潜艇失去动力，困在海底。 “报告艇长！艉部螺旋桨疑似绞缠渔网！”我深吸一口气，压下心头的惊雷。我深知，此刻的每一秒都关乎全艇安危，任何慌乱都是致命的。 “浮力调整水舱注水！”“前进四，右满舵！”舱内口令声此起彼伏，我双手紧紧握住舵轮，一边在脑海中复盘潜艇的航行轨迹和水流方向，判断渔网缠绕位置；一边执行艇长的指令，尝试利用水流的力量，配合艇体转向，撕开渔网一个口子。 随着舵轮的转动，“嘣”的一声异响从艇底传来，我眼神一亮——这是渔网断裂的声音！我果断抓住这稍纵即逝的时机，迅速调整潜艇姿态。潜艇平稳地向前滑出，如同挣脱束缚的深海巨鲸。 “航向恢复正常！”年轻舵手的声音里充满了激动。我紧绷的神经终于放松了下来，才发现汗水早已浸湿了后背。 这次惊心动魄的脱困经历，再次印证了那句话：在深海，细节就是生与死的边界。 这些年，经常有年轻的舵手问我：“为什么在危急的情况下，你的手还能保持那么稳？”我跟他们说：“潜艇就是我们的家。对潜艇的阀门、管路，以及它在不同海况下的‘脾气’，都要摸清楚才行。”这是时间一分一秒压出来的。每一次精准的下潜与上浮，每一次在涌浪中努力保持“深度差零”的稳定，背后都是成千上万次枯燥而严苛的训练。 一次重大任务前夕，有专家提出，为提高任务标准，潜艇深度偏差范围要控制在0.5米以内。熟悉潜艇的人都知道，水下环境复杂多变，想把这数千吨重的庞然大物控制得如此精准，绝非易事。 “可以一试！”面对挑战，我毫不犹豫。之后的出海训练，我都会带上一个摄像机，专门用来记录自己的操作动作。返航后，我逐帧播放视频、反复研究。“这个舵角小了”“这次舵回晚了”……我把一个个细节都记在本子上、刻进脑子里。 任务当天，我双眼牢牢锁定深度计，两手紧握舵轮，到达预定深度后迅即报告，成功完成了任务：我不仅将深度偏差控制在0.1米内，航向偏差范围也比预先提出的指标缩小了近一半。 方寸有度，航迹自远。30年光阴，如深海暗流般无声淌过。从最初面对复杂海况时的屏息凝神，到如今对潜艇脾性了如指掌的从容不迫，我的双手早已与这方寸舵轮融为一体。这无声的航迹，便是我向深蓝大洋、向伟大祖国写下的深情告白。 （郭争洋整理） 草原放“鹰” ■张虎儒 人物名片：张虎儒，陆军某部三级军士长，荣立国防科技三等功1次，获得国家专利6项。 张虎儒 草原的风，是这里真正的主人。它不像寻常水岸林间的风，温软轻柔。这里的风刚硬又执拗，一年到头不知疲倦地刮着，裹挟着草屑和沙土的气息。我们的小小阵地，宛若无垠绿毯间一块孤石，时常被这里的风吹得微微晃动。
