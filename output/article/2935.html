Put More "Dumb Hard Work" (笨功夫) Into Leading Troops

带兵多下“笨功夫”
PLA Daily (解放军报) 5 August 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
A PLA political work commentary uses Battalion Commander Li's four-year tenure — during which his unnamed battalion earned 'Four-Iron' (四铁) commendations, a collective third-class merit citation, and produced multiple competition medalists — to argue that grassroots commanders must prioritize 笨功夫 ('dumb hard work': unglamorous, step-by-step daily effort) over shortcuts that generate visible results but hollow out combat effectiveness. The piece documents a recurring institutional concern: that promotion incentives push company- and battalion-level leaders toward 显功 (visible work) at the expense of 潜功 (latent work), degrading the foundational training and discipline that actually determine unit readiness. Its value is as a record of how PLA political media frames the tension between career incentives and genuine force building for junior officers, not as evidence of a new policy direction.
Translation

Battalion Commander Li of a certain unit served four years in his position and turned a battalion that was originally unremarkable into a model for the entire regiment. His unit won gold and silver medals in higher-echelon skills competitions, dozens of backbone personnel grew into seasoned experts, the internal atmosphere was harmonious, and officer and soldier morale ran high. The unit was successively commended as an advanced unit for the "Four-Iron" (四铁) standard and was awarded a collective third-class merit citation. Battalion Commander Li himself was promoted on the strength of his outstanding performance. When people asked him for the secret to leading troops, he answered: "Put in 'dumb hard work' (笨功夫)."

What is "putting in 'dumb hard work'"? Leafing through Battalion Commander Li's troop-leading journal may provide the answer. His "dumb hard work" is not inaction — it is directing energy precisely where combat effectiveness generation needs it most. When he organized training, he never used "crash methods": he skipped not a single step, omitted not a single detail, drilling each movement to standard one by one and running every subject through the sieve one by one. When he led his unit, he avoided not a single contradiction and sidestepped not a single problem, keeping a constant and clear picture of the ideological dynamics and practical difficulties of every officer and soldier in the battalion. When he managed discipline, he did not let things blow through like "a gust of wind" but instead focused on the day-to-day — work style and discipline, safety management, daily order — with someone responsible for every matter and someone watching every link. Over four years, there were no grand, sweeping "bold strokes," yet through day-after-day "dumb hard work" he forged a battle-ready force that can be deployed, can hold the line, and can win — and in doing so earned the recognition of the organization and the trust of his officers and soldiers.

Savoring this "dumb hard work" carefully, one discovers that behind it lies a rare clarity: this path, which appears slow, is precisely the most solid route to success. Grassroots work is a tangle of a thousand threads; those who lead troops are easily led astray by the character "clever" (巧), always thinking about taking shortcuts, producing highlights, and seeing quick results — only to find that haste makes waste. Force building has its own objective laws. The generation of combat effectiveness is an accumulation process that moves from quantitative to qualitative change. Attempting to leap over the gradual accumulation of daily effort and drive straight to results through "clever shortcuts" will most likely fail to withstand the test of practice. One must understand: every "necessary passage" that is bypassed will ultimately become an "unavoidable detour back." The "real work" (实功夫) that is skipped will become a "historical debt" that must, sooner or later, be repaid with interest.

Put more "dumb hard work" into leading troops. This "dumb hard work" is, at bottom, holding to one's duty, consolidating fundamental skills, and driving the refinement of military skills and the strengthening of capabilities to the utmost. One must honestly follow the laws governing things — not be impatient, not advance recklessly, not muddle through — and discard the mentality of seeking quick results, so that every step falls on the beat of combat effectiveness generation. Do not rely on momentary cleverness; rely instead on pragmatic rootedness, long-term perseverance, and hard, solid work — using the "measuring stick" of actual combat to produce the "real moves" of training. This road is walked slowly, but every step is solid; it is unassuming, yet its effects accumulate over time.

Putting more "dumb hard work" into leading troops is easy to say and hard to do. At present, the tasks of training and war preparation in the force are heavy, the tempo is fast, and the pressure is great. The more this is so, the more it is necessary to calm the mind and do both visible work (显功) and latent work (潜功). Those who lead troops should have the breadth of mind to accept that "success need not come on my watch" (功成不必在我) — not coveting short-term achievement, not seeking short-term fame, willing to do the work that lies "below the horizon," consolidating the foundation and building a solid base. They should have the exacting spirit of "measuring with one ruler all the way to the end" (一把尺子量到底) — not lowering training standards by a single grade, not letting a single instance of work-style cultivation slide, implementing work item by item and polishing it piece by piece, running the character "solid" (实) through the entire process of leading troops. They should have the steadiness of "advancing one pawn a day" (日拱一卒) — sinking down to the front line, anchoring to the battle position, undisturbed by clamor and undeluded by the lure of quick results, building strength through accumulation in day-after-day hard work, and forging a battle-ready force through year-after-year solid effort.

Original Chinese
某部李营长，任职4年，把一个原本并不突出的营带成了全团标杆。所在单位在上级比武中摘金夺银，数十名骨干成长为行家里手，内部氛围融洽、官兵士气高涨，先后被表彰为“四铁”先进单位，荣立集体三等功。李营长本人也因实绩突出，职位得到提升。有人向他请教带兵秘诀，他回答：“下‘笨功夫’。” 何为“下‘笨功夫’”？翻看李营长的带兵日志，或许会找到答案。他的“笨功夫”，不是无为，而是把精力用在战斗力生成最需要的地方。他搞训练，从不搞“速成法”，不跳一个步骤、不漏一个细节，一个动作一个动作抠标准，一个课目一个课目过筛子；他带队伍，不回避一个矛盾、不绕开一个问题，对全营官兵的思想动态和实际困难始终心中有数；他抓管理，不刮“一阵风”，而是注重日常，作风纪律、安全管理、日常秩序，每件事都有人管、每个环节都有人盯。4年下来，没有轰轰烈烈的“大手笔”，却靠着日复一日的“笨功夫”，带出了一支拉得出、顶得上、打得赢的过硬队伍，也赢得了组织的认可和官兵的信任。 细细品味这种“笨功夫”，会发现其背后藏着一种难得的清醒：这条看似缓慢的路，恰恰是通往成功最扎实的路径。基层工作千头万绪，带兵人容易被“巧”字带偏方向，总想着走捷径、出亮点、快见效，结果往往欲速则不达。部队建设有自身的客观规律，战斗力的生成，是一个由量变到质变的积蓄过程。试图跳过日积月累，用“取巧”的方式直奔结果，最终很可能经不起实践的检验。须知，所有绕开的“必经之路”，最终都会变成绕不过去的“回头路”。省下的“实功夫”，终将成为欠下的“历史账”，迟早要加倍偿还。 带兵多下“笨功夫”。这种“笨功夫”，说到底就是守住本分，夯实基本功，把精武强能练到极致。要老老实实按规律办事，不急躁、不冒进、不糊弄，摒弃急于求成的心态，让每一步都踩在战斗力生成的节拍上。不靠一时讨巧，靠的是务实扎根、长期坚守、苦干实干，用实战的“尺子”卡出训练的“实招”。这条路，虽走得慢，但步步扎实；不起眼，却久久为功。 带兵多下“笨功夫”，知易行难。当前，部队练兵备战任务重、节奏快、压力大，越是如此，越需要静下心来，既做显功也做潜功。带兵人应当有“功成不必在我”的胸襟，不贪一时之功、不图一时之名，甘于做那些“地平线以下”的工作，把基础夯实、把底子打牢。应当有“一把尺子量到底”的较真精神，对训练标准不降一格、对作风养成不放一处，将工作一桩桩落实、一件件打磨，把“实”字贯穿带兵全过程。应当有“日拱一卒”的定力，沉到一线、铆在战位，不被喧嚣所扰、不为速成所惑，在日复一日的苦干中积厚成势，在年复一年的实干中带出过硬队伍。
