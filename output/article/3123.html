Entering Military Academy Cadets' Summer "Required Course": Witnessing Their Growth and Transformation at Their Combat Posts

走进军校学员的暑期“必修课”，见证学员们在战位上的成长蜕变
PLA Daily (解放军报) 11 August 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
Naval Aviation University flight cadets completed a 48-hour air-dropped survival exercise on an uninhabited island in Bohai Bay; Joint Logistic Support Force Engineering University cadets ran unannounced fuel-depot fire drills at a coastal installation; and Air Force Engineering University cadets conducted aircraft maintenance internships at a desert base, with the article presenting all three as components of the standard summer troop-internship (随军实习) cycle. The piece documents how PLA military academies structure the theory-to-practice transition for pre-commission cadets across aviation, logistics, and maintenance specialties — the framing around 血性品格 (fighting spirit) and zero-error maintenance discipline points to institutional concern that classroom training alone does not produce combat-ready junior officers. The article's value is as a baseline record of the academy internship model and its political-work framing, not as evidence of a capability or posture change.
Translation

Military Academy Cadets' Summer "Required Course"

The summer blazes; youth is tempered in fire. Though the semester has ended, military academy cadets have not stopped striving. Leaving campus, they set off for grassroots units, honing their skills and steeling their spirit in the heat of summer internships.

From tactical training grounds thick with the smoke of battle, to uninhabited desolate islands fraught with danger, to aircraft maintenance aprons roaring with jet engines, cadets have gone deep into the front lines of training for war, converting what they learned in the classroom into real-combat capabilities, and tempering the fighting spirit (血性品格) of qualified servicemembers.

Interning with troops is a "two-way convergence" of theory and practice—a "required course" for military academy cadets. In this issue, let us enter the special classroom of the summer internship and witness the cadets' growth and transformation at their combat posts.

Sea-and-Sky "Fledgling Eagles" Break Out from a Desolate Island

■ Liu Jing, Liu Meiyuan

[Caption: Naval Aviation University flight cadets conduct field survival training. Photo by Gao Shansong]

Midsummer, Bohai Bay. While many of their peers were enjoying a leisurely summer vacation, flight cadets from Naval Aviation University were air-dropped onto an uninhabited desolate island—where they would complete 48 hours of field survival training in this scorching, desperate environment.

"Jump! Jump!" At the command, cadets leapt from the hatch, and clusters of parachute blossoms opened above the desolate island. The moment they landed, Group One leader Hao Wei and Group Two leader Jin Peng blew their assembly whistles and rapidly rallied their teams. Each group of cadets was required to carry or haul a log throughout—serving as a "casualty"—and within a set time, pass through a "contaminated" zone and an "enemy" blockade line, and complete a series of challenges including cliff climbing, rope traversal, and battlefield first aid.

Under the scorching sun, the sandy ground was soft and yielding; every step felt like treading on burning cotton. "Contaminated zone ahead!" Hao Wei gave a decisive order: "Wet your towels and cover your mouths and noses—follow me, charge!" He led the way, advancing rapidly from the upwind flank, with his team members close behind.

Meanwhile, Jin Peng and his team members found their path cut off by a near-vertical sea-eroded cliff face. The cliff was overgrown with thorns, and loose stones tumbled down intermittently. "I'll go first!" Jin Peng climbed hand and foot; his knees were scraped bloody. The team members below braced his feet on their shoulders and heaved together, slowly pushing the log up to the cliff top.

The vicious sun licked at the beach; their combat uniforms soaked through and dried again, leaving white crusts of salt. As physical strength was rapidly drained, what was even more demoralizing was the complete absence of fresh water and food.

The cadets climbed to the top of the cliff and had barely caught their breath when the exercise control group issued a first-aid task: stop bleeding, perform CPR, apply bandages and immobilization. Everyone forced themselves to crouch down and, with trembling hands, completed the precise procedures. After receiving a "qualified" assessment, the cadets rested briefly and then realized their canteens were empty. Jin Peng shared the last few sips of water with his comrades and forced himself to recall what he had learned in class: filter fresh water from seawater using desalination agents, and collect water droplets through solar distillation. While searching for water, he called out in delight: "Cape gooseberries! They're edible!" Everyone rushed to pick and eat them; a faint sweetness spread across their lips and teeth, and their fighting spirit rekindled within.

At dusk, a drone air-dropped camping supplies. Group One retrieved rope, parachute cloth, and an engineer's shovel; Hao Wei and his team members selected tree trunks as supports and used the parachute cloth to erect a wind-resistant tent. Group Two arrived a step late and received only parachute cloth, a survival knife, and triangular bandages. Working in the dark, they whittled wooden stakes and drove them into the hard ground, erecting a sturdy conical tent. On the first night on the desolate island, the sea wind howled outside the tents, swarms of mosquitoes bit at them, physical strength had long been pushed to its limit—but conviction was the unextinguished light in the cadets' hearts.

On the second day, Group One, which had been leading throughout, suddenly became lost in dense forest. Carrying their "casualty," they crossed an entire mountain, only to find it was not their destination. Exhaustion, hunger, and despair erupted all at once, and what Hao Wei had feared most came to pass—they were going in the wrong direction. He led the team to an open area and, his voice hoarse, pointed to a tree stump: "Look at the growth rings—the side with wider spacing faces the sun. Combined with watch-based orientation, we go this way!" His crisp, forceful words steadied the team's resolve, pulling the disoriented group back together as one. Everyone encouraged each other, shouldered the log, and forged a path through the thorn-covered wilderness.

At that same moment, Group Two, led by Jin Peng, was crossing a swift-flowing river. The cadets hung suspended above the torrent; the river, churning with silt, beat violently against their bodies, and the rope left deep marks on their palms. Though their legs trembled uncontrollably, they gritted their teeth and pressed toward the opposite bank. The first cadets to arrive used a signal mirror to send a distress signal to a drone, then arranged stones on the beach to form a large "SOS." The previous day they had also gotten lost in a valley; now, by achieving the fastest time on the rope traversal, they reversed the score.

On the desolate island, fierce competition raged. Cadets charged toward their limits with solid skills and iron will. Some had their shoulders rubbed raw and bleeding but never let the log touch the ground; others, feet covered in blisters, still pressed forward over jagged rocks. On the final night, the exercise control group air-dropped chicken, potatoes, and seasoning as a reward for those who persevered to the end. Everyone gathered dry grass and dead branches to make fire by friction; sparks flew and died again and again, sweat dripped onto the wood shavings, and arms ached too much to lift—but no one stopped. When the first tongue of flame licked up through the kindling and lit up those young faces, they let out a spontaneous roar in unison.

The meat sizzled and gave off a long-missed aroma; everyone sat around the campfire drinking hot broth, and all their exhaustion transformed into a burning light in their eyes. From the parade ground to an unfamiliar island reef, from single-subject drills to continuous emergency scenarios, they proved with 48 hours of survival in a desperate situation: true inner strength grows, little by little, when there is no way to retreat. This was a tempering experience close to actual combat, and a necessary trial by fire on the path from flight cadet to pilot.

Guarding Every Valve with Care

■ Liu Xinhao

[Caption: Joint Logistic Support Force Engineering University cadets conduct fuel-dispensing operations. Photo by Li Weiliang]

Late at night, an emergency assembly whistle pierced the air above a military fuel depot. Dense fog blanketed the tank farm; an unannounced fire-response drill under low-visibility conditions suddenly commenced.

This was the third week of cadet Luo Yi from Joint Logistic Support Force Engineering University's on-post internship with the troops. His combat post was concealed within a closed depot area by the sea, where he spent his days working with fuel storage tanks and oil pipelines. Before this, he had repeatedly memorized flange seal parameters, practiced valve switching over and over, and run through emergency response procedures in a simulation system against the clock. When the alarm sounded, even though he had made the corresponding preparations, his heartbeat still quickened involuntarily.

"No. 3 diesel storage tank flange seal failure; static electricity has ignited oil vapor; oil-gas concentration continues to exceed the threshold…" The alert was the order. Luo Yi and his supervising Sergeant Fang formed a two-person work team, donned anti-static gear and flame-resistant gloves, used a handheld anemometer to verify wind direction, and established safe operating boundaries. The procedural prerequisites repeatedly emphasized in class, the lines of bold text in the textbook—at this moment they carried the weight of a thousand pounds.

Because oil-gas density is greater than air, low-lying areas with pooled oil were the points of highest deflagration risk. Each work team conducted layered inspections along the oil containment berm from low to high. At the same time, operations near the coast required avoiding oil-gas vortices stirred up by sea breezes—a variable the cadets encountered in practical training that was absent from the classroom.

"Level-one alarm!" The detector reading jumped to the lower explosive limit threshold. Luo Yi signaled a red alert with his hand and simultaneously reported concentration data to the command group, designating a level-one exclusion zone.

With the perimeter established, response operations commenced simultaneously. Sergeant Fang stepped forward in a single stride and shut off the storage tank's emergency cutoff valve. Luo Yi helped his comrade unroll the fire hose and open the foam pipeline valve for the corresponding tank.

Sea fog mixed with high-temperature oil vapor pressed against his face shield; his field of vision narrowed to the nozzle in front of him and the oil surface with open flame. When the response was complete and they withdrew safely, looking at the white foam residue coating the cuffs of his protective suit, Luo Yi suddenly understood: every class in military academy is closely connected to the combat post; every set of parameters he had memorized, every operation he had practiced, ultimately had to translate into the reality of safeguarding security.

On the way back, the sea fog gradually lifted. Outside the depot's perimeter wall, warm yellow lights spread along the coastline, extending all the way to the distant fishing harbor. Gazing at the expanse of homes and hearths in the distance, Luo Yi's thoughts drifted back to a night two years earlier during recruit training.

It was also an emergency assembly with no warning. Luo Yi hurriedly packed his kit, donned his gear, and followed the formation to the iron fence at the perimeter of the barracks. The cold railings stood straight before him; on one side were the urgent commands of the military camp, on the other were residents strolling along the road and children running and playing.

Two completely different lives collided before his eyes. The squad leader in charge, Sergeant Zhang, noticed Luo Yi's distraction. During a rest break, he came to Luo Yi's side, tapped the fence lightly with his fingertip, and said, gazing into the distance: "Look at the two sides of this railing—on one side is training for war, on the other is peaceful life. There are thousands of posts in the military; the places you stand are different, the work you do is different, but in essence it's all the same thing—protecting the peaceful lights out there."

"You don't always need to think about standing in the most prominent place. As long as you can safeguard the good lives of the common people, you're a qualified soldier." At the time, Luo Yi had not fully grasped his squad leader's words; he simply kept them quietly in his heart. Until this moment, standing inside the depot's perimeter wall, with a squad leader still beside him explaining things—but he was no longer the raw recruit who had fumbled even with packing his kit.

"If we guard every valve well, the comrades up front will have the confidence to carry out their missions, and the thousands of families behind us can live in peace and happiness." Luo Yi gripped his detector firmly, his heart resolute. The cadets interning alongside him had also shed the bewilderment of their first days at the academy; after years of institutional training and tempering by the troops, they had undergone a complete transformation. From following orders to taking initiative, their capabilities had grown step by step, and the responsibilities on their shoulders had grown layer by layer—yet that original aspiration of "guarding" had never changed.

Reading the "Arithmetic Problems" at the Combat Post

■ Yang Zhengzhong, He Gege

[Caption: Air Force Engineering University cadets conduct hands-on training under the guidance of maintenance backbone personnel. Photo by Wu Zelin]

High noon, the desert sun blazing overhead. Air Force Engineering University cadet Zhao Rui crouched beneath a fighter jet's wing; the moment his fingertips touched the aircraft skin, he recoiled from the searing heat. This summer, he had voluntarily applied to participate in a post internship at a base deep in the desert. He had assumed that with his solid theoretical knowledge, getting up to speed on support work would not be too difficult—but he had not expected to hit a wall on his very first day.

During the pre-takeoff routine inspection, Zhao Rui worked through the operating procedures item by item, confirmed that interface connections were secure and parameters displayed normally, then stood up to signal completion. But Sergeant Yang Bo did not nod in approval. He pointed to a pipeline fitting: "Feel it again. The desert has heavy wind and sand; the wear rate on sealing rubber rings is three times that of inland areas. The standard inspection cycle in the textbook—out here, you cut it in half."

Zhao Rui's face flushed. Before coming to the unit, his impression of aircraft maintenance had still been confined to the flowcharts in textbooks and the theoretical explanations from the classroom. But an inconspicuous wear mark under his fingertips shattered his fixed assumptions—the textbook contains no desert sand and dust, no temperature swings of nearly 30 degrees Celsius between day and night, and certainly no combat alert that could sound at any moment. The real combat post is never found in ideal operating conditions.

In the days that followed, Zhao Rui experienced firsthand the tempo of a frontline unit.

One Saturday afternoon, an alarm suddenly rang out across the barracks. Officers and soldiers who had been napping rose by reflex, grabbed their gear, and rushed to the flight line. From the moment the alarm sounded to the moment the first support team was in position, only a few short minutes had passed. Watching the figures moving swiftly through clouds of swirling dust, Zhao Rui grasped the full weight of the four characters "sleeping on one's spear, awaiting dawn" (枕戈待旦).

Night comes late to the desert, and it is extraordinarily still. The runway lights came on one by one, and night-flight training commenced on schedule. Zhao Rui stood beside the apron, watching fighter jets pierce the night sky trailing flame, the roar of their engines sending a faint vibration through his chest. Since beginning his on-post internship, he had developed the habit of looking up at the sky—not to see how bright the desert's stars were, but to see how steady the warbirds' flight paths were. He understood that behind every streak of light cutting smoothly through the night sky lay the maintenance soldiers' repeated inspections and repeated verifications.

What truly engraved the mission into Zhao Rui's heart were the two "arithmetic problems" Sergeant Yang wrote on the board during a regulations study session. That day, Zhao Rui had independently completed a single-item inspection for the first time and was feeling pleased with himself when the sergeant turned and wrote two lines on the blackboard.

The first was an addition problem: adding from 1 all the way to 100, the answer is 5,050. "This simple mathematical formula is just like the process of aircraft maintenance and safety support. Every time you feel along a pipeline, every time you verify a parameter, every time you eliminate a hidden hazard, it is a positive accumulation of the safety coefficient. Only when a thousand and one rigorous operations are stacked together can they bear the weight of the warbird's every takeoff."

The second was a zeroing problem: any number multiplied by 0 equals 0. "Zero errors is not a high standard—it is a hard bottom line. Out of a hundred inspection items, get one wrong, and the result is 0. Hundreds of safe support missions, and one slip-up, and all the effort before it is wasted. This is the professional bottom line of maintenance soldiers, and the bottom line of responsibility to comrades' lives and to combat effectiveness."

The two equations on the blackboard took root and were deeply imprinted in Zhao Rui's mind. He thought of the rubber ring wear he had missed on his first day, of the flashlight worn white from use in the sergeant's toolbox, of the work manual filled with data. Those actions he had once thought "repetitive and trivial" turned out to all be part of working that addition problem; those standards that admitted not a shred of ambiguity were all about holding that bottom line.

From that point on, Zhao Rui's internship diary gained a fixed section: "Daily Review." He recorded knowledge points beyond the textbook and his own operational lapses one by one, reinforcing them in his memory before sleep. The next time he walked out onto the flight line, his steps were steadier, his observations more careful; he was unwilling to let any gap go unexamined.

As the internship neared its end, Zhao Rui solemnly signed his name on the volunteer form. As the pen touched the paper, he spoke no grand words—he only thought of the desert poplars (胡杨) found everywhere on the Gobi: the fiercer the wind and sand, the deeper the roots grow.

He looked up toward the end of the runway, where a fighter jet was taxiing and lifting off, the enormous roar of its engines filling his ears. Zhao Rui's heart was far more settled than when he had arrived. He would master his professional knowledge more thoroughly; when he one day takes his combat post, he will absolutely hold that zero-error bottom line, put every inspection and every verification into that addition problem, and bear the warbird steadily aloft and safely home.

由于油气密度大于空气，低洼积油区是爆燃风险最高的点位。各作业组沿拦油堤由低至高分层巡检。同时，濒海作业还要避开海风搅起的油气涡流，这是学员们实训时相较课堂上多出的变量。 “一级报警！”探测仪数值跳至爆炸下限阈值。罗怡打出红色警戒手势，同步向指挥组上报浓度数据，划定一级禁入隔离区。 警戒划定，处置同步展开。方班长一个箭步上前，关停储油罐紧急切断阀。罗怡协助战友甩开消防水带，拧开对应油罐的泡沫管路阀门。 海雾混着高温油气扑在面罩上，视野只剩眼前的枪头与燃着明火的油面。处置完毕安全撤离时，看着防护服袖口沾满的白色泡沫残渣，罗怡忽然明白，军校的每堂课都和战位紧密相连，自己记下的每一组参数，做过的每一次操作，最终都要落到守护安全的实处。 返程路上，海雾渐散。库区围墙外，暖黄色的灯光顺着海岸线铺展开来，一直延伸到远处的渔港。望着远方连片的烟火人家，罗怡的思绪又飘回两年前在新兵连的那个夜晚。 同样是毫无预兆的紧急集合。罗怡匆忙打好背包、穿戴装具，跟着队伍冲到营区外围的铁栅栏旁。冰冷的栏杆笔直地竖在面前，一边是军营里急促的口令，一边是路边散步的居民、追跑打闹的孩子。 两种截然不同的生活在眼前相撞。带队的张班长看出了罗怡的走神。队伍休整时，他来到罗怡身边，用指尖轻敲栅栏，望着远处说道：“你看这栏杆两边，一边是练兵备战，一边是和平生活。部队里岗位千千万，站的地方不一样，干的活不一样，但本质上都是一回事——护住外面这些安稳的灯火。” “不用总想着站在最显眼的地方，只要能守住老百姓的好日子，就都是合格的兵。”那时罗怡没能完全领会班长的话，只是默默记在了心里。直到此刻，他站在油库的围墙内，身边依然是一名班长在讲解，但他不再是当年打背包都手忙脚乱的新兵。 “我们守好每一道阀门，前面的战友执行任务才有底气，身后的千家万户才能平安幸福。”罗怡紧握着检测仪，内心坚定。一同实习的学员们也都褪去了刚入校时的懵懂，经过几年院校打磨和部队淬炼，迎来脱胎换骨的成长。从服从命令到主动担当，他们的能力一步步增强，肩上的责任一层层加重，可那份关于“守护”的初心，从来没有改变。 读懂战位上的“算术题” ■杨正中 何格格 空军工程大学学员在机务骨干带教下展开实操训练。吴泽林 摄 正午，戈壁上烈日当头。空军工程大学学员赵睿蹲在战机机翼下，指尖刚碰上机身蒙皮，就被灼人的温度烫得缩了回来。今年暑假，他主动申请到大漠深处的某基地参加岗位实习。原以为凭借扎实的理论知识，上手保障工作不会太难，但没想到上岗第一天就碰了壁。 起飞前例行检查，赵睿对照操作流程逐项展开，确认接口连接牢固、参数显示正常，便起身示意完成。班长杨博却没点头，指向一处管路接头：“再摸一遍，戈壁风沙大，密封胶圈磨损速度是内陆的3倍，课本里的标准周期，在这儿得减半。” 赵睿脸上一热。来部队之前，他对机务的印象还停留在教材里的流程图和课堂上的原理讲解。可指尖下一道不起眼的磨痕，打破了他的固有认知——书本里没有戈壁的沙尘，没有昼夜近30摄氏度的温差，更没有随时可能拉响的战斗警报。真正的战位，从来不在理想的工况里。 接下来的日子，赵睿切身体会到了一线部队的节奏。 一个周六午后，警铃突然响彻营区，正在午休的官兵条件反射般起身，拎起装具就往外场冲。从铃声响起到第一支保障队伍就位，不过短短几分钟。望着漫天扬尘里疾行的身影，赵睿读懂了“枕戈待旦”4个字的分量。 戈壁的夜来得晚，也格外静。机场跑道的灯光次第亮起，夜航训练准时展开。赵睿站在停机坪旁，看着战机拖着尾焰刺破夜幕，引擎的轰鸣声震得胸腔微微发麻。跟岗实习以来，他养成了抬头看天的习惯——不是看戈壁的星河有多亮，而是看战鹰的航迹有多稳。他明白，每一道平稳划破夜空的光亮背后，都是机务兵们一遍又一遍的排查、一次又一次的复核。 真正让赵睿把使命刻进心底的，是杨班长在法规学习课上写下的两道“算术题”。那天，他第一次独立完成单项检查，内心正高兴时，班长转身在黑板上写下两行字。 一道是累加题：从1一直加到100，答案是5050。“这个简单的数学算式，恰如机务安全保障的过程。每一次摸查管路、每一遍核对参数、每一处清除隐患，都是安全系数的正向叠加。千百次严谨操作叠在一起，才能托得起战鹰的每一次升空。” 一道是归零题：任何数乘0，结果都是0。“零差错不是高标准，是硬底线。上百个检查项，错了一个，结果就是0；几百次保障都安全，出一次纰漏，前面所有努力都白费。这是机务兵的职业底线，也是对战友生命、对战斗力负责的底线。” 黑板上的两道算式，如同生了根，深深印刻进赵睿的脑海。他想起自己第一天漏查的胶圈磨损，想起班长工具箱里磨得发白的手电、记满数据的工作手册。那些他曾以为“重复、琐碎”的动作，原来都是在做那道累加题；那些容不得半分含糊的标准，都是在守着那道底线。 从那以后，赵睿的实习日记里多了一个固定栏目：“每日复盘”。他把课本以外的知识点和自己的操作疏漏一一记下，睡前进行加深记忆。再走上外场时，他的脚步更稳了，观察更细了，不肯放过任何一处缝隙。 实习临近结束，赵睿郑重地在志愿书上签下名字。落笔时他没讲什么豪言壮语，只想起戈壁滩上随处可见的胡杨——风沙刮得越猛，根系扎得越深。 抬眼望向跑道尽头，一架战机正滑行升空，耳边是巨大的引擎轰鸣声。赵睿的心里相比来时踏实许多，他要把专业知识学得再扎实些，将来走上战位，一定守好那道零差错的底线，把每一次检查、每一遍复核都做进那道累加题里，托着战鹰稳稳升空，平安返航。 版式设计：许 硕 照片提供：刘 晶
