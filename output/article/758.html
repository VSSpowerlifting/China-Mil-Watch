About this analysis
Summary
A technical detachment of the Northern Theater Command Navy, garrisoned at a cave ammunition depot deep in the mountains, holds a quarterly 'Pine Needle Badge' (松针徽章) ceremony to commend personnel for daily work and mission performance, using resin-sealed needles from two pine trees planted by the unit's founding generation in the 1950s. The article documents how the detachment's political instructor institutionalized the badge as a retention and morale tool after observing that senior NCOs like Master Sergeant First Class Liu Huibo — thirty years on the mountain, one third-class merit citation — were voluntarily deferring personal honors to younger soldiers. The framing points to a persistent political work problem at remote logistics and technical support postings: sustaining motivation among personnel who have no direct visibility of combat outcomes and whose contributions are structurally difficult to recognize through conventional commendation channels.

Two Evergreen Trees, A Group of Mountain Sentinels

■ Wang Yuanbin, People's Navy News reporter Shi Shuang, PLA Daily special correspondent Fu Kang

The mountains stretch on without end, wind threading through the gaps in the rock. The jagged stone cuts off all clamor. Nestled in this silence is the garrison of a technical detachment of the Northern Theater Command Navy. The officers and soldiers stand watch beside a cave depot deep in the mountains, charged with ensuring the ammunition supply needed by naval vessels.

On this day, a special commendation ceremony was being held in the garrison.

As the detachment's political instructor, Chen, read out name after name, the commended personnel jogged forward one by one and received with both hands a special handcrafted badge — a badge inlaid with sprigs of vivid green pine needles, with the characters for "take root" (扎根) engraved on the back.

"In the 1950s, the unit's first generation of officers and soldiers built this garrison on a barren mountain and planted two pine trees with their own hands, naming them the 'Unity Tree' (团结树) and the 'Cohesion Tree' (向心树). These pine needles were gathered from those two evergreens." Political Instructor Chen told reporters that, in order to carry forward the spirit of the older generation of officers and soldiers — taking root in the deep mountains and persevering without yielding — the detachment holds a "Pine Needle Badge" presentation ceremony every quarter to commend personnel who have distinguished themselves in daily work and major missions.

Staff Sergeant Second Class Zheng Yi was receiving a commendation for the first time. He accepted the "Pine Needle Badge" with solemnity and gripped it tightly in his palm. In that moment, his heart overflowed with the pride of having striven and struggled.

For these officers and soldiers in the mountains, the small "Pine Needle Badge" has long since become a profound emotional bond, one that keeps them rooted here without regret or complaint.

The Green Pines Bear Witness ——

"Guarding the mountain is guarding the heart; what we guard is this inheritance. As long as the roots remain, we will keep watch."

That year, Political Instructor Chen left a naval vessel unit and came to serve at a technical detachment of the Northern Theater Command Navy, deep in the mountains. The unfamiliar environment and unfamiliar personnel left him at a loss as to where to begin in order to lead the unit well.

One afternoon after lunch, he was walking alone through the garrison when he spotted Master Sergeant Second Class Wang Qingwei crouching before a large boulder engraved with the character for "home" (家), a brush dipped in red paint in hand, tracing over the characters stroke by stroke. This puzzled Political Instructor Chen: the unit had issued no such task — why was he doing this on his own initiative?

"These characters were left by our predecessors. I don't want to see them fade," Wang Qingwei answered. "While the characters remain, the roots remain." In that moment, Political Instructor Chen suddenly sensed that the officers and soldiers here possessed a particular kind of strength — not driven by orders, not by slogans, but rooted downward from the heart.

Not long after, the unit decided to hold a themed education activity titled "Carry Forward the Root Vein, Continue the Glory" (传承根脉 赓续荣光), and invited a group of veterans back to the garrison. On the day of the activity, Political Instructor Chen accompanied several veterans on a walk through the garrison. When they reached the two green pines, two of the veterans suddenly stopped in their tracks.

Then, the two white-haired veterans extended slightly trembling hands and gently plucked a few pine needles from the drooping branches, carefully wrapped them in handkerchiefs, and tucked them into their pockets.

One of the veterans was named Li Dehou — the very man who had planted those two trees with his own hands. In the 1950s, he had spent twelve years on this mountain. Now the trees remained green, but his hair had turned white. "Carrying pine needles from these two trees is like keeping my roots here," he said in a hoarse voice. "Wherever I go, my heart is at ease."

"It was far harder back then than it is now," another veteran reflected. "We lived in small shelters we built ourselves, drank water from mountain gullies, and the road beneath our feet was paved with rock we broke off the mountain one hammer blow at a time. Back then everyone was of one mind — no one ever thought of leaving. We just felt that this place needed us, and that what we were doing was meaningful."

The veterans' words were seared deep into Political Instructor Chen's heart. After the activity ended, he turned the matter over for a long time: the resilient, enduring green of those two pines was the embodiment of the spirit of hard struggle passed down through generation after generation of officers and soldiers. But how could that spirit be carried forward and transmitted? To that end, Political Instructor Chen sought out several senior noncommissioned officers to deliberate together.

Master Sergeant First Class Liu Huibo had spent thirty years in these mountains and understood their loneliness better than anyone: "This place can be so quiet it makes you uneasy. Over time, if you can't find your own sense of worth, people won't stay." Wang Qingwei added: "Preaching grand principles is useless. People need to touch it with their own hands and see it with their own eyes — to know that what they're doing here is meaningful and worthwhile."

Political Instructor Chen had a direction. That autumn, he led several backbone personnel to carefully select pine needles from the two trees, then sealed and polished them in clear resin to create the special "Pine Needle Badges." At a summary meeting, Political Instructor Chen personally presented these badges to several officers and soldiers who had distinguished themselves in a mission.

"At first I wasn't sure — would anyone take this badge seriously?" he told reporters.

At the presentation ceremony that day, Political Instructor Chen handed a badge to a senior noncommissioned officer who was normally a man of few words. What he didn't expect was that, after accepting the badge, the old soldier said something that made Political Instructor Chen's eyes sting: "Guarding the mountain is guarding the heart; what we guard is this inheritance. As long as the roots remain, we will keep watch."

In that moment, Political Instructor Chen knew: this "Pine Needle Badge" carried a special weight.

The following year, the technical detachment formally institutionalized the selection and presentation of the "Pine Needle Badge," using it to honor officers and soldiers who, like the green pines, take root downward and grow upward in their daily work and major missions.

New Needles Growing ——

"The confusion in my heart was driven away by something more solid, more burning."

Private Du Zexuan first laid eyes on the "Pine Needle Badge" at the moment he was most adrift.

Half a year earlier, Du Zexuan had boarded the troop transport vehicle full of anticipation, imagining a military life aboard a warship, battling wind and waves. What he did not expect was that, departing from the dock, the silhouette of the warship grew ever more distant. Outside the window, unbroken mountains swept past one after another, the surroundings growing ever more desolate.

As the vehicle drove into the garrison, the two green pines standing amid the mountains came into view — their trunks vigorous, their branches full and lush. But Du Zexuan had no mood to take them in. He was deeply disappointed by the environment, and in his heart he was already having second thoughts.

Not long after joining his squad, Du Zexuan encountered an around-the-clock emergency support exercise. The exercise required standing watch inside the cave depot for a week, which left him feeling apprehensive.

"Once the ammunition you've supported is loaded onto a warship and fired from the barrel, you'll understand why we stand watch here," Squad Leader Li Jiuzhou said earnestly to the new soldiers entering the depot for the first time.

Inside the deep-mountain cave depot, the air was damp and the smell of chemicals sharp. After a few days, Du Zexuan felt pain throughout his joints and found it difficult to breathe. Yet none of his fellow soldiers complained, and unwilling to be the one who gave in, he gritted his teeth and held on.

Not long after that mission, the unit organized a new-soldier education activity. Political Instructor Chen brought them to the two pine trees and recounted the arduous years of founding the unit: "In the 1950s, the older generation of officers and soldiers broke ground and built roads on this uninhabited mountain, and with the tenacity of green pines, built on this wilderness the coastal defense stronghold that stands today!"

Looking at the "Pine Needle Badge" in the political instructor's hand — the pine needles vivid and bright beneath the clear resin — Du Zexuan felt a spiritual resonance for the first time. He felt as though the pine needles had crossed time and space to connect him with predecessors he had never met. "The confusion in my heart was driven away by something more solid, more burning."

At the close of the education activity, Political Instructor Chen led the new soldiers in picking up brushes to together trace over the red characters on the boulder engraved with "home." Crop after crop of new soldiers who had traced those characters gradually came to understand the meaning of perseverance in the course of ordinary days, and slowly grasped the weight of that "Pine Needle Badge."

One night in the early hours, the unit suddenly received orders from higher command: provide emergency ammunition support for a certain vessel. When the mission came, the officers and soldiers moved immediately. Du Zexuan followed the transport vehicles to the destination and completed the hoisting at maximum speed. "Ammunition safely loaded — please verify and accept!" Hearing the veteran call out the handover command, Du Zexuan stood in the ranks and clenched his fists, his heart surging with emotion.

When the mission was successfully concluded, the unit organized a special ceremony to commend the officers and soldiers who had performed outstandingly. Amid warm applause from the entire assembly, Du Zexuan stepped forward and carefully received the "Pine Needle Badge" from Political Instructor Chen's hands. A sense of achievement unlike anything he had felt before welled up within him.

That evening, back in the barracks, Du Zexuan carefully put the precious badge away and wrote a line of verse in neat characters on the flyleaf of his notebook: "Heavy snow presses down on the green pine; the green pine stands straight and tall." It was his interpretation of the badge, and his demand and expectation of himself.

"Our post may seem to be at the edge of the battlefield, yet it is an indispensable link in the fight," Du Zexuan said. "I hope that I, too, can be like those two green pines — driving my roots into this land, and not letting down the glory this badge carries."

Keeping Watch in the Deep Mountains ——

"Roots intertwined, hearts gathered in one place — even the most desolate mountain can be warm as home."

For decades, the two green pines named "Unity Tree" and "Cohesion Tree" have taken deep root in the garrison. The badges made from their needles have carried the spirit of "unity" (团结) and "cohesion" (向心) to every officer and soldier.

"This is a place you don't want to leave once you've come. The longer you stay, the more you treat it as home," said Corporal Du Tianyu.

Du Tianyu still remembers that winter when he was still a private first class. In the dead of night, snow falling heavily, he was struck by acute intestinal obstruction and curled up on his bunk in agony. The deputy detachment commander noticed something was wrong during his bunk inspection, and without a word called two squad leaders, wrapped Du Tianyu in a military overcoat, and carried him down the mountain.

The snow on the mountain road was more than ten centimeters deep, and the wheels kept spinning out. At one steep slope the vehicle could not make it up, so the deputy detachment commander jumped out and took turns with the two squad leaders carrying Du Tianyu on their backs.

By the time Du Tianyu woke up in a hospital bed, it was past three in the morning. The deputy detachment commander and the squad leaders were all at his side. Seeing him open his eyes, the first thing they said was: "How do you feel? Does it still hurt?" Hearing those words, Du Tianyu's tears came all at once.

"In that moment I thought: I could stay here for the rest of my life," he said.

Staying was not easy. After the surgery, Du Tianyu lost more than twenty jin and was so weak he could not do a single pull-up. His comrades cheered him on every day, and his squad leader helped him work out a sound training plan. Day by day, Du Tianyu's body slowly recovered and his scores across all training subjects kept improving. The following autumn, he passed the noncommissioned officer selection examination with excellent marks across all subjects and received a commendation as an outstanding "Four Haves" (四有) soldier.

That day, beneath the two green pines, Political Instructor Chen placed a "Pine Needle Badge" in Du Tianyu's hands and said: "This badge is awarded to those who have made this place their home." Small as the "Pine Needle Badge" was, Du Tianyu felt its weight.

For Corporal Zhang Tao, the years in the deep mountains never felt lonely, because of the care of the comrades around him.

Last year, the unit received an urgent notice: a warship would set sail at dawn the next morning, and they had to complete ammunition loading that same night. That day happened to be Zhang Tao's birthday.

The mission ended deep in the night. His comrades improvised with what was on hand — self-heating hot pots, instant noodles, and packaged bread — to cobble together a "field deluxe set meal," and pressed this special birthday gift into Zhang Tao's hands just before midnight. In the moonlight, his comrades sang the birthday song for him together. The singing carried far, and lingered long in Zhang Tao's heart.

On the way back to the barracks, Zhang Tao looked at the two green pines standing silent and upright in the night, and suddenly understood the meaning of "Unity Tree" and "Cohesion Tree": "Roots intertwined, hearts gathered in one place — even the most desolate mountain can be warm as home."

That warmth from the collective, Zhang Tao quietly passed on to more of his comrades. Because he loves painting, he ordered a good deal of art supplies online. Every weekend, Zhang Tao spreads out his paper and uses his brush to record the scenes in his heart — he has painted warships cleaving through waves, sailing toward the deep blue; painted comrades standing side by side beneath the green pines, laughing and talking; and painted a collective oath-taking ceremony, every person's chest bearing a "Pine Needle Badge."

A "Promise" Made for One Badge

■ Staff Sergeant Second Class Ming Sen

A few days ago, a new soldier asked me: is it hard to earn a "Pine Needle Badge"? I answered without hesitation: hard.

I still remember arriving in the mountains a few years ago, unable to overcome the sense of letdown in my heart, unable to muster enthusiasm for anything I did. The first time I took part in a support exercise, my operations were riddled with errors and omissions, and I dragged down the entire specialty.

At the after-action review, when I heard that my specialty had come in last, I was too ashamed to lift my head. After the results were announced, the political instructor stood before the ranks and presented "Pine Needle Badges" to the individuals who had performed outstandingly. As the applause rang out, the badge gleamed in the sunlight, and my heart filled with longing.

That was the first time I saw what a "Pine Needle Badge" looked like. From that day on, I made a "promise" to myself: through my own effort, I would earn a badge as soon as I could.

I began putting in extra hours in the study room, memorizing specialized knowledge, filling notebook after notebook. At an exchange session not long after, I summoned the courage to speak from the heart: "Before, I dragged the specialty down. In the future I will redouble my efforts to catch up, and I hope that one day I can earn a badge."

The comrades below looked up at me with sincere encouragement. Many senior noncommissioned officers told me they had been through similar experiences and cheered me on. During that period, many comrades came to help me on their own initiative — my squad leader drew up a study and training plan for me, and other backbone personnel used their rest time to give me extra instruction, helping me grow quickly.

One night in the early hours, the unit received orders: a certain vessel was departing on short notice and required overnight ammunition support. The unit leadership, having seen my progress, entrusted me for the first time with responsibility for a key link in the ammunition technical inspection.

From the time the moon was bright and the stars sparse to the first light of dawn, my comrades and I checked the ammunition crate by crate, without even a sip of water. Swirling dust forced its way into our mouths and noses, and I felt a bitter taste rising in my throat. Inventory, out of depot, inspection, loading — I carefully confirmed every step to ensure nothing was left to chance.

That day, the mission was completed successfully, and our support efficiency was recognized by the vessel's officers and soldiers. At the subsequent commendation ceremony, the political instructor stood before the ranks and called out my name first. In that instant, I was overwhelmed with excitement. It was only when the comrade beside me gave me a nudge that I came to my senses, jogged forward quickly, and received the "Pine Needle Badge" amid the applause of the entire assembly.

Back in the barracks, I held the badge and studied it for a long time: it is not merely a few pine needles — it is the crystallization and witness of effort and sweat. This badge represents that I have fulfilled my "promise" to myself, and have truly driven my roots into this mountain.

Now that I have become a squad leader, I have made a "new promise" to myself: to strive to see every soldier under my command wear that "Pine Needle Badge."

Roots in the Mountain Rock, Hearts Pledged to the Sea

■ Wang Yuanbin

During the interview, I asked one question: in all your years of service, do you have any regrets? Master Sergeant Second Class Wang Qingwei's eyes reddened. He said his regret was that he had "never once seen with his own eyes the ammunition he supported fired from the barrel."

Walking into this garrison deep in the mountains, it is easy to be moved by a quality of purity in the atmosphere — the officers and soldiers here speak directly, smile openly, and their eyes are clear. This purity is what allows them to endure the lonely years far from the noise of the world, standing watch day after day over a damp and dim cave depot. Because they hold "I" lightly and "we" heavily.

Master Sergeant First Class Liu Huibo has kept watch here for thirty years, bringing up batch after batch of specialty backbone personnel. Yet when it comes to personal honors, this veteran has been awarded only a single third-class merit citation.

It is not that Liu Huibo is insufficiently outstanding — it is simply that every time commendations and awards are evaluated, he waves his hand: "Give it to the young ones. They need it more than I do." Some say he is foolish; he answers: "If I'd come here for the sake of citations, I never would have come to this mountain gully in the first place." In Liu Huibo's eyes, the growth of his comrades matters more than his own honors, and the progress of the team is worth more than personal gain.

From veteran Li Dehou carefully wrapping pine needles in a handkerchief, to Wang Qingwei voluntarily repainting the characters on the boulder, to Private Du Zexuan solemnly putting away his badge — I found that the emotions of these officers and soldiers are concrete and deep. They love the "Unity Tree" and the "Cohesion Tree," they love the boulder engraved with "home," and they love this home encircled by mountains. Their actions stem not from anyone's requirement, but from an emotional investment that comes from the heart.

In these officers and soldiers, I saw the most genuine and pure image of what a soldier is: regardless of where they are stationed, regardless of personal gain or loss, caring only about how much they have given to their comrades and what contribution they have made to combat effectiveness (战斗力). Generation after generation of officers and soldiers have driven their roots into the mountain rock and pledged their hearts to the sea. Their struggle and their dreams are written on their posts, written in the deep-blue wake of warships.

When the interview ended and I was leaving the garrison, I turned for one last look at those two vivid green pines. The mountain wind passed through, and the pine needles rustled softly. The officers and soldiers here are like the needles on those trees — seemingly small and ordinary, yet full of life's tenacity. Gathered together, they stand vigorous and evergreen, upright in wind and character.

两棵常青树 一群守山人 ■王垣镔 人民海军报记者 石 爽 解放军报特约记者 付康 群山连绵，风穿石隙。嶙峋的山石隔绝了喧嚣，北部战区海军某技术队营区正坐落于这片寂静之地。官兵们守在大山洞库旁，承担着保障舰艇所需弹药的职责。 这天，一场特殊的表彰仪式正在营区举行。 随着该技术队陈教导员念出一个个名字，接受表彰的官兵逐个跑步上前，双手接过一枚手工制作的特殊徽章——徽章镶嵌着一根根翠色的松针，背面印刻着“扎根”二字。 “20世纪50年代，部队第一代官兵在这座荒山上建起营区，亲手栽下两棵松树，并将其命名为‘团结树’和‘向心树’。这些松针，就采自这两棵常青树。”陈教导员告诉记者，为了传承老一辈官兵扎根深山、坚韧不拔的精神，队里每季度都会举行一次“松针徽章”颁发仪式，表彰在日常工作和重大任务中表现突出的官兵。 二级上士郑益是第一次获得表彰。他郑重接过那枚“松针徽章”，紧紧攥在掌心。这一刻，他心中满溢着奋斗的豪情。 对于这群大山里的官兵而言，小小的“松针徽章”早已化作一份深沉的情感认同，让他们无怨无悔地扎根在这里。 “团结树”下，教导员为战士颁发“松针徽章”。王兆光摄 巡查库房。苏腾龙摄 青松为证—— “守山守心，守的就是这份传承，只要根还在，就一直守下去” 那年，陈教导员离开舰艇单位，来到位于大山深处的北部战区海军某技术队任职。陌生的环境与官兵，让他一时不知该从哪里着手，才能把队伍带好。 一天午饭后，他独自在营区散步，却看到二级军士长王庆巍握着一支蘸满红漆的笔，正蹲在那块镌刻了“家”字的大石头前，一笔一画描红。这让陈教导员有些不解：队里并没有布置任务，为什么要主动描红？ “这些字是前辈们留下的，我不想看到它们褪色。”王庆巍回答，“字在，根就在。”那一刻，陈教导员忽然发觉，这里的官兵身上有股特别的力量——不靠命令、不喊口号，他们是发自内心地向下扎根。 不久后，队里决定举办一场“传承根脉 赓续荣光”的主题教育活动，邀请了一批老兵回到营区。活动当天，陈教导员陪着几位老兵在营区里散步。走到两棵青松下时，有两位老兵突然停住脚步。 随后，两位头发花白的老兵伸出微微颤抖的手，从低垂的枝杈上轻轻摘下几根松针，小心翼翼地用手帕包好，收进口袋。 其中一位老兵名叫李德厚，正是当年亲手栽下这两棵树的人。20世纪50年代，他在这座山里待了12年。如今，树犹青翠，发已染白。“带着这两棵树的松针，就好像我的根还在这里。走到哪儿，心都踏实。”他声音沙哑地说。 “当年比现在苦得多，住的是自己搭的小房，喝的是山沟里的水，脚下的路是我们一锤一锤敲碎山上的石头铺的。”另一位老兵感叹道，“那时候大家心齐，谁也没想过走。就是觉得，这地方需要我们，干的事很有意义。” 老兵的话语，深深烙进陈教导员心底。活动结束后，他琢磨了很久：这两棵青松的坚韧常青，正是一代代官兵艰苦奋斗精神的体现。然而，怎样才能把这种精神接续传承下去？为此，陈教导员找到几位老班长一同商议。 一级军士长刘辉波在大山里待了30年，最懂这里的孤寂：“这地方有时安静得让人发慌。时间一长，要是找不到自己的价值，人是留不住的。”王庆巍接着说：“光讲大道理没用，得让大家亲手摸到、亲眼看到，自己干的这些事是有意义有价值的。” 陈教导员心里有了谱。那年秋天，他带着几名骨干从两棵松树上精心挑选，摘下一根根松针，用透明树脂封存、打磨，制成特别的“松针徽章”。在一次总结会上，陈教导员亲手把这些徽章，颁发给几位在任务中表现突出的官兵。 “起初我心里也没底，大家会不会把这徽章当回事？”他对记者说。 那天的颁发仪式上，陈教导员把徽章递给了一位平时话不多的老班长。没想到，接过徽章后，老班长说了一句话，让他忍不住鼻尖发酸：“守山守心，守的就是这份传承，只要根还在，就一直守下去。” 那一刻，陈教导员知道：这枚“松针徽章”，有了一种特殊的分量。 第二年，该技术队正式将“松针徽章”的评选颁发固定下来，用以奖励日常工作和重大任务中如青松般向下扎根、向上生长的官兵。 新叶生长—— “心中的迷茫，被一种更坚实、更滚烫的东西驱散了” 列兵杜泽轩第一次见到“松针徽章”，是在他最迷茫的时候。 半年前，杜泽轩满心期待地登上接兵的汽车，畅想着登上战舰搏击风浪的军旅岁月。没想到，从码头出发，战舰的影子越来越远。车窗外，连绵不绝的大山不断从眼前掠过，环境变得愈发荒凉。 随着汽车驶入营区，矗立于山间的两棵青松也映入眼帘——树干苍劲，枝繁叶茂。但杜泽轩没有心情细细观赏，他对这里的环境十分失望，甚至在心里打起“退堂鼓”。 下连后不久，杜泽轩碰上了一场跨昼夜应急保障训练。这次训练，需要在洞库内驻守一周，这让他心中有些忐忑。 “等你保障的弹药装上战舰、发射出膛，你就会明白我们为什么要守在这里了。”对着第一次进洞库的新兵，班长李久洲语重心长地说。 深山洞库里，空气潮湿、化学气味刺鼻。待了几天，杜泽轩就感觉全身关节疼痛，呼吸不畅。然而，其余战友都没有叫苦，他心里不想服输，就这么咬牙坚持了下来。 那次任务后不久，队里组织了一场新兵教育活动。陈教导员将他们带到两棵松树下，讲述那段艰辛的创业岁月：“20世纪50年代，老一辈官兵在这座荒无人烟的深山里拓荒修路，凭着青松般的坚韧，在荒野之地建起了今天的海防堡垒！” 看着教导员手中的“松针徽章”，透明树脂下，松针青翠鲜亮，杜泽轩第一次产生一种精神上的共鸣。他感觉到，这松针仿佛穿越了时空，将自己与那些未曾谋面的前辈们连接在一起，“心中的迷茫，被一种更坚实、更滚烫的东西驱散了”。 教育活动最后，陈教导员带着新兵们拿起笔刷，共同为那块镌刻着“家”字的大石头描红。一茬茬描过红的新兵，在平淡的日子里逐渐读懂坚守的意义，也慢慢理解了那枚“松针徽章”的重量。 一天凌晨，队里突然接到上级指令：紧急为某舰进行弹药保障。任务来临，官兵们立刻行动起来。杜泽轩跟随转运车辆抵达目的地，以最快速度完成吊装。“弹药已安全装载，请验收！”听到老兵喊出交接口令，那一刻，他站在队伍中攥紧了双手，内心激动万分。 随着任务圆满结束，队里专门组织了一场仪式，表彰此次表现优异的官兵。在全场热烈的掌声中，杜泽轩走上前，小心翼翼地从陈教导员手中接过“松针徽章”，一种前所未有的成就感油然而生。 那天回到宿舍，杜泽轩将这枚宝贵的徽章悉心收藏好，在笔记本扉页工工整整写下一句诗：“大雪压青松，青松挺且直。”这是他对这枚徽章的解读，也是对自己的要求与期待。 “我们的岗位看似处于战场边缘，却是战斗中不可或缺的一环。”杜泽轩说，“我希望，自己也能像两棵青松一样，把根扎进这片土地，不辜负这枚徽章所承载的荣光。” 深山守望—— “根扎在一起，心聚在一处，再荒芜的深山，也可以温暖如家” 几十年来，被命名为“团结树”和“向心树”的两棵青松在营区里深深扎根。用它们松针制成的徽章，也将这份“团结”与“向心”的精神传递给每一名官兵。 “这是一个来了就不想离开的地方。待的时间越久，我们越把这里当家。”中士杜天宇说。 杜天宇还记得那年冬天，他还是一名上等兵。大雪纷飞的深夜，他突发急性肠梗阻，疼得在床上蜷成一团。副队长查铺时发现他不对劲，二话不说叫上两个班长，用大衣裹住他往山下送。 山路上的积雪有十几厘米厚，车轮不停地打滑。行到一个陡坡，车上不去了，副队长就跳下车，和两个班长轮流背着杜天宇往前走。 等杜天宇在病床上醒来，已是凌晨3点多。副队长和班长都守在他身边，看他睁开眼，第一时间开口问道：“感觉怎么样？还疼不疼？”听到问话，杜天宇的眼泪一下子就下来了。 “那一刻我想，我可以一辈子待在这里。”他说。 要留下来并不容易。手术后，杜天宇掉了20多斤，虚弱得连一个单杠都拉不起来。战友们每天给他加油打气，班长还帮他制订了科学的训练方案。一天天过去，杜天宇的身体慢慢恢复，各项训练成绩也不断提高。次年秋天，他以全科目优秀的成绩顺利通过军士选拔考核，并获得“四有”优秀士兵的表彰。 那天，在两棵青松下，陈教导员将一枚“松针徽章”交给杜天宇，对他说：“这枚徽章，奖给把这里当成家的人。”不大的“松针徽章”，却让杜天宇感觉沉甸甸的。 对中士张涛而言，深山里的岁月，是因为有了身边战友的关心，才让他从未感到孤寂。 去年，队里接到紧急通知：战舰将在次日清晨出航，他们必须当晚完成弹药装载。这一天，恰好是张涛的生日。 任务结束，已是深夜。战友们就地取材，用自热小火锅、泡面和速食面包拼凑出一份“野战豪华套餐”，赶在零点之前，将这份特别的生日礼物递到张涛手中。月光下，战友们集体为他唱起生日歌。歌声传得很远，也久久回荡在张涛心底。 返回宿舍的路上，张涛望着夜色中沉默挺立的两棵青松，忽然读懂了“团结树”与“向心树”的涵义：“根扎在一起，心聚在一处，再荒芜的深山，也可以温暖如家。” 这份来自集体的温暖，被张涛默默传递给更多战友。因为喜欢绘画，他从网上购买了不少绘画材料。每到周末，张涛都会展开画纸，用画笔将心中的一幕幕场景记录下来——他画过战舰劈开波浪，驶向深蓝；画过战友们在青松下并肩说笑的身影；也画过一场集体宣誓仪式，每个人胸前都别着一枚“松针徽章”。 一枚徽章的“约定” ■二级上士 明 森 前几天，有新兵问我，获得一枚“松针徽章”难吗？我毫不犹豫地回答：难！ 还记得几年前刚到大山里，我难以克服心中的落差感，干什么都提不起劲。第一次参加保障演练，我的操作错漏频出，拖了整个专业的后腿。 复盘总结会上，听到所在专业成绩垫底，我羞愧得抬不起头。成绩公布完，教导员站在队列前，为表现优异的个人颁发“松针徽章”。掌声响起，那枚徽章在阳光下闪闪发亮，让我心中充满渴望。 这是我第一次见到“松针徽章”的模样。从那天起，我和自己做了一个“约定”：凭借自己的努力，早日赢得一枚徽章。 我开始在学习室加班加点背记专业知识，笔记本上记得满满当当。不久后的一场交流会，我鼓起勇气讲出心里话：“之前我拖了专业后腿，未来我会加倍努力赶上来，希望有一天能获得一枚徽章。” 台下的战友投来了真挚鼓励的目光。不少老班长告诉我，他们也有过相似的经历，为我加油打气。那段时间，许多战友主动来帮助我——班长为我制订了学习训练计划，其他骨干利用休息时间给我“开小灶”，让我快速成长起来。 一天凌晨，单位接到命令：某舰艇临时出航，需要连夜完成弹药保障。队领导看到我的进步，第一次让我负责弹药技术检查的关键环节。 从月明星稀到晨光初露，我和战友们一箱接一箱地检查弹药，连一口水都顾不上喝。飞扬的烟尘钻进口鼻，我感觉嗓子眼往外冒着苦味。清点、出库、检查、装载……我仔细确认每个环节，确保万无一失。 那天，任务顺利完成，我们的保障效率受到舰艇官兵认可。后来的表彰仪式上，教导员站在队列前，第一个念出了我的名字。那一瞬间，我激动不已。直到身旁的战友推我，我才反应过来，快步跑上前，在全场的掌声中接过那枚“松针徽章”。 回到宿舍，我拿着徽章端详了很久：它不只是几根松针，更是努力和汗水的结晶与见证。这枚徽章代表着，我完成了和自己的“约定”，也真正把根扎进这座大山。 如今，已经成为班长的我，又和自己订下了一个“新约定”：努力让我带的兵都戴上那枚“松针徽章”。 根扎山岩 心寄大海 ■王垣镔 采访中，笔者问了一个问题：入伍这些年，有什么遗憾吗？二级军士长王庆巍眼眶泛红，说自己的遗憾是“从未亲眼见过保障的弹药出膛”。 走进这座深山中的营盘，很容易被一种纯粹的氛围感染——这里的官兵说话直接，笑容坦诚，眼神干净。这种纯粹，让他们忍受住远离喧嚣的寂寞岁月，日复一日守着潮湿阴暗的洞库。因为他们把“我”看得很轻，把“我们”看得很重。 一级军士长刘辉波在这里守了30年，带出一批又一批专业骨干。可论起个人荣誉，这位老兵仅荣立过一次三等功。 并非刘辉波不够优秀，只是每次评功评奖时，他总是摆摆手：“给年轻人吧，他们比我更需要。”有人说他傻，他却回答：“我要是为了立功，当年就不来这山沟沟了。”在刘辉波眼里，战友的成长比自己的荣誉更重要，团队的进步比个人的收获更有价值。 从老兵李德厚小心地用手帕包起松针，到王庆巍主动为山石描红，再到列兵杜泽轩将徽章郑重收藏，笔者发现，官兵们的情感是具体而深沉的——他们爱“团结树”和“向心树”，爱那块刻着“家”字的大石头，也爱这个被群山环抱的家。他们的举动不是出于谁的要求，而是发自内心的情感倾注。 从这群官兵身上，笔者看到了军人最真实纯粹的模样：不管身处何地，不计个人得失，只关心自己为战友付出了多少、为战斗力贡献了什么力量。一代代官兵把根扎进山岩，把心寄予大海，他们的奋斗与梦想，就写在战位上，写在战舰的深蓝航迹中。 采访结束离开营区时，笔者又回头看了眼那两棵青翠的松树。山风拂过，松叶簌簌作响。这里的官兵就像树上的松针，看似微小普通，却饱含着生命的坚韧，聚在一处，便能苍劲常青、风骨挺立。
