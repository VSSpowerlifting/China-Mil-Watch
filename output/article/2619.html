Following the Footsteps of Heroes in the Karakoram

在喀喇昆仑追随英雄的脚步
PLA Daily (解放军报) 27 July 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
A First Sergeant named Sun Tao, serving in a border defense regiment of the Xinjiang Military District, has spent more than a decade running political education sessions for new recruits at the Kangxiwa Martyrs' Cemetery in the Karakoram at 4,280 meters, using the graves of 83 border-defense soldiers killed since the 1960s—and, since 2020, the tomb of Galwan Valley casualty Chen Xiangrong—as the centerpiece of what the unit calls 寻根 ('searching for roots') instruction. The article documents the institutional mechanism by which Chen Xiangrong's death and his phrase '清澈的爱，只为中国' ('Pure love, only for China') are being converted into a repeatable recruitment and retention tool: a 2024 re-enlistee, People's Armed Police veteran Tan Zhenliang, is shown cycling through the same doubt-to-resolve arc that Sun Tao and Chen Xiangrong each experienced, with the cemetery visit and patrol route serving as the structured intervention. This is standard political work content; its value is as a record of how a Xinjiang Military District border regiment operationalizes martyr veneration to manage morale attrition in an extreme-altitude posting, not as evidence of a new policy line.
Translation

Standing in the Kangxiwa Martyrs' Cemetery, watching the new recruits of the "Xiang Rong Squad," Sun Tao—a First Sergeant in a border defense regiment of the Xinjiang Military District—recalled the first time he stood before the martyrs' monument. It was also a summer, the Karakoram still cold, icy winds carrying pellets of snow across the rows of tombstones and rustling against the wreaths. Before the monument, Sun Tao stood among the new recruits, head tilted back, gazing up at the 13 characters carved into the stone: "Martyrs Who Defended the Motherland's Borders Are Immortal."

At 4,280 meters above sea level, that is the vertical height of this martyrs' cemetery above the sea. But few new recruits arriving on the plateau for the first time can immediately grasp its "spiritual altitude" (精神高度).

Here, the diurnal temperature range exceeds 30°C, ultraviolet intensity is several times that of the plains, and oxygen content in the air is less than half that at lower elevations. Food sits right in front of you, yet your throat feels blocked—two bites and the nausea rises. Without physical endurance, you cannot withstand the wind and snow, cannot hold your post steady, cannot guard the nation's gateway.

The old company commander continued the time-honored method passed down among the border defense company's officers and soldiers: holding an "eating competition." One bowl of rice counted as passing, two bowls as good, three bowls as excellent, with one steamed bun counting as one bowl of rice.

Eating—an utterly ordinary act on the plains—became a contest of willpower in the Karakoram. That day, when Sun Tao finally set his empty bowl on the table, the old company commander patted him on the shoulder: "Sun Tao, you've cleared the first hurdle on the plateau!"

Later, the company organized its officers and soldiers to visit the Kangxiwa Martyrs' Cemetery to pay tribute to the fallen heroes. The road was rough, and Sun Tao was still slightly dizzy when he stepped off the vehicle.

Row upon row of neatly arranged tombstones spread along the contours of the mountain, standing in silence. Sun Tao stood on the hillside, raised his hand in salute at the command, and felt a heaviness settle quickly in his chest. In that moment, he turned over in his mind what the political instructor had said—"What stands higher than the altitude?"—that spiritual altitude (精神海拔) forged by generation after generation of border guards with their lives and loyalty, a monument of faith standing forever at the nation's foremost frontier.

Sun Tao walked that winding road from the garrison to the cemetery for more than ten years. For every new intake of recruits, the first lesson was always held here. Then, among one such intake, came a new recruit named Chen Xiangrong.

Already a squad leader, Sun Tao quickly noticed Chen Xiangrong—a young man whose eyes held both longing and uncertainty. This 18-year-old from Pingnan, Fujian, had turned down a posting with better conditions and insisted on going to the frontline in the border region. But when he actually arrived in the Karakoram, the extreme and harsh plateau environment showed him no mercy. Severe altitude sickness kept him awake all night; on patrol, he would be bent double gasping for breath after only a few hundred meters. Gazing out at the vast expanse of snow-capped mountains and Gobi, he once quietly asked Sun Tao: what is the point of standing guard here every single day?

Sun Tao did not lecture him at length. He understood clearly that empty moralizing would not untangle Chen Xiangrong's doubts—but the stories carved into this land had the power to reach straight into a person's heart.

That year's "searching for roots" (寻根) education class was again held at the Kangxiwa Martyrs' Cemetery. Standing before the monument, Sun Tao, just as the old company commander had done in his time, recounted the stories of the 83 border-defense heroes buried here since the 1960s, through to the honorary title of "Karakoram Iron Sentinel Post" (喀喇昆仑钢铁哨卡) conferred by higher authorities on the regiment's Xianshenwang Border Defense Company. Sun Tao recalls that Chen Xiangrong stood at almost the same spot where he himself had once stood, head tilted back in the same way, listening with rapt attention.

On June 15, 2020, the wind in the Galwan Valley cut like a blade. Chen Xiangrong, serving as a shield-bearer, charged at the very front of the formation, did not retreat a single half-step, until he finally fell.

From that moment on, the battle cry Chen Xiangrong had written—"Pure love, only for China" (清澈的爱，只为中国)—spread across the length and breadth of the country, knocking on the hearts of countless young people.

Private First Class Tan Zhenliang was one of the young men whose blood was ignited by that story. The first time he heard of Chen Xiangrong's deeds, he was serving in a People's Armed Police unit, and a question formed in his mind: "As someone who enlisted the same year, why was he able to speak words so full of deep feeling?"

In 2024, Tan Zhenliang decided to enlist a second time. The leadership at the local people's armed forces department told him there was one slot on the southern Xinjiang plateau—the conditions were extremely harsh. He barely hesitated: "I'll go." He had a premonition that he would follow in Chen Xiangrong's footsteps and set foot on that heroic land.

Sure enough, he was assigned to the company where Chen Xiangrong had served. What came with it was severe oxygen deprivation, bone-chilling cold, and avalanches that arrived without warning. Staring at the desolation stretching to the horizon without a trace of green, this young man full of ardor began to have second thoughts, and felt more than anything a gnawing self-reproach: why couldn't he be as resolute and strong as his fellow soldiers?

Sun Tao saw his inner conflict at a glance—it was the spitting image of Xiangrong that year, and the spitting image of himself back then. He took Tan Zhenliang to two places.

—First stop: the Kangxiwa Martyrs' Cemetery. Sun Tao walked him past more than 100 tombstones, stopping finally before Chen Xiangrong's. Sun Tao said that Chen Xiangrong, in order to reduce his load and adapt to the oxygen-deficient environment, had forced his body weight down from 75 kilograms at enlistment to 55 kilograms.

—Second stop: Sun Tao took Tan Zhenliang out onto the patrol route. When they finally reached the deepest point of the river valley, before them rose towering, unbroken snow-capped mountains, and beneath their feet lay territory that must not be violated. Heaven and earth were silent, with only the sharp crack of the wind. They stopped, raised their right hands before a great boulder: "These magnificent mountains and rivers—not one inch shall be yielded!" Tan Zhenliang's voice echoed through the mountains.

That night, he wrote in his diary: "Pure love, forever young, forever undefiled." The following year, higher authorities organized a marksmanship competition; Tan Zhenliang won first place outright and was awarded an individual third-class merit citation.

Original Chinese
站在康西瓦烈士陵园，看着“祥榕班”的新兵，新疆军区某边防团一级上士孙涛想起自己第一次站在烈士纪念碑前的情景。 那也是一个夏天，喀喇昆仑依旧寒冷，寒风卷着雪粒抚过陵园的座座墓碑，拍在花圈上簌簌作响。纪念碑前，孙涛站在新兵队伍里，仰头望向碑上“保卫祖国边防的烈士永垂不朽”13个大字。 海拔4280米，是这座烈士陵园距离海平面的垂直高度。但很少有初上高原的新兵，能立刻读懂它的“精神高度”。 这里昼夜温差超过30℃，紫外线强度是平原的数倍，空气含氧量不足平原的一半。饭菜摆在眼前，喉咙却像被堵住一般，吃两口就反胃想吐。 没有体力就扛不住风雪、站不稳哨位、守不住国门。老连长沿用边防连官兵流传下来的老办法：举办“吃饭比赛”。一碗米饭为及格，两碗为良好，三碗才算优秀，一个馒头抵一碗米饭。 吃饭，这件在平原上稀松平常的事，到了喀喇昆仑却成了一场意志比拼。那天，当孙涛终于把空碗放在桌上，老连长拍拍他的肩：“孙涛，你过了高原第一关！” 后来，连队组织官兵来到康西瓦烈士陵园缅怀英烈。一路颠簸，下车时孙涛还有些晕眩。 一排排整齐的墓碑顺着山势铺展，静静矗立。孙涛站在山坡上，听着口令抬手敬礼，胸口很快沉了下来。那一刻，他思考着指导员所说的“比海拔更高的是什么”——那是一代代戍边人用生命和忠诚铸就的精神海拔，是永远屹立在祖国最前沿的信仰丰碑。 从营区到陵园的那一段蜿蜒公路，孙涛一走就是十几年。每一批新兵入营，第一堂课必定设在这里。再后来，这批队伍里来了一名叫陈祥榕的新兵。 已经是班长的孙涛，很快注意到了眼里带着憧憬和迷茫的陈祥榕。这个从福建屏南走来的18岁青年，放弃了条件更好的岗位，执意要到边疆一线。可真到了喀喇昆仑，极端恶劣的高原环境并没有对他网开一面。强烈的高原反应让他整夜睡不着觉，巡逻走不了几百米就喘得直不起腰。望着茫茫雪山戈壁，他也曾悄悄问孙涛：天天守在这儿，意义是什么？ 孙涛没讲太多大道理。他清楚，空洞的说教解不开陈祥榕的心结，不过刻在这片土地上的故事，却有直抵人心的力量。 那年的寻根教育课仍设在康西瓦烈士陵园。站在纪念碑前，孙涛像当年的老连长那样，从上世纪60年代被埋葬于此的83名戍边英雄的故事，讲到上级授予该团神仙湾边防连“喀喇昆仑钢铁哨卡”荣誉称号……孙涛回忆，陈祥榕和当年的自己站在差不多的位置，同样仰着头，听得入神。 2020年6月15日，加勒万河谷风如刀割。陈祥榕作为盾牌手冲在队伍最前排，半步没有后退，直至最后倒下…… 从此，陈祥榕写下的战斗口号——“清澈的爱，只为中国”传遍大江南北，叩响了无数年轻人的心门。 上等兵谭振良就是被这个故事点燃热血的年轻人之一。第一次听到陈祥榕的事迹时，他正在武警某部服役，心中好奇：“作为同年兵，为什么他能喊出如此饱含深情的话？” 2024年，谭振良决定二次入伍。人武部的领导告诉他，南疆高原有一个名额，环境非常艰苦。他几乎没有犹豫：“我去。”他有一种预感，自己将追随陈祥榕的足迹，踏上那片英雄的土地。 果然，他被分到了陈祥榕生前所在连队。随之而来的，是严重的缺氧、刺骨的严寒，雪崩更是说来就来。望着极目处无一点绿色的荒凉，这个满腔热血的年轻人有些后悔了，心里更多的是懊恼：自己怎么不像其他战友一样坚定、强大？ 孙涛一眼就看出了他的纠结——像极了那年的祥榕，也像极了当年的自己。他带着谭振良走了两个地方。 ——第一站是康西瓦烈士陵园。孙涛带他走过100多块墓碑，最后停在陈祥榕的墓碑前。孙涛说，陈祥榕为了减小负荷，适应缺氧环境，体重从入伍时的75公斤硬是减到了55公斤。 ——第二站，孙涛带谭振良踏上巡逻路。终于抵达河谷最深处时，眼前是巍峨连绵的雪山，脚下是不容侵犯的国土。天地寂静，唯有风声猎猎作响。他们停下脚步，在一块巨石前举起右手：“大好河山，寸土不让！”谭振良的声音在山间回荡。 那晚，他在日记中写道：“清澈的爱，永远年轻，永远纯粹。”第二年，上级组织射击比武，谭振良一举夺魁，荣立个人三等功。
