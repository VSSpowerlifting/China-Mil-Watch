The Stories Hidden in That Cowhide Backpack Are a Patriotic Heart and Love of Country Passed Down Through the Generations

那只牛皮背包藏着的往事，是代代相传的报国情爱国心
PLA Daily (解放军报) 11 July 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
A Tibetan PLA officer from Danba County, Sichuan, recounts four generations of military service beginning with his great-grandfather's enlistment in the Danba Tibetan Independent Division (丹巴藏民独立师) during the Long March and running through his own 2020 commission after graduating first in his class from Kunming University of Science and Technology's national defense student program. The piece is standard political work content published in official military media; its value is as a record of how the PLA frames ethnic minority loyalty and intergenerational military identity for junior officers, not as evidence of a policy shift or new recruitment initiative. The framing — centering the 1935 Long March encounter, the 1951 Tibetan People's Regiment (原藏民团), and the 2008 Wenchuan response — places Tibetan service within a continuous Party-loyalty narrative that the institution deploys to reinforce belonging among minority-background personnel.
Translation

Inheritance in a Tibetan Village ■ Qu Rong, Chen Lei (illustrator)

I was nine years old when my grandfather passed away. On the day of the burial, a fierce wind howled across the plateau. My father squatted before the grave and slowly poured highland barley wine into the earth. The wind swept past and scattered the scent of the wine in an instant.

Before he died, my grandfather had made a special point of leaving me a cowhide backpack. It was a pack issued in 1951 to the original Tibetan People's Regiment (原藏民团). The leather surface had been worn to a shine from years of use, and the damaged spots had been carefully mended. At nine, I did not yet understand the deeper meaning of a keepsake, but I never forgot the solemn look on my father's face when he received it.

From childhood I knew that my grandfather's left leg was different from other people's. His kneecap had been shattered by a bullet, and he walked with a limp. Every summer, grandfather would roll up his trouser leg and sit at the entrance of the watchtower (碉楼) spinning wool thread, and the sunken old scar on his knee would show clearly, like a piece of bluestone that had been chiseled away.

I once gently touched the scar and asked: "Does it hurt?" "Not anymore." "Did it hurt during the fighting?"

Grandfather set down what he was doing, was silent for a long while, then braced himself up and limped toward the hearth room (锅庄室, the central living area of a Tibetan village house). At the doorway he turned and said quietly: "Your second great-uncle hurt. He didn't come back."

I learned only later that the name of my grandfather's second younger brother had been carved onto a stone stele in a martyrs' cemetery. On every anniversary of second great-uncle's death, grandfather would take out the cowhide backpack, stroke it gently, and then put it carefully away.

Once, grandfather was busy at the entrance of the watchtower when his hands suddenly stopped. He gazed at the distant snow mountains in silence for a long while, then slowly began to speak: "Back then there were three of us brothers, plus two brothers-in-law — five of us enlisted together." He counted on his fingers: "Two became martyrs, two were disabled. Later the organization arranged official posts for us, but my third brother and I both declined. In our whole lives we never once asked anything of the organization."

That sense of awareness (觉悟) had its roots in 1935. In the deep autumn of that year, the Red Army passed through Danba on the Long March. The soldiers wore thin clothing and marched through frost and snow; at night they slept under the eaves of the watchtowers — they did not knock on doors, did not enter homes, did not take a single grain of food from the people. My great-grandfather, who at the time herded livestock for a tusi (土司, a hereditary chieftain), had seen the old armies plunder and oppress the people many times. When he first encountered this force, he was full of suspicion and wariness, and spent several days watching from inside the watchtower.

Later, a Red Army cadre whose face was covered in frostbite sores and whose hands were cracked and chapped found him and gripped his hands tightly. A tongsi (通司, interpreter or interpreter-guide) translated the cadre's words into Tibetan: "We have come to help you turn your fate around (翻身)."

"Turn your fate around" was a phrase my great-grandfather had never heard before. Gradually he came to understand it meant no longer being bullied, and living one's own life on one's own terms. What moved him was not only the words, but also those hands — hands that, like his own, bore all the marks of hard labor. After that, my great-grandfather set down his yak-herding whip, joined the Danba Tibetan Independent Division (丹巴藏民独立师), and followed the Red Army forward.

In the deep autumn of 1950, my great-grandfather had been bedridden for more than two months. Tibetan medicine simmering in a copper pot made a gurgling sound, and its bitter smell filled the entire room. His three sons kept quiet watch at his bedside.

My great-grandfather reached a hand out from beneath his chuba robe (氆氇袍子). Those hands, which had gripped a rifle for years, had thick knuckles and deformed joints; they trembled slightly as they hung in the air. His three sons rushed forward and took the old man's hands.

"Always… follow… the Party." My great-grandfather's voice came in broken fragments; he had to pause after each word. Then, with great difficulty, he forced out two more words: "Re… member."

The butter lamp flickered without ceasing. The three brothers knelt in silence and carved their father's instructions into their hearts.

My father is a grassroots cadre who spent years going down to villages and townships. When he came home, he would always sit silently by the fire pit drinking butter tea. Once, he spoke up — a rare thing: "Your father never served in the army, but like your grandfather and great-grandfather, I wanted the lives of ordinary people to get better and better. Your mother can't read, but she has kept firmly in her heart the names of every ancestor and martyr in our family. Don't forget your roots!"

When I was small, my mother bought me a set of children's clothing styled after a military uniform — a green jacket with red cloth collar tabs. I would pace back and forth in the small courtyard in front of the Tibetan village wearing it, feeling very proud.

In those days, my uncle would occasionally come back to the village on home leave, wearing a sharply pressed military uniform with a neat belt, his sleeves rolled up to his forearms to reveal dark, muscular arms. I always stood beside him and looked up at him. That was the image I admired most.

Later, my mother told me that my uncle had taken part in major non-war military operations (非战争军事行动) many times. In the 2008 Wenchuan earthquake, my uncle led a team into the disaster zone. During the rescue, his ulcerative colitis flared up repeatedly; he fainted from the pain three times, and each time he came to he said only one thing: "It's nothing. Keep going." After the mission ended, he too collapsed and was rushed to the hospital. At the time, my uncle stood 1.8 meters tall, but his weight had plummeted to 110 jin (55 kilograms). When he returned home after being discharged from the hospital, my mother looked at the uncle before her, now so gaunt he was barely recognizable, and quietly went back to her room to brew another pot of butter tea.

In 2020, I placed first in every individual subject and first overall in the national defense student graduation examination at Kunming University of Science and Technology, and was named a "Provincial Outstanding Graduate." Faced with multiple options, I was uncertain. I took leave on my own and returned to Danba.

Back home, I took the cowhide backpack out of the wooden chest, set it on my knees, and ran my hands over it again and again.

My father walked in from outside and sat down by the fire pit.

"Have you made up your mind?" he asked.

"One organization has offered me very good terms," I said.

My father glanced at me, then turned his gaze back to the fire pit: "When your great-grandfather followed the Red Army, he never asked about terms."

I jerked my head up: "That's different! He had no choice back then — but I do now!"

The firewood in the fire pit crackled and popped. My father did not get angry. He slowly picked up his butter tea, took a sip, and then said quietly: "You're right. You have a choice." He paused, then gently patted the cowhide backpack on my knees and said: "When you've made your choice, put this back."

I stared at that backpack. The stitches mending it looked like silent scars, one after another. I thought of the sunken hollow on my grandfather's left knee as he spun wool thread, and of my uncle's frail body after he had collapsed from illness…

"Tomorrow I'll go back to Kunming. To the army."

My father said nothing. He reached out and added a piece of firewood to the fire pit. The flames leapt up suddenly, and the whole room was lit bright.

Not long ago, after finishing a comprehensive exercise (综合演练), my combat uniform was soaked through with sweat. Back in the dormitory, I opened a parcel sent from home — inside was yak jerky that my mother had dried herself in the sun.

That evening, my father called. Through the receiver came the intermittent sound of plateau wind: "Did you get the yak jerky? Your mother dried it herself. She asked me to tell you — everyone at home is fine, don't worry." A few brief words of parting advice, and then he hung up quickly.

I took out a piece of yak jerky and chewed it slowly. That distinctive salty, savory flavor spread through my mouth. That taste carried the concern and hopes of my family, and gave me the strength to put down roots in the military camp.

Original Chinese
藏寨里的传承 ■曲 绒 陈 磊 绘 我9岁那年，祖父离世。下葬当日，高原狂风呼啸。阿爸蹲在墓前，缓缓将青稞酒洒进土里。大风掠过，转瞬吹散了酒气。 祖父去世前特意叮嘱，将一只牛皮背包留给我。那是1951年原藏民团配发的背包，皮面因常年使用已摩挲得发亮，破损处被仔细地缝补好。9岁的我尚且不懂遗物的深意，却始终记得阿爸接下背包时郑重的模样。 我幼时便知晓，祖父的左腿和常人不同。他的膝盖骨曾被子弹打碎，走路一瘸一拐的。每到夏天，祖父卷起裤腿坐在碉楼门口捻羊毛线，膝盖上那块凹陷的旧伤疤便清晰显露，像一块被凿损的青石。 我曾轻轻摸着伤疤问：“疼吗？”“现在不疼了。”“打仗的时候疼不疼？” 祖父停下手中的活计，沉默许久，然后撑起身，一瘸一拐地走向锅庄室（藏寨房屋生活核心区），临进门时回头低声说：“你二爷爷疼，他没能回来。” 后来我才知晓，祖父二弟的名字已镌刻在烈士陵园的石碑上。每逢二爷爷忌日，祖父都会取出那只牛皮背包轻轻抚摸，而后小心收好。 有一回，祖父坐在碉楼门口忙活，双手忽然停住，望着远处的雪山静默许久，缓缓开口：“当年我们弟兄3个，加上两个妻弟，5个人一起参军。”他伸出手指细数道：“两个烈士，两个伤残，后来组织给安排公职，我和三弟都没要，一辈子没跟组织开过一次口。” 这份觉悟，始于1935年。那年深秋，红军长征途经丹巴。战士们衣衫单薄，踏霜冒雪行军，夜晚露宿在碉楼屋檐下，不敲门、不进屋、不取百姓一粒粮食。彼时给土司放牧的曾祖父，见惯了旧军队劫掠欺压百姓。初见这支队伍，他满心惊疑，躲在碉楼里观望了数日。 后来一名满脸冻疮、双手皴裂的红军干部找到他，紧紧握住他的双手。通司（译员或译员兼向导）将干部的话翻译成藏语：“我们是来帮你们翻身的。” “翻身”是曾祖父从未听过的词。后来，他慢慢弄懂，意思就是不再受人欺负，自己的日子自己做主。打动他的不仅是话语，还有那双和自己一样，满是劳作痕迹的手。此后，曾祖父放下牦牛鞭，加入丹巴藏民独立师，追随红军前行。 1950年深秋，曾祖父已经卧床两个多月。铜壶里熬煮的藏药发出“咕嘟咕嘟”的声响，苦涩的味道溢满整间屋子。3个儿子静静守在榻前。 曾祖父从氆氇袍子里伸出手来。这双常年握枪的手，指节粗大、关节变形，悬在半空微微发颤。3个儿子扑上前，握住老人的手。 “永远……跟……党走。”曾祖父的声音断断续续，每说一字都要停顿一会儿。接着，他又艰难吐出两个字：“记……住。” 酥油灯火摇曳不息，三兄弟默默跪地，把父亲的嘱托刻入心间。 我的阿爸是基层干部，常年下乡驻村，回家时总沉默地坐在火塘边喝酥油茶。一次，他难得地开口：“阿爸这辈子没当过兵，但和你爷爷、太爷爷一样，想让老百姓的日子越来越好。你阿妈不识字，却把家里每一位先辈、英烈的名字都牢牢记在心里。别忘了自己的根！” 在我小的时候，阿妈给我买了一套军装样式的童装，绿色的衣服配着红布领章。我穿着它在藏寨前的小院里来回踱步，很是神气。 那时偶尔回乡探亲的舅舅，会穿着笔挺的军装、扎着规整的腰带，将袖子挽至小臂，露出黝黑结实的胳膊。我总站在他身边仰头凝望，那是我最向往的模样。 后来，阿妈告诉我，舅舅曾多次参与重大非战争军事行动。2008年汶川地震，舅舅带队挺进震区。救援途中，他的溃疡性结肠炎屡次发作，3次疼到晕厥，每次苏醒他都只说一句：“没事，继续干。”任务结束后，他也病倒了，被紧急送往医院。当时舅舅一米八的身高，体重骤降到110斤。出院归乡后，阿妈看着眼前已消瘦到脱相的舅舅，默默回屋多熬了一壶酥油茶。 2020年，我在昆明理工大学国防生毕业联考时取得各专项第一、综合第一的成绩，获评“省级优秀毕业生”。面对多种选择，我有些犹豫，独自请假返回丹巴。 回到家中，我取出木箱里的牛皮背包，放在膝头，一遍遍摩挲着。 阿爸从门外走进来，在火塘边坐下。 “想好了？”阿爸问。 “有单位给我开了很好的待遇。”我说。 阿爸看了我一眼，又把目光收回火塘：“你太爷爷跟着红军走的时候，没问过待遇。” 我猛地抬起头：“那不一样！他那时候没得选，可我现在有！” 火塘里的柴火噼啪炸了一声。阿爸没有发火，只是缓缓端起酥油茶，喝了一口，然后轻声说：“你说得对。你有得选。”他顿了顿，轻轻拍了拍我膝头的牛皮背包，说：“等你选好了，就把这个放回去吧。” 我盯着那个背包，上面缝补的针脚像一道道沉默的伤疤。我想起祖父捻羊毛线时左膝上那块凹陷，也想起舅舅病倒后瘦弱的身体…… “我明天回昆明，去部队。” 阿爸没有说话，伸手往火塘里加了一块柴。火苗猛地蹿高，映得整间屋子都亮了。 不久前，刚完成综合演练，我的作训服已被汗水浸透。回宿舍后，我拆开家里寄来的包裹，里面是阿妈亲手晾晒的牦牛肉干。 当晚，阿爸打来电话，听筒里裹挟着高原断断续续的风声：“牛肉干收到了？你阿妈自己晒的。她让我跟你说，家里都好，别担心。”简短的几句叮嘱后，便匆匆挂断。 我取出一块牦牛肉干细细咀嚼，那独有的咸香在嘴里漫开。这味道带着家人的牵挂与期望，带给我扎根军营的力量。
