Follow These 5 Unique Numbers to Understand Wang Ji's "Life in Numbers"

循着这5个独特的数字，读懂王戟的“数字人生”
PLA Daily (解放军报) 14 August 2026

About this analysis
Summary
Wang Ji, a software engineering academician at the National University of Defense Technology (国防科大) and lead expert on a national defense 973 project in high-assurance software (高可信软件), lost both legs to aortic dissection in January 2013 and subsequently developed uremia requiring dialysis three times weekly, yet continued to lead the six-year project to an acceptance review conclusion of internationally leading technical level and personally supervised 43 graduate students during his recovery. The article is a PLA Daily profile in the 'Model of the Era' (时代楷模) series — standard political work content — and its value is as a record of how the institution frames individual sacrifice and scholarly perseverance as military virtue, not as evidence of a capability development or organizational change. The framing of high-assurance software as a 'cold, hard, and slow' foundational discipline that requires generational continuity points to an institutional concern about sustaining a research pipeline in a field the PLA considers critical to informatized warfare (信息化战争), though the article does not specify current project scope or unit affiliation beyond the university.

Wang Ji's "Life in Numbers"

■ PLA Daily Reporter Chu Yanan

Recently, the deeds of "Model of the Era" (时代楷模) Wang Ji have been widely celebrated after media coverage. Fate has struck him blow after heavy blow, testing Wang Ji again and again. The hardships that did not knock him down have all become steps in his life, lifting up an ever more upright soul. Looking back on this road strewn with hardship, a set of numbers inscribes the ordeals he has endured and opens a window through which we can understand him. In this issue, let us follow these 5 unique numbers to understand Wang Ji.

—The Editors

**A Life-and-Death Struggle in the ICU**

**147 days** — for an ordinary person, nearly five months of passing time; for Wang Ji, more than 210,000 minutes of thick, viscous stagnation.

In the intensive care unit, he was jolted awake by a smothering pain. A dull, heavy ache seeped out from between his bones, washing over his entire body wave by wave. The monitor beside him beeped on, day and night.

The first abnormality he noticed was that his body felt lighter — the outline beneath the blanket was missing a piece. He reached his hand under the covers and slowly felt downward; where his thighs should have been, he found only gauze and bedsheets.

On January 23, 2013, Wang Ji suffered a sudden aortic dissection. From his chest to the base of his legs, the walls of his blood vessels were torn open. Surgery followed upon surgery:

January 24: aortic replacement.

January 25: decompressive fasciotomy of the left lower leg.

February 1: bilateral lower-limb amputation.

March 11: re-amputation of the right lower limb.

April 10, 17, 26: debridement, debridement, debridement…

By the end, no one could keep count. All anyone knew was one cruel fact: the soldier lying before them would never be able to stand again.

For nearly half his life, Wang Ji had always been on his feet as he pressed forward in scientific research. He stood in formation, stood at the lectern, stood at the front lines of research.

But now he sank into the white bedding of a hospital bed, a body with no point of support. He instinctively tried to bring his heels together, but he could no longer hold the crisp military posture.

The IV needles in his hands were changed again and again, puncture hole upon puncture hole.

He had always been a man who chased time; now time had become the heaviest shackle.

The monitor's beeping struck his eardrums one beat at a time, like a clock with no end. He tried counting the sounds to fall asleep, but when he opened his eyes, only a few minutes had passed.

Wang Ji's eyes suddenly reddened. He said nothing, only let out a soft sigh.

As the days of lucidity grew longer, he first asked for his students' theses to revise, then picked up medical books — asking about drug names, about indicators, about every detail of post-operative recovery. He had spent his whole life solving scientific problems; this time, he wanted to solve the problem of his own body.

Wang Ji still wanted to stand up.

He made a special trip to a hospital in Beijing to be fitted with prosthetics, and gripped the parallel bars in the rehabilitation room, moving forward one step at a time. When he could actually walk a few steps, he had someone record a video and sent it to his family, friends, and doctors.

He said nothing special in those days, but everyone could feel it — Wang Ji's heart had brightened. For a time, the hope of seeing Wang Ji stand again became everyone's wish.

But the prosthetics were too heavy. After a few steps, beads of sweat would seep through Wang Ji's shirt at the waist. A single lap of less than 300 meters took him a full hour to walk, and he walked at least three laps every day. The medical staff said: "We've never seen a patient push himself this hard."

In the end, Wang Ji took off the prosthetics and told those around him: "The same amount of effort, spent on research, can accomplish more."

Having truly tried to stand up, he could sit down in peace. This was not a light-hearted "reconciliation with fate" — it was a man who had charged forward for half a lifetime, precisely calculating his own life equation: spend limited energy where it is most worth holding the line.

**A Soldier's Battle Station**

How large is **0.2 square meters**?

Three A4 sheets of paper laid side by side — nearly enough to cover the seat cushion of Wang Ji's wheelchair. At the center of the cushion is a sunken depression that can no longer spring back.

Wang Ji's elbows, pressed year after year against the edges of the wheelchair armrests, have cracked and curled the black leather, wearing a pale groove half a finger's width wide — like an invisible boundary line.

Within that line is his battle station (战位). To reach the theses and books beside him, he must lean forward and extend his arm, calculating the angle and distance.

Beyond that line is the laboratory he once knew so well. He used to move with swift strides, bounding up steps in two or three paces; even after pulling several all-nighters in a row, his step was still light. He had always harbored a driving ambition: to build China's own high-assurance software (高可信软件).

What is "high-assurance"?

"Reliable" (可靠) is not enough — it must be "trustworthy" (可信), and more precisely, "highly trustworthy" (高可信).

Software, like a person, can fall "ill." A single erroneous instruction can cause a warship to lose power in heavy seas; a single system defect can cause space tracking and control to lose its target at a critical moment. In informatized warfare (信息化战争), a software failure is a catastrophe. Wang Ji is the "software physician" of this field.

While the outside world chased waves of computing power and hardware iteration, he remained, decade after decade, on the "niche and hardcore" (冷门硬核) track of high-assurance software technology. The "diagnostic instruments" in his hands are a software toolset he forged at the cost of his full mental and physical resources. This toolset treats not only common ailments but also the tiny lesions buried deep within code.

At the end of the last century, research on high-assurance software technology in China was almost entirely a blank. It was Academician Chen Huowang, Wang Ji's doctoral supervisor, and Wang Ji himself who first proposed the concept. Wang Ji later opened the first related courses at the National University of Defense Technology (国防科大). He wrote the textbooks; he set the standards. In those days, his battlefield seemed to stretch without limit.

During an interview, he smiled, glanced down at himself, and told the reporter: "Here I am, trapped by a wheelchair."

Was he truly trapped?

After more than a year of rehabilitation, Wang Ji, smiling, wheeled himself back to campus. Not long after his return, he took the lead in applying for a certain national defense 973 project.

This is a strategic high ground in major basic research for national security — the equivalent of the nation's "scientific prospecting team," drilling into the theoretical bedrock of disciplines to lay the foundation and chart the path for the technological breakthroughs of the next generation of defense equipment.

At the time, across the entire military's software technology field, there was only one such project. Who led it would directly determine how deep and how solid that foundation could be.

When word spread that Wang Ji had taken on this responsibility, everyone worried on his behalf: a man who still had long scars across his chest and abdomen, who was still practicing how to sit steadily in a wheelchair — could he bear pressure of this magnitude? The lead expert (首席专家) not only had to control the technical direction but also coordinate across units, conduct frontline investigations, and attend countless review and evaluation meetings. Could he withstand that intensity?

Those who heard him present for the first time always let their gaze fall first on the wheelchair — the military uniform crisp, the trouser legs folded neatly on the seat cushion. But once he began dissecting the technical plan and pinpointing the path forward, within a few sentences all the doubts that had hovered over the wheelchair drifted away, carried off by his line of thinking.

During the six years of intensive project work, his schedule was fuller than it had been before he fell ill.

In the conference room he would sit for seven or eight hours at a stretch, a cup of water often placed in front of him — but from the opening to the close of the meeting, the rim of the cup was never touched. From the conference room to the restroom, the wheelchair had to make four turns and pass through two doors. He did not want anyone to stop what they were doing to accompany him on that trip.

Over those six years, he traveled on assignment more than 70 times. Review meetings, milestone evaluations, acceptance defenses — every occasion that required the lead expert's presence, he missed not one. One winter, he flew alone to Beijing for a meeting. Participants who did not know him watched him wheel himself in, their eyes sweeping to the empty trouser legs, and audibly caught their breath.

No one ever heard him complain. When team members urged him to rest, he would reply mildly: "I want to see if I've still got it."

In 2021, the project passed its acceptance review. The review conclusion, in black and white, read: overall technical level at the international forefront and domestically leading, with some indicators internationally leading.

That day, a smile spread across his face — the same quiet expression as always. The untouched water cup and the acceptance report pinned under the corner of the table rested in the warm sunlight, as if keeping a silent promise.

**Days Cut Apart by Dialysis**

In the final year of the intensive acceptance process for the national defense 973 project, a thin slip of a diagnostic report once again cast a shadow over Wang Ji's life — uremia.

Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday: dialysis three times a week.

Cold numbers forced their way into his tightly packed research rhythm, completely rewriting every ordering of his time.

In the past, so as not to trouble others or interrupt the pace of his research, he had gone entire meetings without touching a drop of water. Now, medical orders strictly limited his daily fluid intake to no more than 500 milliliters.

Drinking and eating had to be measured and calculated with precision. The most ordinary desires of ordinary people were, for him, laden with prohibition.

Without continuous, uninterrupted blocks of time for research and teaching, his entire schedule had to be arranged around dialysis sessions. After each treatment, exhaustion arrived on schedule; he could only steal time inch by inch in the gaps between physical and mental depletion.

On dialysis days, Wang Ji would expertly wheel himself to the bedside, use his upper body strength to support himself, steadily shift his body onto the bed, then lie quietly, pull the thin blanket over himself, and wait for treatment.

The scattered pale-purple bruises on his arms, and a brownish old scar nearly 10 centimeters long, are the marks left by years of repeated needle punctures. The instant the needle pierced his skin, his brow would furrow slightly, then smooth out. Pain had long since become routine.

The light from the ward ceiling fell in even, clean squares; outside the window, the Liuyang River flowed slowly, and Hongshan Bridge stood still above the water. The machine hummed softly; blood circulated through the tubing with a steady gurgle.

The hours of dialysis that others would regard as torment were, for Wang Ji, the most tranquil moments of solitude in his ordinary days. The unresolved bottlenecks in ongoing projects, the road ahead for his discipline, the cruxes in students' theses — all of these took shape and became clear in the steady rhythm of circulation.

Most of the time, he is a teacher, a problem-solver, a pathfinder. Yet when the dialysis machine's rhythmic beeping begins, the identity he works so hard to conceal surfaces naturally — he is a patient who must live with illness for the rest of his life.

Perhaps the outside world assumes he is gritting his teeth and enduring, suffering through it all. But for Wang Ji, the words "forcing through" (硬撑) have never existed.

Fate has compressed his radius of life again and again; the boundaries of his body have been layered over with numbers. Yet his loyalty to serving the nation, his original aspiration in scholarship, and his perseverance in inquiry have not contracted by a single fraction.

When clean blood flows back into his limbs, Wang Ji's world is once again filled with one research project after another, leaving those in the ward with the image of a figure always hurrying onward.

**The Whiteboard, Erased and Written Again**

On the coordinate system of Wang Ji's life, "43" is two overlapping marks.

One marks the years: this year is the 43rd year since he first walked through the gates of the National University of Defense Technology.

One marks the passing of the torch (薪火): after returning from grave illness, he personally supervised **43** master's and doctoral students.

When doctoral student Li Minglong first became Wang Ji's student, his most immediate impression of his supervisor was strictness. Wang Ji rarely used slides when guiding students; instead, he had them work through derivations on a whiteboard with a marker. Every time Li Minglong stood at the whiteboard, he was tense, because behind him his supervisor was watching every line of every formula, and the slightest lapse in logic would bring an immediate halt.

Liu Wanwei — once a student, now a colleague — spent more than 20 years at the whiteboard alongside Wang Ji. In 2005, just after beginning his doctoral studies, he summoned the courage to propose that he wanted to try improving a certain classical algorithm. Wang Ji was silent for a moment, then said: "This is not an easy road. If you make up your mind to do it, I'll walk it with you."

That "walking together" lasted four years. Countless late nights, under the fluorescent lights of the conference room, it was often just the two of them — teacher and student — working through the whiteboard. The most difficult step they pondered over and over, deriving it for eight or nine months without making the slightest headway. Liu Wanwei even thought about giving up. Wang Ji, unhurried, picked up a book and handed it to him: "Take a look at this. Maybe it will give you some inspiration."

Wang Ji said that this was how he himself had come through it, and that his own supervisor had guided him the same way.

While Wang Ji was hospitalized, his students took turns keeping watch outside the ward. Late one night, Liu Wanwei opened the care log and saw that the previous attendant had written, in neat, careful characters: "Professor Wang, a peaceful night tonight."

When this moment came up during the interview, a single tear caught in the crease at the corner of Liu Wanwei's eye, slow to fall. He wiped the corner of his eye with the back of his hand and said: "Back then, all we hoped for was that the professor would be safe."

All the students assumed that once he had crossed that threshold, their supervisor would slow down. But Wang Ji, returning to the whiteboard, only tightened his pace: he gathered students at the whiteboard for derivations more frequently than before; in the gaps between dialysis sessions, he would always clutch a corrected thesis and go over the details with students.

Thirteen years have passed. Liu Wanwei has become a faculty member himself, and has gradually come to understand the deepening urgency in his supervisor.

The road of high-assurance software is "cold, hard, and slow" (冷、难、慢); it must be walked by one generation passing the baton to the next. Wang Ji understands his own physical condition clearly. He only hopes that his students will grip the baton a little more tightly.

That urgency is a responsibility inscribed in the lineage of mentorship (师承). The whiteboard Wang Ji uses most often is the very one passed down to him from his supervisor, Academician Chen Huowang — in those years, the Galaxy (银河) team pressed forward through hardship with nothing but a whiteboard and a pen.

The whiteboard is erased and written again; class after class of students comes and goes; and that foundational spirit is passed on along with them.

The young people who once stood nervously at the whiteboard, marker in hand, have mostly become pillars of the field. Some have stayed at the university to teach, fighting shoulder to shoulder with Wang Ji; others have gone to the front lines, carrying this approach to scholarship to more distant places.

**One Family, Three Separate Paths**

Thirteen years have passed since Wang Ji's grave illness. Broken apart, this number also captures the shape of this family: one family, divided into three separate paths (兵分三路).

Over those 13 years, a family has traveled a long road around a single wheelchair.

The first to grip the wheelchair's handle was Wang Ji's mother, Liu Guizhi.

She has been a strong-willed woman her whole life. She was a university student in the 1960s and later became a technical backbone at a factory. In her later years, however, her days revolved around two wheelchairs: in 2008, her husband Wang Xin fell ill and became paralyzed, and the first wheelchair entered the home; five years later, her son Wang Ji came back from the edge of death, and a second wheelchair was placed alongside the first.

When her son went out on assignment, she would go downstairs with him, slowly pushing the wheelchair by its handle. The wheels rolled softly over the ground; she fixed her gaze on the hunched figure before her, and tears flowed without a sound.

Wang Ji did not turn around. He only paused for a moment and said gently: "Mom, don't be like this. I always have to face this. My life is full and rich — isn't that a good thing?"

She wiped her tears and kept pushing the wheelchair forward.

The wheels rolled over the concrete, rolled through a mother's twilight years, and rolled into a child's childhood.

In first grade, Wang Ji's daughter wrote an essay full of imaginative ideas: "A Wheelchair That Can Walk." Give Dad's wheelchair two feet; embed a magnet between the wheels and the seat; when it needs to climb stairs, the magnet clicks into place, the wheels become "feet," and Dad won't need to trouble anyone.

In fifth grade, Dad went to Nanjing for uremia treatment, and Mom went to Tianjin to tackle an important research project. With no one to look after her, she had to take a leave from school and became Mom's "little shadow," learning to hand over wrenches and stick on labels, making the exhausted adults laugh. No one supervised her homework; no one chased after her to help her catch up on lessons. She only watched the upright figure of her father in his wheelchair, learned from her mother's unflappable composure, and followed along forward in a hazy, half-understanding way. As a small child, when she saw her parents' medals, she always burned with the desire to surpass them. Now she only wants to grow up quickly, so she can grip the wheelchair handle steadily and push a little further in her mother's place.

For all 13 years, the one who has steadily pushed this wheelchair forward is Wang Ji's wife, Huang Chun.

She is wrapped in a quiet quality — it is hard to say whether it is the equanimity worn smooth by years, or the restraint of someone exhausted to the very limit. As she speaks, she unconsciously pinches the bridge of her nose, again and again at the same spot, until it turns red.

Huang Chun's days are a series of multiple-choice questions with no standard answer.

Send her husband to work first, or send her daughter to school first? Choose the former, and the child is late; choose the latter, and her husband misses a hot breakfast.

Take her daughter to Tianjin, or stay behind? A major project was at a critical stage — she was the chief designer and had to be there; but bringing the child along would disrupt the child's schooling.

Both sides pull at her; neither can be let go.

She always says: "Every position must have someone holding it." But what she holds is far more than one position.

Over those 13 years, not one of her identities has allowed her to simply "leave her post" (离岗). Every multiple-choice question had to be weighed and weighed again on both sides of the scale. She said only two things: "There was only this choice" and "Nothing can be perfect in every way" — her tone as light as a thin mist.

As she said these things, the hand pinching her nose did not stop; her fingertips pressed harder and harder, her knuckles quietly exerting force, the red mark spreading from the bridge of her nose to the corners of her eyes. Those words were so heavy, lodged in her throat, as if they would spill out the moment she opened her mouth: "I am truly so tired."

She is just an ordinary person too. There are moments when she wants to shed every identity and go back to being a woman who can lean on her husband's shoulder. Then she would not have to feign strength in the face of a critical-condition notice, would not have to weigh her loved one's treatment against her daughter's schooling again and again.

If she truly stopped to rest for a while, who could bear to reproach her even half a word? Who could bear to urge her forward even half a step?

But she did not stop. She only pinched the bridge of her nose, one press after another, using that sharp, physical pain to press back down — bit by bit — the surging emotions, the weakness that had risen to her lips.

Wang Ji understands all of it.

Precisely because he understands, he refuses to stop. He knows that every fraction more he can do for himself lightens his wife's burden by a fraction; every step he takes forward lowers by a measure the anxiety she carries.

When the pain in his residual limbs becomes unbearable, he will dial his wife's number. When she picks up, he says nothing — just a few seconds of silence. His wife understands everything; Wang Ji knows she understands everything.

After hanging up, Huang Chun looks out the window at the rain and says quietly: "Wang Ji's residual limbs must be hurting again."

王戟的“数字人生” ■解放军报记者 楚亚楠 日前，“时代楷模”王戟的事迹经媒体报道后广为传颂。命运一次次沉重叩击，不断考验着王戟。那些没有将他击倒的苦难，都化作生命的台阶，托举起一个更加挺拔的灵魂。回望这段布满艰辛的路，一组数字镌刻下他所经历的磨难，也打开了我们理解他的一扇窗口。本期，让我们循着这5个独特的数字，读懂王戟。 ——编 者 ICU里的生死拉锯 可此刻，他陷进病床的白色被褥里，成了一具找不到支点的躯壳，下意识想并拢脚跟，却再也摆不出笔挺的军姿。 手上的留置针换了又换，针孔叠着针孔。 从前他是追时间的人，如今时间成了最沉的枷锁。 监护仪的“嘀嘀”声一下一下，砸在耳膜上，像没有尽头的钟。他试过数着声响入睡，但睁开眼发现，仅仅过去了几分钟。 王戟的眼倏地红了。他没说什么，只是轻轻地叹了口气。 清醒的日子多了，他先拿来学生的论文修改，又翻起医书，问药名，问指标，问术后恢复的每一个细节。他这辈子都在破解科研难题，这一次，他想破解自己身体的难题。 王戟还想站起来。 他专程到北京的医院，给自己装了假肢，扶着康复室的双杠，一步一步挪动。真能走几步的时候，他特意让人拍视频，发给家人、朋友和医生。 那几天，他没说什么，但谁都能感觉到，王戟的心变亮堂了。一时间，能再次看到王戟站起来的样子，成为大家的心愿。 可假肢太重，走几步，王戟的腰上就渗出汗珠。不到300米的一圈路，他要走整整1个小时，每天至少走3圈。医护人员说：“从没见过这么拼的病人。” 最终，王戟把假肢脱了下来，对身边人说：“同样的力气，花在科研上，能做的事更多。” 真正努力站起来过，就能踏实坐下了。这不是轻飘飘的“与命运和解”，而是冲锋半生的人，精准算出了自己的生命方程——把有限的能量用在最值得坚守的地方。 一个军人的战位 0.2平方米有多大？ 3张A4纸并排铺开，几乎盖住王戟的轮椅坐垫。坐垫正中有一个凹陷的坑，再也弹不回去。 王戟的双肘，长年抵着轮椅扶手边缘，使黑色皮面皲裂翻卷，磨出一道半指宽的浅白印子，像一条看不见的边界线。 线以内，是他的战位。探身、伸胳膊，他要计算好角度、距离，才能够得到身边的论文和书本。 线以外，是他熟悉的实验室。曾经的他步履如风，三两步就能跨上台阶；连续熬几个夜，脚步照样轻快。他心里一直憋着一股劲：要做中国自己的高可信软件。 什么是高可信？ “可靠”不够，要“可信”，再确切一点，是“高可信”。 软件像人一样会“生病”。一行错误指令，能让舰船在风浪中失去动力；一个系统缺陷，足以让航天测控在关键时刻丢失目标。信息化战争中，软件一旦出错，后果就是灾难。王戟，就是这个领域里的“软件良医”。 当外界追逐算力的浪潮、硬件的迭代时，他几十年如一日，坚守在高可信软件技术这条“冷门硬核”赛道上。他手中的“诊疗器”，是他耗尽心血锻造的软件工具集。这套工具治的不仅是寻常病痛，还有深埋代码中的点点病灶。 上个世纪末，国内对高可信软件技术的研究几乎一片空白，是导师陈火旺院士和王戟率先提出这一概念。后来，王戟在国防科大首开相关课程。教材是他写的，标准是他定的。那时候，他的战场仿佛铺得无限远。 接受采访时，他笑着低头瞥了眼身下，对记者说：“我就这样，被轮椅困住了。” 真的被困住了吗？ 经过一年多的康复训练，王戟带着微笑，摇着轮椅回到了校园。刚回来没多久，他就牵头申请了某国防973项目。 这是国家安全重大基础研究的战略制高点，相当于国家的“科研勘探队”，专挖学科底层的理论富矿，为未来一代国防装备的技术突破打牢底座、探明路径。 当时，在全军软件技术领域，这样的项目仅此一项。选谁挂帅，直接决定了这块地基能打多深、多坚固。 王戟接下这副担子的消息传开后，大家都替他捏着一把汗：胸腹还留着长长的疤痕，连坐稳轮椅都还在练习的人，能扛得住这种级别的压力吗？要知道，首席专家不仅要把控技术方向，还要跨单位协调、到一线调研、参加数不清的论证评审。这强度，他能否顶得住？ 初次听他报告的人，目光总是先落在轮椅上——军装挺括，裤管平整地叠在坐垫上。可等他开口拆解技术方案、点破攻关路径，三两句间，那些悬在轮椅上的顾虑，都跟着他的思路飘走了。 项目攻坚的那6年，他的日程排得比生病前还满。 会议室里他一待就是七八个小时，面前经常放着一杯水，但从开场到散会，杯沿都没碰过一次。从会议室到卫生间，轮椅要转4个弯、过2扇门。他不想让任何人停下手里的活，陪他走这一趟。 6年里，他出差70余次。论证会、节点评审、验收答辩，凡首席专家必须到的场合，他一次没落下。一年冬天，他独自飞往北京开会。不熟悉他的参会者，看见他摇着轮椅进场，目光扫到空落落的裤管，当场倒吸一口凉气。 没人听他喊过苦。团队成员劝他歇歇，他只淡淡地回一句：“我想试试自己还行不行。” 2021年，项目顺利通过验收。评审结论白纸黑字写着：整体技术水平国际前列、国内领先，部分指标国际领先。 那一天，他的脸上浮现了笑容，依旧是往日那般安静的模样。那只全程没动过的水杯和桌角压着的验收报告，静置于暖阳中，仿佛守着一个沉默的约定。 被透析切割的日子 某国防973项目紧锣密鼓验收的最后一年，一张薄薄的诊断单，再次给王戟的人生蒙上一层阴霾——尿毒症。 周二、周四、周六，一周3次透析。 冰冷的数字，强行切入他紧凑的科研节奏，彻底改写了他所有的时间秩序。 曾经，为了不给人添麻烦、不中断科研进度，他全程滴水不沾。如今，医嘱严格限定，每日摄入水量不超过500毫升。 饮水、进食，他都要掐着刻度精准计算。普通人最寻常的口腹之欲，对他来说却充满禁忌。 没有完整连贯的科研、教学时段，他的全部日程都要围绕着透析时间安排。每次治疗结束，疲惫如期袭来，他只能在身心透支的缝隙中，一寸寸抢时间。 每到透析日，王戟熟练地将轮椅滑到病床边，靠上肢发力支撑，稳稳挪动身体，再静静躺卧、盖好薄被、等待治疗。 胳膊上错落的淡紫淤青、一道近10厘米的褐色陈旧瘢痕，是经年反复穿刺留下的印记。针头刺入皮肤的刹那，他眉头轻轻一蹙，随即舒展。疼痛，早已是日常。 病房天花板的灯光一格一格匀净落下，窗外浏阳河缓缓流淌，洪山桥静立水面。机器嗡鸣轻响，血液顺着管路汩汩循环。 旁人视作煎熬的透析时光，却是王戟平日里最沉静的独处时刻。那些悬而未决的项目堵点、学科发展的前路、学生论文的症结，在平稳的循环里梳理成型、脉络分明。 多数时候，他是师者、是攻坚者、是领路者。然而，当透析机规律的“嘀嘀”声响起时，那个被他极力掩盖的身份，就会自然浮现——他是一个需要终身与病痛共处的病人。 或许外界默认他在咬牙坚持、苦苦煎熬，可于王戟而言，从无“硬撑”二字。 命运一次次压缩他的生活半径，身体的边界被数字层层加码，可报国的忠心、治学的初心、求索的恒心，从未收缩分毫。 当洁净的血液重新回流四肢，王戟的世界再一次被一个个科研项目填满，给病房的人们留下匆匆赶路的身影。 白板擦了又写 在王戟的人生坐标系上，“43”是两道重合的刻度。 一重是岁月：今年是他踏入国防科大校园的第43个年头。 一重是薪火：重病归来后，他亲自带了43名硕博研究生。 博士生李明龙刚成为王戟的学生时，对导师最直观的感受就是严。王戟指导学生时很少用幻灯片，而是让学生在白板上用马克笔推导。李明龙每次站在白板前都很紧张，因为身后导师紧盯着每一行公式，逻辑稍有差池就会被立刻叫停。 昔日的学生、如今的同事刘万伟，跟着王戟在白板前走了20多年。2005年，他刚读博就鼓起勇气提出：想尝试改进某项经典算法。王戟沉默片刻说：“这条路不好走。如果你下决心做，我陪你一起。” 这一陪，就是4年。无数个深夜，会议室的白炽灯下，经常只剩师徒二人对着白板推敲。最艰难的一个步骤，他们反复琢磨，推导了八九个月，也没半点收获。刘万伟甚至想过放弃，王戟却不疾不徐，找来一本书递给刘万伟：“看看这个，也许会有启发。” 王戟说，自己就是这样走过来的，他的导师也这样辅导过他。 王戟住院时，他的学生们轮流在病房外守护。那天深夜，刘万伟翻开陪护记录本，看到前一个陪护的人在上面工工整整地写道：“王老师，今夜平安。” 采访时聊起这一幕，一滴泪卡在刘万伟眼角的褶皱里，迟迟没落下。他用手背抹了一下眼角，说：“那会儿，只盼老师平安。” 所有学生都默认，迈过这道坎，导师应该会慢下来。可重回白板前的王戟，节奏反而绷得更紧：召集学生在白板前推导的次数更频繁了；透析间隙，他也总攥着改好的论文跟学生核对细节。 13年过去，刘万伟已成为教员，也渐渐读懂了导师日益加重的急切。 高可信软件这条路“冷、难、慢”，要靠一代代人接棒走下去。王戟清楚自己的身体状况，只希望学生们能把接力棒攥得更紧一点。 这份急切，是刻在师承里的责任。王戟常用的白板，正是从导师陈火旺院士手中接过来的——当年银河团队在艰难中攻坚，也是靠着一块白板、一支笔。 白板擦了又写，一届届学生来了又走，这份底色也跟着传了下去。 当年攥着马克笔神情紧张的年轻人，如今大多成了领域里的中坚力量。有人留校执教，和王戟并肩作战；有人奔赴一线，把这套治学之道带去了更远的地方。 一个家，兵分三路 距离王戟那场重病，已过了13个年头。这个数字拆开看，也正是这个家的样子：一个家，兵分三路。 13年里，一家人围着同一台轮椅，走了很远的路。 最先握住轮椅扶手的，是王戟的母亲刘桂枝。 她一辈子要强。她是20世纪60年代的大学生，后来成为某工厂技术骨干。进入晚年，她的日子却围着两辆轮椅转：2008年老伴王心患病瘫痪，家里添了第一辆；5年后儿子王戟死里逃生，屋里并排摆上了第二辆。 儿子出差，她跟着下楼，扶着把手慢慢推。轮椅的轱辘轻轻碾着地面，她盯着面前那个蜷缩的身影，眼泪悄无声息地流。 王戟没有回头，只顿了顿，柔声说：“妈，别这样。我总要面对的。我过得很充实，这不是很好吗？” 她擦了泪，推着轮椅继续向前。 轮椅碾过水泥地，碾过一个母亲的晚年，也碾进一个孩子的童年。 读小学一年级时，王戟的女儿写了一篇充满奇思妙想的作文《能走的轮椅》：给爸爸的轮椅装两只脚，轮子和椅身之间嵌一块磁铁，要爬楼的时候，磁铁一吸，轮子换成“脚”，爸爸就不用麻烦了。 读小学五年级时，爸爸去南京治尿毒症，妈妈赴天津攻坚重要课题，没人照看的她只好休学，成了妈妈的“小跟班”，学会了递扳手和贴标签，逗笑了疲惫的大人。 没人盯她写作业，也没人追着给她补功课。她只是望着轮椅上爸爸笔直的身影，学着妈妈遇事不慌的样子，懵懵懂懂跟着往前赶。小时候看见爸爸妈妈的奖章，她总憋着劲想超过他们。现在她只想快点长大，能稳稳握住轮椅把手，替妈妈多推一段路。 整整13年，推着这台轮椅稳稳走下来的，是王戟的妻子黄春。 她身上裹着一层安静的气质，说不清是岁月磨出来的平和，还是疲惫到极致后的克制。说话间，她总不自觉地捏鼻梁，一下又一下捏同一个地方，捏到泛红。 黄春的日子，是一道道没有标准答案的选择题。 先送丈夫上班，还是先送女儿上学？选前者，孩子会迟到；选后者，丈夫赶不上热早饭。 带女儿去天津，还是留下来？重大项目攻关，她是主任设计师，必须顶上；带上孩子，就会耽误孩子的学业。 两头都是牵挂，两头都放不下。 她总说：“每个位置都要有人守住。”她守的又何止是一个位置呢？ 13年里，没有一个身份允许她擅自“离岗”。每一道选择题，都要在天平两端掂了又掂。她只说了两句话：“只能这样选”“不可能事事完美”，语气轻得像一层薄雾。 说这些话的时候，她捏鼻梁的动作没有停，指尖越捏越重，关节悄悄用力，红痕从鼻梁漫到眼角。那句话好沉，卡在她喉咙里，好像一张嘴就要滚落出来——“我真的好累。” 她也只是个普通人，也会有那么几个瞬间想卸下所有身份，做回能倚在丈夫肩头的女人。这样，她就不用对着病危通知书故作坚强，不用在爱人的治疗和女儿的学业间反复权衡。 倘若她真的停下来歇一歇，谁又忍心苛责她半分？谁又舍得催她再往前半步？ 可她没有。她只是一下一下捏着鼻梁，用那一点尖锐的、生理性的疼，把翻涌的情绪、到了嘴边的软弱，一点点按回心底。 王戟都懂。 正因为懂，他才不肯停下来。他知道，自己多自理一分，妻子的担子就轻一分；自己往前进一步，她悬着的心就降一寸。 残肢疼得钻心的时候，他会拨通妻子的电话。通了，他却不说话，就沉默几秒。妻子什么都明白，王戟也知道她什么都明白。 挂断电话，黄春望向窗外的雨，低声说道：“王戟的残肢，一定又在疼了。” 版式设计：王 凤 图片摄影：栾 铖
