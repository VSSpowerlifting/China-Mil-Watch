Nuclear Political Work

On His Final Journey, He Still Pressed Forward for Family and Nation

最后一程，他仍为家国前行
PLA Daily (解放军报) 31 May 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
Fourteen years after his death, Chinese military media is republishing a commemorative profile of Lin Junde, a Chinese Academy of Engineering academician who spent 52 years at the Malan Base (马兰基地) in Lop Nor developing nuclear test instrumentation, including the clockwork pressure self-recording device that supported China's first atomic bomb detonation in 1964, and who continued working on a laptop in his hospital ward until dying of cholangiocarcinoma on May 31, 2012. The piece fits a recurring PLA propaganda genre that uses 'Two Bombs, One Satellite' scientists to model the ideal of self-abnegating service — captured here in the stock phrase 干惊天动地事，做隐姓埋名人 ('do deeds that shake the heavens; be a person who buries their name in anonymity') — and its value is as a record of how the defense establishment frames sacrifice and institutional loyalty for a contemporary audience, not as evidence of new policy or capability.
Translation

Fourteen years ago today, in a ward at Xi'an Tangdu Hospital, a gaunt old man wearing an oxygen mask forced his ailing body upright and sat at a desk to work. The glow of a computer screen fell across his graying brows and hair; the room was utterly still, broken only by the intertwined sounds of a mouse clicking and a cardiac monitor beeping — these were the final hours of Academician Lin Junde, age 74.

Lin Junde was born in 1938 in a remote mountain village in Yongchun, Fujian. His father died young, leaving his mother to raise five children alone in grinding poverty. He dropped out before finishing primary school to farm the land, yet he never abandoned the pursuit of knowledge. In the dim light of an oil lamp, secondhand books picked up wherever he could find them were his only solace.

The dawn of New China illuminated the path forward for this boy from a poor family. With the help of a state scholarship, Lin Junde returned to the classroom.

In 1955, Lin Junde was admitted to the Department of Mechanical Engineering at Zhejiang University, where, supported by the state, he studied diligently and completed his degree with distinction. "Children of the poor learn to shoulder responsibility early" — the hardships of life taught him that without the Communist Party, he would have had nothing. Carrying that sense of gratitude, Lin Junde always threw himself into his work with exceptional intensity.

In the year he graduated, he swore an oath to serve the motherland, chose to enlist, and entered the Harbin Military Engineering Institute to pursue advanced study in nuclear testing.

Shortly after enlisting, Lin Junde was assigned to the Malan Base to participate in the development of China's first atomic bomb. With a heart set on serving the nation, he walked into Lop Nor — known as the "Sea of Death."

Deep in the Gobi of the northwest, yellow sands rolled endlessly across a vast and uninhabited expanse.

At the time, foreign technology was blockaded and there was no domestic experience to draw upon. Lin Junde led his team to start from scratch, developing a clockwork-type pressure self-recording instrument that provided critical data for the detonation of China's first atomic bomb, an achievement that earned a State Science and Technology Progress Award. That year, he was only 26 years old — just four years out of Zhejiang University.

From that point on, he remained rooted in the desert Gobi.

[Photo caption: Lin Junde at work. Image source: China Military Network]

Fifty-two years spent alongside the desert: he broke through multiple core technologies; received 2 State Invention Awards, 3 State Science and Technology Progress Awards, and 27 military and ministerial-level science and technology progress awards; was cited for meritorious service once at the first-class level, once at the second-class level, and twice at the third-class level; in 1987, he was honored to attend the All-Army Congress of Heroes and Model Figures; in 1990, he was designated by the state as an outstanding young and middle-aged expert with exceptional contributions; in 1999, he attended the commendation conference for scientists who made outstanding contributions to the "Two Bombs, One Satellite" program; in 2001, he was elected an academician of the Chinese Academy of Engineering……

Then, in May 2012, Lin Junde's condition deteriorated. After a massive gastrointestinal hemorrhage, he was transferred to the ICU. When he regained consciousness, he said, weakly but with resolve: "There is no computer here, and visiting hours are limited — I cannot work. Please transfer me back to a general ward." When doctors urged him to remain under observation, he shook his head with great difficulty: "Lying here like this is worse than dying. I would rather have one day of quality than ten days without it."

[Photo caption: The gravely ill Lin Junde (second from right) insisting on working. Image source: screenshot from CCTV's "National Memory" program]

On the afternoon of May 30, his body was threaded with tubes, yet he still sat at his desk for hours at a stretch. So as not to be disturbed, he twice demanded that his drainage tube and nasogastric tube be removed, his voice very soft but brooking no argument: "Working with tubes in me puts me off my game. What I need is time and efficiency." His family quietly wiped away tears; he kept his eyes fixed on the screen — inside it lay his final concerns.

On May 31, 2012, Academician Lin Junde completed the last stretch of his life's journey. From diagnosis of cholangiocarcinoma to death: only 27 days. Throughout those 27 days, he treated the hospital ward as a battlefield and his laptop computer as his final weapon, fighting until the very moment the flame of his life was extinguished.

The green waveform on the monitor gradually flattened into a straight line — he gave his last heartbeat, together with a lifetime of wisdom and loyalty, unreservedly to the motherland. From Malan in Lop Nor to a hospital bed in Beijing, from the detonation of the first atomic bomb to bending over his desk in his final hours, he spent his entire life giving meaning to the words: "Do deeds that shake the heavens and the earth; be a person who buries their name in anonymity (干惊天动地事，做隐姓埋名人)." His heartbeat has stopped, but the nuclear shield he planted in the Gobi will stand guard over the motherland forever.

Text: Fu Yingying

Original Chinese
14年前的今天，西安唐都医院的病房里，一位身形枯槁的老人戴着氧气面罩，强撑着病体坐在桌前工作，电脑屏幕的光落在他花白的眉发间，屋内静谧无声，只能听到鼠标和心电监护仪交织的声音——这是74岁的林俊德院士最后的时光。 1938年，林俊德出生在福建永春的深山村落里。父亲早逝，母亲独撑五个孩子，家境贫寒。他小学未读完便辍学务农，却从未放弃求知。昏暗油灯下，捡来的旧书是唯一的慰藉。 新中国的曙光，照亮了寒门少年的前路。靠着国家助学金，林俊德重回课堂。 1955年，林俊德考入浙江大学机械系，在国家帮助下勤学苦读，圆满完成学业。“穷人的孩子早当家”，艰苦的生活让他明白，没有共产党就没有自己的一切。抱着这样的感恩之情，林俊德在工作时总是特别拼命。 毕业那年，他立誓报效祖国，选择了参军，进入哈尔滨军事工程学院进修核试验专业。 林俊德参军后不久，就被派往马兰基地参与第一颗原子弹的研发。他怀着一颗报国心，走进了被称为“死亡之海”的罗布泊。 大西北的戈壁深处，黄沙滚滚，杳无人烟。 那时，国外技术封锁，国内没有任何经验可借鉴，林俊德带领团队从零做起，研制出钟表式压力自记仪，为我国首颗原子弹爆炸提供了关键数据，还拿到了国家科技进步奖。这一年，他只有26岁，从浙江大学毕业才4年。 此后，他一直扎根在大漠戈壁。 正在工作的林俊德。图片来源：中国军网 52载与大漠为伴，突破多项核心技术，获得国家发明奖2项，国家科技进步奖3项，军队和部委级科技进步奖27项；荣立一等功1次、二等功1次、三等功2次；1987年，光荣出席全军英模代表大会；1990年被国家评为有突出贡献的中青年专家；1999年出席“两弹一星”突出贡献科技专家表彰大会；2001年当选中国工程院院士…… 直至2012年5月，林俊德病情恶化，消化道大出血后被送进ICU。醒来后，他虚弱却坚定地说：“这里没有电脑，探望时间又有限，我没法工作，请把我转回普通病房。”医生劝他多观察，他艰难地摇摇头：“这样呆着，比死了还难受。我宁要有质量的一天，也不要没有质量的十天。” 病重的林俊德（右二）坚持工作。图片来源：中央电视台《国家记忆》栏目截图 5月30日下午，他身上插满管子，却依然坐在办公桌前，一坐就是几小时。为了不被打扰，他两次要求拔掉引流管和胃管，声音很轻却不容商量：“带着管子工作不在状态，我需要的是时间和效率。”家人偷偷抹泪，他却只顾盯着屏幕——那里面，装着他最后的牵挂。 2012年5月31日，林俊德院士走完了人生最后一段路。从确诊胆管癌到去世，仅仅27天。这27天里，他把病房当作战场，把笔记本电脑当作最后的武器，一直战斗到生命之火熄灭的那一刻。 监护仪上的绿色波形渐渐拉成一条直线——他把最后一次心跳，连同毕生的智慧与忠诚，毫无保留地献给了祖国。从罗布泊的马兰到北京的病床，从第一颗原子弹爆炸到临终前的伏案工作，他用一生诠释了什么叫“干惊天动地事，做隐姓埋名人”。心跳停止了，但他种在戈壁滩上的核盾牌，永远坚守祖国。 文字：付颖颖 海报：王珣
