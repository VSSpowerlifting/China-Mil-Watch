Visiting the Frontier in the New Era | One More Year, and It Will Be a Century

踏访新时代边关丨再过一年，就是百年
PLA Daily (解放军报) 1 August 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
On the eve of Army Day 2025—the PLA's 99th founding anniversary—PLA Daily published a feature tracing frontier garrison life across the Tibet Military District, the easternmost outpost (where First Sergeant Lei Hui has served since the company's founding in 2008), a 5,592-meter snow-mountain observation post, and South China Sea island positions, anchored by a video call between a 99-year-old veteran of the 'Red Ninth Company' and its current officers and soldiers. The piece is standard political-work content ahead of a milestone anniversary; its analytical value is as a record of how the PLA frames intergenerational loyalty, the 建军一百年奋斗目标 (centennial army-building goal), and 戍边精神 (frontier-defense spirit) to junior enlisted personnel, not as evidence of changed posture or new capability at any of the named locations.
Translation

One More Year, and It Will Be a Century

■ PLA Daily Reporter Chen Xiaojing

The wind comes from the frontier.

It sweeps across the unbroken snowline of the Karakoram, brushes past the first ray of sunlight at the easternmost outpost, circles around the waves of the South China Sea islands and reefs, and spirals around the radar antenna atop some nameless peak. It is Army Day again — that most singular of days. Following the wind into outpost after outpost along the frontier, what rushes to meet you is the breath of the single step between 99 years of history and the century soon to arrive, and the unwavering resolve (矢志不变的坚守) that has never changed.

What do 99 years mean to an army? What do they mean to a soldier standing watch at the frontier?

On the eve of this year's Spring Festival, the lights in the study room of the "Red Ninth Company" of a certain brigade under the Tibet Military District came on early. The entire company's officers and soldiers sat together in solemn rows, waiting for a special video call. On the other end of the screen was Lu Chen, the company's former political instructor, now 99 years old — he joined the revolution in 1943, served 47 years in the military, and spent half his life keeping watch over the snow-covered plateau. The old soldier slowly straightened up before the camera, the corners of his wrinkled eyes etched with the frost of years. When he spoke, his voice was slightly hoarse but every word rang with force: "Comrades, I am a member of the 'Red Ninth Company.' I once took root on the plateau, just as you do now."

Across the screen, the old soldier and the new soldiers made a pledge: in 2027, on the centennial of the founding of the army and the centennial of the company's founding, the hundred-year-old veteran will meet with the officers and soldiers once more across the distance. A 99-year-old veteran and soldiers of eighteen or nineteen years of age, under the same military flag, made the same promise.

The old soldier is not old — youth is still growing.

At the easternmost outpost, at 5:14 in the morning, the first ray of sunlight falls on the boundary marker. First Sergeant Lei Hui lightly wipes the marker, his movements practiced and precise. This is his 18th year standing guard at the eastern extreme. When the company was formed and took over the island post in 2008, the island was overgrown with knee-high weeds, with no fixed barracks, no water or electricity supply — only a few temporary prefabricated huts. "Set the post before settling in, patrol before making camp" — the officers and soldiers forged the patrol routes step by step with their own feet. Eighteen years on, new-model patrol vehicles, unmanned aerial vehicles, and motorized sleds have been fielded, and digital equipment has been updated generation after generation. Lei Hui's training notebooks record the company's 18-year transformation from "holding the line" to "fighting to win." Over 18 years, he went from new recruit to veteran; over 18 years, a company grew from its founding to become a benchmark.

One person's 18 years and an army's 99 years converge before the boundary marker. That is time's measure of loyalty, a covenant between founding purpose and steadfast resolve.

At the summit of a snow mountain at 5,592 meters above sea level, a group of new recruits had a "cloud-top encounter" with a border-guard stone (戍边石) before going on watch for the first time. On the other end of the screen was a display case at the Military Museum of the Chinese People's Revolution — inside it sat a border-guard stone from their very outpost, the red characters reading "Observation Post 5592" standing out with particular prominence. Standing watch in a zone forbidden to life, the soldiers used their spare time to gather mountain stones, grinding, tracing, and painting them — every brushstroke an expression of devotion to the post, every touch of color an act of loyalty to the motherland. This special encounter was an important lesson for new recruits to feel the glory of the outpost and carry forward the spirit of frontier defense (戍边精神).

A stone traveled thousands of kilometers from a snow mountain to the Military Museum; a spirit has been passed down from veteran to recruit across decades.

Chairman Xi has emphasized that youth are the new force driving the great rejuvenation of the Chinese nation. Ninety-nine years ago, a gunshot rang out over Nanchang, piercing the deep darkness of night. Over those 99 years, from the spark at Jinggangshan to the outposts along ten-thousand-li of frontier, generation after generation of young officers and soldiers have assembled under the military flag, guarding the sacred national territory with blood and loyalty.

Today, who are the people standing watch over the frontier line on the threshold of 99 years?

They are the new recruits who, in temperatures below minus 30 degrees Celsius, follow the silhouette of their squad leader and trudge forward one step at a time on patrol; they are the veterans deep in the forest at remote outposts who treasure the frost-covered photographs on the "wall of smiling faces" as footnotes to their youth; they are the old company commander at the Little White Poplar Outpost (小白杨哨所) who crossed mountains and rivers to return to his second hometown and, after more than ten years away, can still accurately call out the name of every soldier in the group photo; they are the first sergeant gazing out over the sea and sky at the South China Sea islands and reefs, declaring the oath "the national flag hangs on my chest, the motherland is in my heart"; they are the ordinary soldiers on the Gobi Desert swinging pickaxes to dig the earth, wresting green back from nature inch by inch; they are every silently standing figure along the motherland's long land borders and coastlines.

From post-2000 new recruits to veterans who have held the line for nearly 30 years — at the frontier, the word "youth" (青春) means more than age; it is a posture of wholehearted devotion to the Party (铁心向党), a relay of passing the torch from one generation to the next, and an unchanging quality that transcends mountains and seas.

Achieving the centennial goal of army-building (建军一百年奋斗目标) is a relay race, and every soldier must run their own leg well. Looking from the 99th year of the army's founding toward the 100th — every soldier standing guard at the frontier is listening, in their own way, to the Supreme Commander's charge and orders, standing their post well, keeping their watch well, and guarding the border well for the motherland.

The 99-year-old veteran is waiting for the centennial promise; the veteran of 18 years at the frontier stands watch in an 18-year-old company; the new recruit just arrived at the post is writing their name into the next chapter of glorious history, composing their own song of youth.

Ninety-nine years — years of extraordinary struggle (岁月峥嵘). One more year, and it will be a century.

When that day comes, the wind will still blow in from the frontier. It will sweep across the same snow mountains, brush past the same boundary markers, circle around the same islands and reefs. Only by then, new young faces will be standing watch at the posts, their upright bearing catching all the echoes of loyalty carried in that wind.

Engels said: time is the army. At this moment, for the soldiers standing guard at the frontier, those words carry a meaning all their own.

Original Chinese
再过一年，就是百年 ■解放军报记者 陈小菁 风从边关来。 掠过喀喇昆仑连绵的雪线，拂过东极哨所的第一缕阳光，绕过南海岛礁的浪花，在某座无名高山的雷达天线上回旋。又是一年八一建军节，如此特别的日子，跟随风的脚步走进座座边关哨所，扑面而来的，是99年的光阴与即将到来的100年之间，那一步之遥的呼吸，那矢志不变的坚守。 99年，对一支军队意味着什么？对一名守在边关的士兵又意味着什么？ 今年春节前夕，西藏军区某旅“红九连”学习室的灯早早亮了。全连官兵端坐一堂，等待一场特殊的连线。屏幕那一端，是99岁的连队老指导员路晨——1943年参加革命，47载军旅，半生守望雪域高原。老兵在镜头那边缓缓坐直，布满皱纹的眼角写满岁月的风霜。他开口时声音微哑却字字铿锵：“战友们，我是‘红九连’一员，曾经和你们一样扎根高原。” 隔着屏幕，老兵与新兵做了一个约定：2027年，建军百年、建连百年之际，百岁老兵将与官兵再次隔空相见。99岁老兵与十八九岁的战士，在同一面军旗下，许下同一个诺言。 老兵不老，青春依然在生长。 东极哨所，清晨5时14分，第一缕阳光落在界碑上。一级上士雷辉轻拂界碑，动作娴熟利落。今年是他戍守东极的第18年。2008年连队组建上岛接防，彼时的岛上荒草没膝，无固定营房、无水电保障，只有几间临时搭建的板房。“没安家先设哨，未扎营先巡逻”，官兵硬是用双脚一点点蹚出巡逻路。18年过去，新型巡逻车、无人机、摩托雪橇列装，数字化装备更新换代。雷辉的训练笔记，记录了18年来连队从“守得住”到“打得赢”的转型历程。18年，他从新兵变成老兵；18年，一支连队从初建到成为标杆。 一个人的18年，与一支军队的99年，在界碑前重叠。那是时间对忠诚的丈量，是初心与坚守的约定。 海拔5592米的雪山之巅，一批新兵在初次上哨前与一块戍边石“云端相见”。屏幕那头，是中国人民革命军事博物馆的展柜——里面陈列着一块来自他们哨所的戍边石，上面的“5592观察哨”几个红色字样格外醒目。坚守在生命禁区，战士们利用业余时间捡拾山石，打磨、勾线、上色，每一笔都是对哨位的热爱，每一抹色彩都是对祖国的忠诚。这场特殊的见面，是让新兵感受哨所荣光、传承戍边精神的重要一课。 一块石头，从雪山到军博走了几千公里；一种精神，从老兵到新兵传承了数十载光阴。 习主席强调，青年是实现中华民族伟大复兴的生力军。99年前，南昌城头一声枪响，划破沉沉暗夜；99年来，从井冈山的星火到万里边关的哨位，一代代青年官兵在军旗下集结，用热血与忠诚守护着神圣国土。 今天，站在99年门槛上守望边防线的，是一群什么样的人？ 是在零下30多摄氏度的严寒中，跟着班长的背影一步一个脚印巡逻跋涉的新兵；是在林海深处点哨里，把“笑脸墙”上一张张冰霜覆面的照片，珍藏为青春注脚的老兵；是在小白杨哨所，跨越千山万水重返第二故乡，在阔别10余载后依然能准确叫出合影中每一名战士名字的老连长；是在南海岛礁凝望海空，喊出“国旗挂胸前，祖国装心中”誓言的一级上士；是在戈壁滩上挥镐刨坑，把绿色一寸寸从大自然手中夺回来的普通一兵；是在祖国漫长陆地边境线和海岸线上，每一个默默伫立的身影。 从“00后”新兵到坚守近30年的老兵——“青春”二字，在边关不只意味着年龄，更是一种铁心向党的姿态、一种薪火相传的接力、一种跨越山海不变的底色。 实现建军一百年奋斗目标是一场接力跑，每名军人都要跑好自己那一棒。从建军99年瞩望建军100年——每一名戍守边关的军人，都在用自己的方式聆听统帅的嘱托和号令，为祖国站好岗、放好哨、守好边。 99岁的老兵在等100年的约定；戍边18载的老兵守在18岁的连队；刚上哨的新兵，正把名字写进下一段光辉历史，谱写属于自己的青春之歌。 99年，岁月峥嵘。再过一年，就是百年。 到那时，风还会从边关来。掠过同样的雪山，拂过同样的界碑，绕过同样的岛礁。只是那时候，又会有新的年轻面孔坚守在哨位上，用挺拔的身姿，接住这阵风里所有关于忠诚的回响。 恩格斯说，时间就是军队。此时此刻，对坚守边关的军人来说，这句话别具一番深意。
