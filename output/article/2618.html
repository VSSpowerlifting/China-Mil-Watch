About this analysis
Summary
Staff Sergeant First Class Tian Dongliang, a battlefield casualty care instructor at a brigade under the People's Armed Police Guangdong General Corps, was removed from his lead instructor position two years ago when a lesson plan review found his materials outdated and disconnected from 新质任务需求 (new-quality mission requirements); after 177 revisions, cross-unit observation visits, and reinstatement through the corps' itinerant 巡教巡考 (instruction and examination) process, his lesson plan was rated a model and his heuristic methods — including 方法对抗 (method competition) and 开小口、挖深井 (opening a small aperture, drilling a deep well) — have been adopted brigade-wide and selected for General Corps training materials. The article documents the brigade's institutional response to a specific structural problem: over-reliance on individual instructor experience (能人组训, 'capable individuals running training') that caused training quality to fluctuate with personnel turnover, addressed through a tiered 准入链 (qualification chain) that makes lesson plan approval a prerequisite for teaching. The brigade commander's candid acknowledgment of 本领恐慌 (capability panic) among instructors facing new-domain equipment — where teaching materials lag fielding — extends a pattern visible across PAP and PLA training reform coverage: the gap between equipment modernization and instructor competence remains an unresolved institutional bottleneck.

**News Sample: A Lesson Plan Revised 177 Times**

■ Zhu Hang, Wen Xian

This summer, inside the medical training room of a certain brigade under the People's Armed Police Guangdong General Corps, Staff Sergeant First Class Tian Dongliang was bent over his desk revising a battlefield casualty care lesson plan. Layers of paper and ink, old and new, recorded the traces of revision.

"The SCENE assessment method for combat casualty care environments has received a strong response in recent training — I recommend increasing its instructional weight." During a recent mobility force medical support exercise, Tian Dongliang received feedback from soldiers and adjusted his instructional design, supplementing the relevant content.

Over the past two years, this lesson plan has been revised 177 times — a number that reflects Tian Dongliang's profound transformation from "experience first" to "utility as the foundation."

A lesson plan review two years ago sounded the alarm for Tian Dongliang, who was then the lead instructor for the battlefield casualty care subject at the brigade. A lesson plan that had previously earned him high praise failed the review because its content was outdated and disconnected from new-quality mission requirements (新质任务需求).

"That lesson plan was resting on its laurels (功劳簿), barely touched for years." Tian Dongliang admitted candidly that the real-world challenges far exceeded his expectations: carefully designed instructional methods failed to produce good results in training, and years of accumulated experience repeatedly "stalled" when confronted with new subjects. He suddenly realized that resting on those same laurels was his own long-calcified way of thinking. A two-year process of "grinding the lesson" (磨课) began from that moment.

Over those two years, Tian Dongliang carried a notebook into the front lines of exercises and training: consulting examiners on changes to scoring criteria, discussing efficient training organization methods with backbone cadre, and crouching on the training ground to capture every sticking point and every "didn't expect that" in soldiers' execution. From the logical architecture of instructional materials to the direction of innovation in teaching methods, to the precise positioning of key content — every link was repeatedly refined.

"I'm not afraid of revising — I'm afraid of revising and having it be useless." During that period, every time he added a new casualty care action, he would practice it himself more than ten times before finding soldiers to verify its effectiveness, ensuring that everything "brought in from the field" in the lesson plan consisted of genuine, practical techniques (真招实招) needed on the battlefield.

177 revisions produced a refined, high-quality lesson plan, and also forged Tian Dongliang's capacity to organize training in close alignment with actual combat and with the courage to innovate. Today, heuristic instructional methods such as "method competition" (方法对抗) from the lesson plan have been promoted across the brigade; core content such as training organization procedures has been selected by the General Corps for inclusion in medical support training materials; and the continuously expanding "problem bank" in the lesson plan is driving training toward greater depth and substance.

**From "Capable Individuals Running Training" to "Standards Running Training" —**

**Lesson Plan Review Stops a Veteran "Combat Instructor" in His Tracks**

Not long ago, the People's Armed Police Guangdong General Corps concluded its itinerant instruction and examination (巡教巡考) activity on front-line treatment capabilities. The lesson plan of Staff Sergeant First Class Tian Dongliang of a certain brigade was rated a "model lesson plan."

After rigorous trial instruction and teaching performance review, Tian Dongliang was reinstated as lead instructor for the battlefield casualty care subject at the brigade. At that moment, exactly two years had passed since he was removed from that position.

The starting point of this road back lay in an exploration undertaken by the unit.

Two years ago, brigade leadership discovered that the quality of training organization for certain subjects depended heavily on the personal capabilities of individual instructors; instructional experience was passed down orally among backbone cadre, and once an instructor was rotated out or departed the unit, soldiers' training results would fluctuate.

At the same time, some instructors had gone long periods without studying their lesson plans — in some cases, lesson plan content had gone unchanged for years, with only individual words, phrases, or dates modified.

"This dependence on experience has affected the scientific rigor and quality-effectiveness of training organization," the brigade leadership pointed out. "We must break this experience-dependent training organization model of 'capable individuals running training' (能人组训)."

To that end, the brigade Party committee adjusted the mechanism for cultivating professional-position instructors. Departing from the previous review approach — in which passing an evaluation of instructional methods was sufficient to "take the post" — the brigade explored and established a rigid review system: "Only when the lesson plan stands up may one take the podium to teach; only when the lesson is taught solidly may one be assigned a teaching position; only when teaching performance is strong may one qualify as lead instructor." This progressively layered "qualification chain" (准入链) became a threshold every instructor in the brigade must cross; those who fail must "re-forge" (回炉).

After the system was piloted, at the very first stage — the lesson plan review — multiple veteran "combat instructors," including Tian Dongliang, were compelled to "stand down."

The review panel pointed out that Tian Dongliang's lesson plan was insufficiently integrated with the unit's new missions and lacked support from case data and new operational and training methods.

After the review, Tian Dongliang consulted a large volume of battlefield casualty care cases and repeatedly refined every step, attempting to close the gap.

However, after submitting several successive drafts, the response he received each time was: "There's progress, but it's still not quite there."

Staff Officer Li of the Operations and Training Section commented: "If you want to get through the lesson plan review, the last few drafts can basically score 70 points. But if you genuinely want to grind out a good lesson, you can step outside the 'small circle' in your own head and open up your thinking."

These words awakened Tian Dongliang. After trial instruction and teaching restored his qualification to teach, Tian Dongliang used opportunities such as the General Corps' itinerant instruction and examination activities on front-line treatment capabilities and instructional review observation events to visit every brigade-level unit in the General Corps, researching instructional theory and exchanging instructional methods with other instructors.

During one cross-specialty cross-training observation, faced with the demanding conditions of limited time and trainees with zero baseline, many instructors flatly declared it "impossible." Unexpectedly, by the end of instruction, multiple groups of officers and soldiers had, under their instructors' guidance, competently mastered basic actions and key tactical response procedures. Tian Dongliang keenly observed that the groups with outstanding instructional results had also received high scores at the lesson plan review stage.

Through the "re-forging" process, Tian Dongliang and others gradually came to a deep understanding of the value of this exploration: first, it distills instructors' experience into standardized written text, achieving systematic inheritance of knowledge and skills; second, it guides instructors to clarify pathways for capability advancement, shortening the growth cycle for trainees; third, it breaks the dependence on experience, achieving the transformation from "capable individuals running training" to "standards running training."

**From "Standardized Instruction" to "Heuristic Methods" —**

**Military Democracy (军事民主) Injects New Vitality into Instruction**

During an instructional observation, two instructors from a certain brigade demonstrated the same subject but with entirely different steps, content, and methods — instruction was immediately halted. After class, when Tian Dongliang and others reviewed the lesson plans, they found that some content deviated from the syllabus, and instructional elements were missing.

"A standardized lesson plan plays an indispensable foundational role in instruction," Tian Dongliang observed.

But a good lesson plan is far more than standardization. Tian Dongliang organized the foundational framework of the lesson plan, clarified "what to practice and to what standard" for trainees in each action module, and made priorities and standards clear to trainees. At the same time, he streamlined theoretical skills unrelated to the main thread, added a "core instructional objectives" section, and focused on three core areas: cultivating tactical coordination awareness, mastering technical means, and improving the capacity for on-the-spot response (临机处置能力).

A clear lesson plan framework not only straightened out his instructional thinking but also allowed Tian Dongliang to gradually sense the pain points in training organization.

"To solve problems more efficiently, I visited every company." Tian Dongliang recounted an experience of being hit with "three consecutive questions" at one company —

The first time, he lectured soldiers on "battlefield triage," speaking fluently and in detail. During a break, a Private First Class asked him: "Sergeant, inside an armored vehicle there's not enough space — the casualty is lying sideways and I can't roll him over. What do I do?" Tian Dongliang had no answer.

The second time, he lectured on "timing of tourniquet application," and a soldier asked: "What if the casualty is psychologically traumatized and out of control?" Again, Tian Dongliang had no answer.

The third time, he simply stopped lecturing and asked the soldiers: "What are your questions?" The soldiers spoke all at once: "How do you drag a heavy casualty into a shell crater by yourself?" "How do you find a vein at night without a light?" Tian Dongliang filled three full pages of notes. When he returned, he added those three pages to the lesson plan and wrote down one reflection: "What we used to teach was what was in the textbook. What officers and soldiers want is what works on the battlefield. There is a considerable gap between the two."

Identifying the problem was only the starting point; solving it was the key. To that end, Tian Dongliang explored a heuristic and inductive instructional approach of "opening a small aperture, drilling a deep well" (开小口、挖深井), vigorously promoting military democracy (军事民主) and guiding trainees to jointly work through difficult problems in the classroom.

During one field lesson, Corporal Xiao Xun fixed a drag strap buckle to a "casualty's" tactical vest and dragged and carried the "casualty." Tian Dongliang immediately halted the action: "The casualty has neck and arm injuries — dragging will inevitably cause secondary injury." Xiao Xun then tried several other methods; each time Xiao Xun adjusted his approach, Tian Dongliang organized everyone to analyze the advantages and disadvantages of that method. After several rounds of discussion, the group decided to adopt the lateral crawl carry method. Tian Dongliang calls this training organization method "method competition" (方法对抗).

Today, the "method competition-style" research instruction, "problem research and deduction-style" (问题研推式) scenario instruction, and "actual-position grouping-style" (实岗编组式) exercise instruction designed by Tian Dongliang have been widely promoted across the brigade. As the question bank continues to expand, lesson plan content for each subject is increasingly close to actual combat and grows ever thicker — officers and soldiers of one company were inspired during organized practice sessions and divided first-aid supplies by mission type to achieve modular carry; a medic from one battalion innovated and improved operational techniques to shorten tourniquet preparation time…

**From "Done and Finished" to "Dynamic Freshness" —**

**Turning the Lesson Plan into Something Living and Continuously Growing**

The revision and refinement of a lesson plan is a process of becoming thinner, then thicker again.

Adding "casualty care methods when the casualty is psychologically traumatized"; deleting training content related to nighttime conditions "using a flashlight for illumination," and supplementing training in battlefield casualty care skills under no-light conditions such as "tactile positioning method" (触感定位法)…

"The syllabus and teaching materials are macro-level and principle-based, while the exercise and training ground is specific and dynamic. How do you translate macro-level requirements into actionable instructional behaviors? How do you refine principle-based provisions into concrete training steps? All of this requires the lesson plan as a 'bridge' connecting the syllabus and teaching materials to the exercise and training ground." Moving from instructor to lead instructor requires thinking more deeply. Tian Dongliang established a lesson plan "freshness period" (保鲜期): drawing on research findings and exercise feedback, certain modules in the lesson plan are annotated with usage cycles, reminding him to update them dynamically.

"Revising a lesson plan cannot be 'done and finished.'" During the interview, Company Commander Wang of the medical company commented: "Tian Dongliang has turned the lesson plan into something living and continuously growing."

Private First Class Luo Yawen of one company once brought Tian Dongliang an insight. During an exercise last year, Luo Yawen pointed out that the hands-on skills in Tian Dongliang's "crush injury first aid" module were outdated: "In the decompression step, what we've worked out is to ligate the limb and record the time before releasing pressure, and to first apply local cooling with cold water to the injured limb. These critical details were never mentioned in class — the methods we worked out ourselves are more practical."

Tian Dongliang carefully recorded Luo Yawen's treatment method, verified it repeatedly, and added it to "crush injury first aid techniques."

Reading through the lesson plan carefully, one can find that Tian Dongliang's lesson plan draws on multiple "sources of freshness" (保鲜源头) — asking headquarters examiners for new standards, asking "peers" for new instructional methods, asking grassroots soldiers for new "pain points," asking historical missions for new "experience," asking exercise after-action reviews for new "lessons learned"…

Last autumn, the incidence of lower back injuries at a certain unit rose noticeably. Through conversations with grassroots soldiers, Tian Dongliang found this was related to the unit's frequent carrying and lifting of "casualties" during self-rescue and buddy-rescue training, combined with insufficient lower back strength in some soldiers. He promptly added "3 categories of specialized lower back strength training targeting self-rescue and buddy-rescue subjects" to the lesson plan.

"Even the best lesson plan cannot remain unchanged." Before competing for the lead instructor position this time, Tian Dongliang felt an increasingly urgent sense of pressure and self-studied multiple military-career online courses to expand the boundaries of his knowledge. Cutting-edge content such as unmanned vehicle escort support, intelligent life monitoring and positioning, and AI triage has been incorporated into the lesson plan one after another. Multiple application scenarios have also been added to supplement traditional training content such as bandaging and immobilization, and projections have been made regarding the service life of corresponding equipment and the timing of future revision milestones.

"Keeping the lesson plan 'fresh' is a continuous process. Only by continuously shortening the instructional feedback cycle can the lesson plan's 'soft power' (软实力) be converted into trainees' tangible hard competencies." Tian Dongliang said.

**Sharp Perspective**

**A Lesson Plan Is Not a Fixed "Specimen"**

■ Chen Junjie

A lesson plan is the basic guide by which instructors organize instruction and an important foundation upon which trainees master skills. A lesson plan is not a fixed "specimen." Its "front sight" must always be aligned with the battlefield "target," always resonating at the same frequency as trainees' needs, and approaching the battlefield and securing future victory through continuous iteration.

Writing a lesson plan requires first answering the question of what the battlefield needs — thoroughly absorbing the dynamic changes in mission tasks, adversaries, battlefield environments, and weapons and equipment. Proactively go deep into the front lines of exercises and training, embed within combat formations, conduct after-action analysis of major exercises, skills competitions, and live-force opposition drills, distill experience and lessons, understand "pain points" and "bottlenecks," and sense the pulse of the battlefield. Promptly convert problems exposed during major missions, operational methods formed after new equipment is fielded, and experience summaries from opposition exercises into new content, new standards, and new thinking in lesson plans, continuously working backward from battlefield requirements to derive instructional content.

For different trainees, instructional design cannot apply "one ruler to measure everything" — it must produce a precise "profile" (画像) of their baseline capabilities, position requirements, and learning characteristics to achieve differentiated, tiered instruction tailored to the individual. Through daily observation, discussion sessions, and baseline assessments, grasp trainees' knowledge reserves and physical and technical skill status to establish the starting point of instruction; combine trainees' position and mission requirements to set instructional objectives; understand trainees' cognitive patterns, reception habits, and interest preferences to select the most appropriate instructional approach, and precisely formulate "advancement roadmaps" for trainees at different levels. Establish a routine feedback mechanism, collect real-time classroom feedback, identify weak links in lesson plans, and conduct follow-up tracking of instructional outcomes, allowing lesson plans to be continuously optimized through the cycle of "feedback — revision — re-instruction."

Instructors must, with a sense of responsibility toward officers and soldiers and toward combat effectiveness, attach importance to lesson plan updates, always maintain a "reset mindset" (归零心), target the "difficult points," "bottlenecks," and "pain points" of instruction, invest effort and energy, dynamically revise and optimize with the spirit of "hammering in a nail" (钉钉子精神), dare to start over from scratch, and pursue higher standards through repeated deliberation and refinement. Only by maintaining an open attitude at all times and the perseverance and tenacity of sustained effort can one continuously "refresh" instructional quality and keep the lesson plan's "freshness" perpetually "online."

**Editor's Connection**

**This Edition's Editor in Dialogue with the Commander of a Certain Brigade Under the People's Armed Police Guangdong General Corps —**

**From "Experience First" to "Utility as the Foundation"**

As the starting point of instruction, lesson plans directly affect the quality and effectiveness of military training. As the transformation of military training advances in depth, what new challenges does lesson plan writing face? How can one break through the barriers of formalism and point directly at battlefield requirements? Around this topic, this edition's editor connected with the commander of a certain brigade under the People's Armed Police Guangdong General Corps for an exchange.

**Editor:** In your view, what new changes have there been in lesson plan writing in recent years?

**Brigade Commander:** If the source is impure, the water flowing from it will be murky. As the core vehicle of instruction, lesson plan writing must be "precise" (精) and "substantive" (实), rejecting formalism and pursuing practical effectiveness. Today, writing lesson plans increasingly requires using the standard of fighting wars as the yardstick, and setting the methods and content of instruction and practice in accordance with the syllabus and the characteristics of trainees, adapting to local conditions. In recent years, as comprehensive integrated exercises have increased, the form of instruction has also shifted — from being primarily "I lecture, you listen; I demonstrate, you watch; I direct, you practice" toward primarily forging the combat qualities of both the instructor and the trainee. The purpose of instruction is no longer simply to teach, but to inspire everyone to think and practice through the process of teaching and learning, transforming the one-way-indoctrination training ground into a mutual-instruction, mutual-learning (互教互学) practice field, ultimately forming an atmosphere of "teaching and learning advancing together" (教学相长).

**Editor:** You just mentioned rejecting formalism. How can grassroots lesson plans avoid becoming "superficial" and hold the lifeline of "precision" and "substance"?

**Brigade Commander:** First, it must be made clear that the core of a military lesson plan is use, not appearance. The standard for judging whether a lesson plan is good has never been how many words and charts it contains or how beautiful its formatting is, but whether officers and soldiers enjoy listening in class, whether they can learn the skills, and whether it works on the battlefield. "Precision" means accurate content, concise language, clear logic — cutting the complex to get straight to the instructional core. "Substance" means close to actual combat, close to officers and soldiers, close to the mission — no hollow designs, no empty slogans, no wasted effort. We resolutely oppose lesson plans that have "combat-realistic orientation" (实战化) on their lips and written simply on paper, yet contain no substantive content whatsoever. True combat-realistic orientation is embedded in instructional design, instructional thinking, and instructional methods — embedded in every instructional detail — so that officers and soldiers can apply what they learn immediately after learning it, and applying it improves training quality and effectiveness. That is the fundamental point.

**Editor:** What challenges do instructors currently need to overcome in lesson plan writing?

**Brigade Commander:** They are mainly reflected in two areas:

First, the inertial thinking inherent in the instructor corps itself. Some instructors still harbor empiricism in their minds, adhering to the idea that "one trick is enough to get by everywhere" (一招鲜吃遍天), lacking the holistic thinking and reverse thinking needed to step outside their own professional domain and view problems from a broader perspective, and lacking the courage to boldly explore and innovate in lesson plan writing, trial instruction, and training organization.

Second, the "capability panic" (本领恐慌) brought about by training in new domains and new-quality areas (新域新质). As new equipment is fielded, the updating of teaching materials often lags behind equipment development, and many instructors can only "feel their way across the river." Instructors need to transform from being purely "training executors" into "pathfinders" (先行者) who understand the technology and can conduct in-depth research.

在“回炉”锻造中，田东亮等人渐渐深刻认识到此次探索的价值：一是将教练员的经验提炼为规范文本，实现知识技能的系统化传承；二是牵引教练员明晰能力进阶路径，缩短参训人员成长周期；三是破除经验依赖，实现从“能人抓训”到“标准抓训”的转型。 从“规范教学”到“启发方法”—— 军事民主激发教学新活力 在教学观摩中，某支队两名教练员演示同一课目，步骤、内容、方法却大相径庭，教学随即被叫停。课后，田东亮等人查阅教案发现，部分内容脱纲离谱，教学要素缺漏…… “一份标准化教案对于教学有着不可或缺的基础作用。” 田东亮评价道。 但一份好教案，绝不仅是标准化。田东亮梳理教案基础脉络，明确各动作模块受训者“练什么、练到什么程度”，让受训者清楚重点和标准。同时精简与主线无关的理论技能，新增“教学核心目标”板块，聚焦战术协同意识、掌握技术手段、提升临机处置能力三大核心。 清晰的教案脉络，不仅理顺了教学思路，还让田东亮逐渐感知到教学组训中的痛点。 “为更高效破题，我跑遍了各个中队。”田东亮讲述了在某中队被“三连问”的经历—— 第一次，他给战士们讲“战场检伤分类”，讲得头头是道。休息时一个上等兵问他：“班长，在装甲车里空间不够，人横躺着，我翻不过来，怎么办？”田东亮答不上来。 第二次，讲“止血带使用时机”，战士问：“要是伤员精神受刺激、不受控制，怎么办？”田东亮又答不上来。 第三次，他索性不讲了，问战士：“你们有啥困惑？”战士们七嘴八舌：“怎么一个人把大体重者拖进弹坑？”“夜间不开灯怎么找血管？”……田东亮记了满满3页纸。回来后，他把这3页纸加进教案，写下一句心得：“咱们以前教的，是教材上有的；官兵想要的，是战场上用的。这中间存在不小的距离。” 发现问题只是起点，解决问题才是关键。为此，田东亮探索“开小口、挖深井”的启发诱导式教学，大力发扬军事民主，引导受训者在课堂上共研难题。 一次现场课，下士肖逊将拖拽带锁扣固定在“伤员”战术背心上，拖拽搬运“伤员”。这个动作随即被田东亮叫停：“伤员颈臂受伤，拖拽必然造成二次伤害。”接着肖逊又想了几种方法，肖逊每调整一种方法，田东亮便组织大家分析该方法的优劣，经过几轮探讨，最终决定采取侧身匍匐搬运法。这种组训方法，田东亮称之为“方法对抗”。 如今，田东亮设计的“方法对抗式”研究教学、“问题研推式”想定教学、“实岗编组式”演练教学等被支队广泛推广，随着题库不断扩充，各课目教案内容也越来越贴近实战，越写越厚——某中队官兵在组织练习环节受到启发，将急救用品根据不同任务进行划分，实现模块化携带；某大队卫生员创新改良操作手法，缩短止血准备时间…… 从“完事即止”到“动态保鲜”—— 把教案变成活的、不断生长的东西 教案的修改完善，是一个变薄又变厚的过程。 增加“伤员精神刺激下救护办法”；删减夜间“利用手电筒照明”条件下的相关组训内容，增补“触感定位法”等无光条件下的战场救护技能训练…… “大纲、教材是宏观的、原则性的，而演训场是具体的、动态的。如何将宏观要求转化为可操作的教学行为？如何将原则性规定细化为具体的训练步骤？这都需要教案这个连接大纲、教材与演训场的‘桥梁’。”从教练员到主教练员，需要思考得更多，田东亮设定教案“保鲜期”：结合调研收获和演训反馈，教案部分模块标注了使用周期，提示自己动态更新。 “修改教案不能‘完事即止’。”采访中，卫生队王队长评价道，“田东亮把教案变成了活的、不断生长的东西。” 某中队上等兵罗亚文曾给田东亮带来启发。去年的某次演训中，罗亚文指出田东亮“挤压伤急救”这一模块实操技能过时：“解除挤压环节，我们摸索的是将肢体结扎并记录时间后再解压，伤肢处理是要用凉水先局部降温。这些紧要的细节课堂上完全没有提到，我们摸索的方法更实用。” 田东亮认真记录罗亚文的处置方法，经过反复验证，增补进“挤压伤急救技巧”。 细览教案，可以发现田东亮的教案还有多种“保鲜源头”——向机关考官问新标准、向“同行”问新教法、向基层战士问新“痛点”、向历史任务问新“经验”、向演训复盘问新“教训”…… 去年秋天，某单位腰伤发病率明显上升，田东亮与基层战士交流发现与该单位在自救互救训练中频繁背抱“伤员”、部分战士腰部力量不足有关。他随即在教案中新增了“针对自救互救课目的3类腰部力量专项训练”。 “再好的教案也不能一成不变。”在此次竞争主教练员之前，田东亮紧迫感越来越强烈，自学多门军职在线课程，拓展知识边界。无人车伴随保障、智能生命监测与定位、AI分诊等前沿内容陆续被纳入教案。对于包扎、固定等传统训练内容也补充了多种应用场景，并对相应装备器材使用期限和下步修订节点进行了预判。 “教案‘保鲜’是个持续的过程，不断缩短教学反馈周期，才能让教案的‘软实力’转化为受训者实实在在的硬本领。”田东亮说。 锐观点 教案不是凝固的“标本” ■陈俊杰 教案，是教练员组织教学的基本遵循和受训者掌握技能的重要依托。教案不是凝固的“标本”，它的“准星”要始终对准战场“靶心”，始终与受训者需求同频共振，在持续迭代中逼近战场、制胜未来。 编写教案，要先回答好战场需要什么的问题，充分吃透使命任务、作战对手、战场环境、武器装备的动态变化。主动深入演训一线、嵌入战斗编组，复盘分析重大演训、比武竞赛、实兵对抗情况，提炼经验教训，了解“痛点”“堵点”，感知战场脉搏；把重大任务中暴露出的问题、新装备列装后形成的战法、对抗演训中的经验总结及时转化为教案中的新内容、新标准、新思路，不断从战场需求倒推教学内容。 对不同受训者，教学设计不能“一把尺子量到底”，而要对其基础能力、岗位需求、学习特点进行精准“画像”，实现分层分类、因材施教。通过日常观察、座谈了解、摸底考核，掌握受训者的知识储备、体技能状况，明确教学的起点；结合受训者岗位任务要求，确立教学目标；了解受训者的认知规律、接受习惯、兴趣偏好，选择最合适的教学方式，为不同层次受训人员精准制定“进阶路线图”。建立常态反馈机制，收集课堂即时反馈，查找教案薄弱环节，跟踪回访教学效果，让教案在“反馈—修订—再教学”的循环中不断优化。 教练员要本着对官兵负责、对战斗力负责的态度，重视教案更新，常怀“归零心”，瞄准教学“难点”“堵点”“痛点”，投入心血、倾注精力，以“钉钉子”精神动态修订、优化，敢于推倒重来，在反复推敲打磨中追求更高标准。只有时刻保持开放的态度和久久为功的恒心韧劲，才能不断“刷新”教学质量，让教案的“新鲜度”始终保持“在线”。 编辑连线 本版编辑对话武警广东总队某支队支队长—— 从“经验为先”到“实用为本” 教案作为教学的起点，直接影响练兵质效。当前军事训练转型纵深推进，教案编写面临哪些新挑战？如何破除形式藩篱直指战场需求？围绕这一话题，本版编辑与武警广东总队某支队支队长进行连线交流。 编辑：在您看来，近年来教案编写有什么新的变化？ 支队长：源头不正，水流必浊，作为教学的核心载体，教案编写必须“精”“实”，摒弃形式主义，追求实用实效。如今，编写教案时，更多要求以打仗标准为准绳，根据大纲和受训者特点，因地制宜设定教和练的方法内容。近年来，随着综合连贯演练增多，教学的形式也从单纯的“我讲你听、我做你看、我说你练”为主向锤炼施教者本人和受训者战斗素养为主转变。教学的目的已不是单纯的教，而是启发大家在教与学的过程去思、去练，让单向灌输的训练场变成互教互学的练兵场，最终形成一种“教学相长”的氛围。 编辑：您刚刚提到摒弃形式主义，基层教案如何避免“浅表化”，守住“精”与“实”的生命线？ 支队长：首先要明确，军事教案的核心是用，不是看。评判一份教案好坏的标准，从来不是文字图表多不多、格式美不美，而是官兵在课堂上爱不爱听、训不训得会、战场上管不管用。所谓“精”，就是内容精准、语言精练、逻辑清晰，删繁就简，直奔教学核心；所谓实，是贴近实战、贴近官兵、贴近任务，不搞空洞设计、喊虚浮口号、做无用功。我们坚决反对把“实战化”挂在嘴边、简单写在纸上，却没有任何实质内容的教案。真正的实战导向，是藏在教学设计、教学思路、教学方法里，是藏在每一个教学细节里，让官兵学完就能用，用了就能提升训练质效，这才是根本。 编辑：当前教练员在教案编写方面有哪些需要克服的挑战？ 支队长：主要体现在两个方面： 一是教练员队伍本身的惯性思维。一些教练员的脑子里还存在经验主义，奉行“一招鲜吃遍天”，缺乏跳出自身从事的专业领域去看待问题的全局思维、逆向思维，对教案编写、试讲试教、任教组训等环节缺少大胆探索创新的勇气。 二是新域新质训练带来的“本领恐慌”。随着新型装备列装，教材更新往往滞后于装备发展，很多教练员只能“摸着石头过河”，教练员需要从单纯的“训练执行者”向懂技术、能钻研的“先行者”转变。
