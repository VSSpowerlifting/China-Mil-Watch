Exercises Modernization Doctrine

Accelerating the Transformation and Upgrading of Military Training | "Gold-Medal Pilot" Returns to the "Dragon-Tiger Rankings"

加快实现军事训练转型升级丨“金牌飞手”重登“龙虎榜”
PLA Daily (解放军报) 24 August 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
A brigade of the 72nd Group Army has, since early 2025, restructured its UAV assessment standards to weight air-ground coordination, manned-unmanned system linkage, and tactical integration alongside individual flight proficiency — a shift that caused experienced 'gold-medal pilots,' including Staff Sergeant First Class Zhao Kunjie, to fail the 'Dragon-Tiger Rankings' despite scoring full marks on basic subjects. The article documents a concrete institutional problem the PLA has acknowledged repeatedly but struggled to fix: UAV operators trained in single-aircraft, single-system pipelines lack the coordination habits needed to function inside a joint fires chain. The brigade's remedy — mandatory joint coursework, simultaneous ground-UAV exercises, and electromagnetic-interference scenarios — fits the broader push to operationalize 新域新质作战力量 (new-domain, new-quality combat forces) doctrine at the unit level, though the article gives no data on how widely this assessment reform has spread beyond this brigade.
Translation

A Brigade of the 72nd Group Army Deepens Manned-Unmanned Collaborative Training —— "Gold-Medal Pilot" Returns to the "Dragon-Tiger Rankings"

■ Jian Lei, Liu Xilei, and PLA Daily Special Correspondent Lu Dongyu

The brigade organizes air-ground collaborative reconnaissance training. Photo by Zhang Naiyou.

In summer, a brigade of the 72nd Group Army conducted an intensive combat-realistic UAV assessment. At the assessment site, Staff Sergeant First Class Zhao Kunjie skillfully operated a UAV and successfully completed a patrol flight mission.

Zhao Kunjie is a well-known "gold-medal pilot" in his unit, having placed in superior-level competitions on multiple occasions. During this assessment, he performed steadily throughout and scored full marks on all basic subjects. Yet when the overall assessment results were posted, Zhao Kunjie's name was nowhere to be found on the list of "outstanding pilots."

"Zero errors on the basic subjects — so why wasn't he rated outstanding?" Several young UAV pilots were baffled.

"No matter how skilled you are flying a single aircraft, that's only passing the basic skills threshold. If you can't keep pace with the ground element and can't integrate into tactical operations, you'll be useless on the battlefield." Squad Leader Zhang Hai's words struck directly at Zhao Kunjie's weak point. It turned out that although Zhao Kunjie had scored full marks on the basic subjects, he had lost significant points in air-ground coordination, intelligence linkage, and tactical cooperation, leaving his composite score below the outstanding standard.

"'Gold-medal pilots' failing to make the 'Dragon-Tiger Rankings' was not uncommon in this assessment," Staff Officer Li of the brigade's operations and training section told this reporter. In the past, UAV training was organized primarily around single-aircraft, single-system operations, with personnel from different specialties each training on their own, and coordination was relatively lacking. As new-domain, new-quality combat forces (新域新质作战力量) accelerate their integration into the joint operations system, the brigade has, since the beginning of this year, benchmarked against combat requirements and institutionalized air-ground coordination, manned-unmanned system linkage, and related content as mandatory coursework (必修课) in routine training, substantially increasing the score weighting of coordination subjects in assessments.

Many backbone personnel initially did not take the adjustment to training content seriously. Zhao Kunjie had a similar mindset: after years of grinding through the training ground, having encountered all manner of operational environments and special situations, he felt his accumulated "experience and routines" were more than sufficient to handle any assessment. What he did not anticipate was that this time, points were deducted on seemingly minor coordination details.

This assessment forced Zhao Kunjie and his comrades to confront a reality: no matter how sharp your individual skills, if you cannot integrate into the system, you cannot be considered truly capable; no matter how outstanding your performance in a single area, if you cannot keep pace with battlefield changes, you cannot win.

Brigade leaders explained that, in order to strengthen manned-unmanned collaborative training and drive alignment between personnel capabilities and battlefield requirements, they adjusted assessment standards to guide personnel away from outdated notions of "fighting alone" and "each fighting their own battle" (单打独斗、各自为战), and to embed systems-coordination (体系协同) operational thinking into daily training. They convened backbone personnel from all specialties to jointly sort through coordination problems and clarify air-ground coordination procedures; they routinely organized UAV sub-units and ground squads to train simultaneously and conduct joint exercises; and they set up special situations such as strong electromagnetic interference and communications blackouts on an ad hoc basis, compelling everyone to strengthen coordination skills through realistic, full-resistance training.

After this assessment, Zhao Kunjie underwent a transformation. At the control station, he no longer focused solely on equipment parameters and flight attitude; instead, he coordinated tactical intent with ground-element comrades in advance and grasped the rhythm of combat. Once the UAV was airborne, he kept his eyes fixed on the battlefield situation, ensuring that wherever the ground element advanced, air support followed.

"Equipment is being upgraded, training programs are being refined, and the battlefield is constantly evolving," reflected Battalion Commander Min of a battalion in the brigade. Modern warfare is about systems and integration — old experience alone will not take you far, and a single-domain advantage alone will not win. Only by actively integrating into the operational chain and training with an eye toward combat requirements can you withstand the test of the battlefield.

In a tactical assessment held not long ago, Zhao Kunjie served as the primary UAV pilot in coordination with a ground element during a combined attack. Once the assessment began, he operated the UAV to relay battlefield information in real time, with zero delay and zero error throughout, successfully completing the coordinated combat mission. When the overall assessment results were posted, Zhao Kunjie had returned to the "Dragon-Tiger Rankings."

Embrace the System, Integrate into the System

■ Yang Yue

"One who does not plan for the whole is not fit to plan for a part." This saying is not the exclusive province of commanders — it is a warning that every officer and soldier in the combat system must internalize. Modern warfare has long since left behind single-system, single-domain contests and entered the stage of systems confrontation (体系对抗). Practice has repeatedly demonstrated: one weak link can lead to the loss of one domain, and even to passivity across the board. Winning future wars requires far more than excellence at a single position; only when every link and every post firmly establishes coordination awareness and systems thinking (协同意识和体系思维) can local excellence support overall victory.

Whether it is a reconnaissance UAV patrolling overhead or an assault unit on the forward position, every element is interlocked and tightly connected. A misalignment at one link, a lag in one instance of coordination, will constrain the overall effectiveness of combat operations. If officers and soldiers cannot break out of the parochial mindset of "tending their own plot of land" (守好一亩三分地) and focus only on honing individual combat skills, they will become the weak point in the systems combat chain.

Winning on the future battlefield requires all combat units to clench into a fist. This news item from a brigade of the 72nd Group Army reminds every officer and soldier: you must both cultivate your own duties and sharpen your own skills, and also embrace the system and keep the overall situation in mind. Only by actively integrating into the systems combat chain can you consolidate the foundation for victory and forge a truly iron fist.

Not Just "Able to Fly," but "Able to Fight"

■ Staff Sergeant First Class Zhao Kunjie, a Brigade of the 72nd Group Army

For a long time, I placed the center of gravity of my training on UAV flight control and equipment inspection and calibration. I always felt that as long as I mastered the operations, I could fly the UAV well.

The assessment not long ago gave me a profound jolt. On the assessment floor, I was single-mindedly focused on controlling the UAV's flight attitude and clearing up the reconnaissance feed, striving to make every step of my operation airtight. Yet precisely because I was too focused on my own "prescribed movements" (规定动作), I neither paid attention to manned-unmanned coordination nor considered the overall rhythm of the squad, and in the end I was not rated outstanding.

This assessment sounded an alarm for me, and made me realize that "able to fly" does not mean "able to fight." After identifying my own weak points, I shifted the center of gravity of my training to coordination and cooperation. Before each joint exercise begins, I communicate in advance with ground comrades from other specialties, align on the overall action procedures, and adjust my flight reconnaissance posture to match the operational rhythm; after training ends, I actively participate in after-action reviews, identify deficiencies, and work hard to close the coordination gap.

Dedicated effort yields results. Through one coordinated training session after another, I have gained a clearer understanding of the UAV's role in squad coordination and even in battalion-company-level confrontations. Beyond that, the process of joint exercises with comrades from other specialties has given me a more direct understanding of their tactical intent and combat rhythm. In the recent tactical assessment, it was precisely because of my seamless coordination with the ground element that we successfully seized the strike window and delivered a decisive blow to the opposing force.

This period of experience has taught me: you must not only "be able to fly," but "be able to fight." Every post is one link in systems combat — fighting alone cannot win a systems battle. Going forward, I will continue to integrate into systems training, solidly practice coordination and cooperation, and strive to forge myself into a reliable, capable link in the system.

(Compiled by Du Yijie)

第72集团军某旅深化有人无人协同训练—— “金牌飞手”重登“龙虎榜” ■简蕾 刘羲磊 解放军报特约通讯员 陆栋钰 该旅组织空地协同侦察训练。张乃友摄 夏日，第72集团军某旅一场无人机实战化考核如火如荼进行。考核现场，一级上士赵坤杰娴熟地操控无人机，顺利完成巡飞作业。 赵坤杰是单位响当当的“金牌飞手”，曾多次在上级比武中斩获名次。这次考核，他全程稳定发挥，基础课目全部拿到满分。然而，考核总成绩公示后，“优秀飞手”名单里竟然没有赵坤杰的名字。 “基础课目考核零失误，为啥评不上优秀？”几名年轻无人机飞手很是不解。 “单机飞得再熟练，那也只是基本技能过关。跟不上地面节奏、融不进战术行动，上了战场，根本发挥不了作用。”班长张海的一番话直击赵坤杰的短板。原来，赵坤杰虽然基础课目考核都是满分，但在空地协同、情报联动、战术配合等方面失分较多，综合成绩达不到优秀标准。 “‘金牌飞手’未能登上‘龙虎榜’，在这次考核中并不少见。”该旅作训科李参谋告诉笔者，过去无人机训练主要围绕单机单装操作展开，不同专业人员各练各的，协同配合比较欠缺。随着新域新质作战力量加速融入联合作战体系，今年以来，他们对标实战需求，将空地协同、人装联动等内容固化为平时训练“必修课”，大幅提高协同课目在考核中的分数权重。 对于训练内容的调整，不少骨干起初并没当回事。赵坤杰也有类似想法：在训练场摸爬滚打这些年，见识过不少作战环境与特情，靠着积累的“经验套路”足以应对考核。可他没有想到，这次考核，分数扣在了不起眼的协同细节上。 这次考核，让赵坤杰与战友们认清一个现实：手上功夫再拔尖，融不进体系就算不上过硬；单项能力再突出，跟不上战场变化，就打不了胜仗。 该旅领导介绍，为加强有人无人协同训练，推动官兵能力与战场需求相匹配，他们调整考核标准，引导大家摒弃“单打独斗”“各自为战”等陈旧观念，把体系协同的作战思维落实到日常训练。他们召集各专业骨干共同梳理协同难题，厘清空地配合流程，常态组织无人分队与地面班组同步训练、协同合练，临机设置强电磁干扰、通信中断等特情，倒逼大家在真打实抗中练强协同本领。 这次考核后，赵坤杰经历了一场蜕变——操控台前，他不再只盯着装备参数、飞行姿态，而是提前与地面分队战友对接战术意图，掌握战斗节奏；无人机升空，他紧盯战场态势，确保地面分队推进到哪里，空中支援就跟进到哪里。 “装备在升级，大纲在完善，战场也在不断更新。”该旅某营闵营长感慨，现代战争打的是体系、拼的是融合，光靠老经验走不远，仅凭单项优势打不赢，只有主动融入作战链条，着眼实战需求练兵，才能经得起战场检验。 前不久的一场战术考核中，赵坤杰作为主无人机飞手配合地面分队协同进攻。考核展开后，他操控无人机，实时回传战场信息，全程零延迟、零误差，圆满完成协同作战任务。考核总成绩公示，赵坤杰重登“龙虎榜”。 胸怀体系 融入体系 ■杨 悦 “不谋全局者，不足谋一域。”这句话并非指挥员专属，而是作战体系中每名官兵必须领悟的警言。现代战争早已告别单装单域较量，进入体系对抗阶段。实践反复证明：一环薄弱，便可能导致一域失守，乃至全盘被动。打赢未来战争，仅靠单个战位过硬远远不够，只有每个环节、每个岗位都牢固树立协同意识和体系思维，才能以局部过硬支撑全局制胜。 无论是空中巡弋的侦察无人机，还是前沿阵地上的突击单元，各要素环环相扣、紧密衔接。一个环节的衔接失当、一次协同的配合滞后，都会制约整体作战效能的发挥。如果官兵跳不出“守好一亩三分地”的本位思维，只顾锤炼单打独斗的作战本领，就会成为体系作战链条的薄弱点。 决胜未来战场，需要各作战单元攥指成拳。第72集团军某旅的这则新闻提醒每名官兵，既要深耕本职、练强本领，更要胸怀体系、心系全局，唯有主动融入体系作战链条，才能夯实胜战根基、锻造过硬铁拳。 既要“能飞”，更要“能战” ■第72集团军某旅一级上士 赵坤杰 一直以来，我把训练重心放在无人机飞行操控、设备检查调试上，总觉得只要把操作练娴熟就能飞好无人机。 不久前那场考核，给了我很大触动。考场上，我一门心思控制无人机飞行姿态、调清侦察画面，努力让每一步操作都滴水不漏。然而，正因为我过于关注自己的“规定动作”，既没有重视有人无人协同，也没有考虑班组整体节奏，最终考核未能获评优秀。 这次考核给我敲响了警钟，更让我意识到，“能飞”不代表“能战”。找准自身短板后，我将训练重心调整到协同配合上。每次合练展开前，我提前和地面其他专业战友沟通，对接整体行动流程，配合作战节奏调整飞行侦察状态；训练结束后，我主动参与复盘、查找不足，努力补齐协同短板。 功夫不负有心人。在一次次协同训练中，我对无人机在班组协同乃至营连对抗中的定位更加清晰。不仅如此，与其他专业战友合练的过程，让我对他们的战术意图、战斗节奏有了更直观的认识。前不久的战术考核，正是由于我跟地面分队配合默契，才顺利抢占打击窗口，给予对手致命一击。 这段时间的历练让我明白，既要“能飞”，更要“能战”。每个岗位都是体系作战中的一环，单打独斗打不赢体系作战。下一步，我要继续融入体系训练，扎实练好协同配合，努力把自己锻造成靠得住、顶得上的体系一环。 （杜亦杰整理）
