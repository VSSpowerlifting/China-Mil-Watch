Ordnance Soldiers in the Deep Mountains: Hearts Fixed on Sea and Sky, Chests Full of Thunder

深山中的雷弹兵：心系海天，胸怀雷霆
PLA Daily (解放军报) 30 June 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
The ammunition detachment of an unnamed Naval Aviation field station, stationed at a mountain garrison remote from the coast, is the subject of this feature, which profiles Staff Sergeant Third Class Liu Ye (19 years at the unit), Corporal Zou Jiawen, Lieutenant Zhang Yalun, Wang Biao, and Bao Chaoxuan as they execute ordnance technical preparation, transfer, and informatized quality management in support of multi-aircraft-type sorties with a 25-minute load window. The piece documents the detachment's ongoing push to compress ordnance preparation timelines and its recent fielding of a quality monitoring information management system — framing both under the concept of 向战转型 (transforming toward combat) — and records that Liu Ye's standardized loading procedures for a newly inducted aircraft type were subsequently adopted unit-wide. The framing points to institutional pressure on rear-echelon aviation ordnance units to absorb new equipment and accelerate sortie generation, though the specific aircraft types, ordnance types, and field station location remain undisclosed.

Far from the Sea, Close to the Warbirds

■ Ma Jing

These ordnance soldiers are far from the sea.

They hold their posts in little-known deep mountains, most of them not given to many words. They carry extraordinary stories, yet they are accustomed to understating them.

"Every post matters!" During the interviews, the ordnance soldiers often had this phrase on their lips. This reporter tried to understand their inner world, and as the interviews deepened, gradually came to understand: the steadfast watch kept below the mountains lifts the charge above; the sacrifice made offstage forges the brilliance in the clouds. Though they stand outside the spotlight, it is precisely because of their dedication that the warbirds spread their wings and soar again and again. The ordnance soldiers say that as long as they see the aircraft take off safely with the ordnance loaded, they feel every sacrifice has been worth it.

Staff Sergeant Third Class Liu Ye, an ordnance soldier, told this reporter that in his idle moments he likes to stand at a high point and gaze into the distance—gazing in the direction the warbirds have flown, gazing at the cooking smoke rising from nearby villages, gazing at the setting sun sinking behind the distant mountains. Year after year, he has lived these days.

In their spare time, the ordnance soldiers often hold "grass-cutting competitions." Liu Ye told this reporter with confidence that he is "the fastest grass-cutter here."

What Liu Ye calls a "grass-cutting competition" is simply everyone strapping on brush-cutters and tending the weeds in the garrison courtyard together, to see who can clear them fastest. In the sunlight, grass clippings drift into the air, and the smell of fresh-cut grass fills it. Liu Ye has been here for 19 years, and has been cutting the courtyard grass for 19 years.

Greatness is found in the ordinary; glory lies in perseverance. The ordnance soldiers take every matter seriously, large or small, because they hold firmly to these convictions: "The post is small, the responsibility is great"; "Far from the sea, close to the warbirds." Only when one generation of soldiers accelerates its pace can it drive the next generation to press forward. Look—through cold winters and hot summers, generation after generation of ordnance soldiers have kept watch here in relay, letting the mission shine through steadfastness and struggle.

The interview ended at dusk, and this reporter was about to leave by vehicle. The red sun hung low in the west; evening light filtered through gaps in the pine forest, scattering dappled shadows on the ground. The soldiers stood in a row at the garrison gate, waving farewell. As this reporter rolled down the car window, it suddenly became clear: they stand just like those green pines silently upright on the mountain, silently giving their youth to the barracks, using consummate and solid skill to complete every ordnance support mission, helping the warbirds sail safely into the distance.

Glory never belongs exclusively to great figures, great moments, or grand narratives. In the silence between mountains and sea, the ordnance soldiers' song of steadfastness and struggle never ceases. As the vehicle slowly pulled away from the garrison, this reporter looked back at the rugged mountain road and suddenly realized—this road is the ordnance soldiers' road of youth, their road of growth, their road of struggle, and their road of dreams.

Ordnance Soldiers: Hearts Fixed on Sea and Sky, Chests Full of Thunder

■ Ma Jing, Sun Chao

*Upon receiving the order, ordnance soldiers deploy urgently. Photo by Ma Jing*

A square garrison courtyard sits at the foot of a mountain.

This is the station of the ammunition detachment of a Naval Aviation field station. Inside the courtyard, a group of young ordnance soldiers stand their post. Storage, maintenance, transfer—each piece of ordnance, after technical preparation, is delivered by their hands to beneath the wings, ready at any moment to head out over sea and sky. The ordnance soldiers' stage is far from the sea, but they lift the warbirds into flight in their own way. In the world of the ordnance soldier, the condition of a single piece of ordnance connects at one end to the wings of the warbird and at the other to the peace of the sea and sky.

Hearts fixed on sea and sky, chests full of thunder. When the sharp sword is unsheathed, that howl, that roar, is the ordnance soldiers' silent oath—the echo of their original aspiration (初心).

Between Sowing and Harvest Lies a Road Called "Perseverance"

"The night at the naval port is so still; the waves rock the warships gently; young sailors rest their heads on the swells, smiling sweetly in their dreams…" The song "Night at the Naval Port" (军港之夜) has ignited the naval dreams of countless ambitious young people.

Back then, 18-year-old Liu Ye came to the military carrying a naval dream, never expecting to put down roots at the foot of this mountain—and to stay for 19 years. When he first arrived, feelings of loneliness and boredom crept over his young heart like ants, again and again.

But at some point he can no longer recall, Liu Ye gradually came to love this place. Time passed; wrinkles crept to the corners of his eyes, yet his gaze grew ever more resolute.

Ordnance soldiers deal with weapons and ammunition year-round. The risk factor is high; there is no room for the slightest error.

One summer, during ordnance technical preparation, an operator was securing the tail fin of a piece of ordnance when a small screw suddenly stripped. Even though the ordnance had already passed earlier inspections, Liu Ye's heart lurched—that stripped screw was an unknown variable.

Liu Ye and his comrades reported the situation up the chain while preparing to replace the screw. But the screw was in an awkward position, and after searching the entire tool room they could not find the right extraction tool.

In the urgency of the moment, Liu Ye and his comrades picked up a short-handled screwdriver, placed a thin sheet of high-friction paper against the screw head, and pressed the screwdriver with the palm of the hand to turn it. At last, they extracted the stripped screw and replaced it with a new one.

They had followed the operating manual precisely—so why had the screw stripped? After repeated investigation, Liu Ye identified the problem: the operating instructions in the manual assumed that the operator was already proficient with the equipment. If an operator lacked proficiency and could not control the torque properly, stripping was entirely possible. Liu Ye reported the situation to higher authority; soon afterward, the manufacturer combined this feedback with practical conditions and optimized the relevant manufacturing process for that type of equipment.

"The longer I serve, the more cautious I become," Liu Ye admitted frankly. The moment he steps to his station, he tenses up immediately, throwing himself entirely into the inspection and operation, afraid of missing even the most subtle hidden hazard.

Last winter, Liu Ye, as a backbone member of a mission detachment, was dispatched by higher authority to carry out an ordnance support mission at a remote location. He and his comrades set out from the foot of the mountain and, after a circuitous journey, finally reached the mission airfield.

On the first day after arriving, standing at the edge of the airfield, Liu Ye was struck by the scene before him: on the long runway, various types of aircraft were either taking off toward training airspace or returning from training to land. These were sights Liu Ye had never seen before.

He then walked into the ordnance technical preparation room. The informatized equipment had a strong sense of technological sophistication, and Liu Ye exclaimed repeatedly, "This is incredible."

His eyes could not take in enough of the novelty; waves of feeling surged in his heart. That night, he lay in bed tossing and turning, with a persistent sense of unreality, as scenes from years past floated before him.

That year, Liu Ye had been executing an ordnance escort mission. The early spring nights were still cold; Liu Ye and his comrades pitched their tent inside a railway car and slept beside the ordnance packing crates. The car was stifling, and they could not sleep at night. In the end, Liu Ye and his comrades spent more than ten days on that escort route before successfully completing the mission.

From the crude, sweltering railway car to the technologically sophisticated ordnance technical preparation room, Liu Ye felt the rapid development of the force over these years, and along the way harvested the fruits of his own growth.

Nineteen years in uniform, he has always believed that between sowing and harvest lies a road called "perseverance"—

Through winters and summers, he led the mission detachment to innovate and optimize the emergency ordnance support model, effectively shortening ammunition loading preparation time.

Through springs and autumns, he led the mission detachment to explore and develop a refined support and training organization model that integrates training with combat, and tactics with technique.

Time and again, ordnance was safely transferred; time and again, the warbirds were seen safely aloft. And all the while, a voice grew ever clearer in Liu Ye's heart: the small patch of ground at the foot of the mountain is the destination worth pursuing with everything he has.

Sprint with Full Force to Leave Rivals Behind, and Stride Forward to Surpass Yourself

Corporal Zou Jiawen is a directly recruited non-commissioned officer. On the day he first arrived at the ammunition detachment, Zou Jiawen looked over the bright-yellow three-story building before him, and a flicker of disappointment crossed his eyes.

"What is the meaning of being here every day?" In his free time, Zou Jiawen would often sit in the courtyard gazing at the rolling mountains, waiting for a warbird to pass over the garrison, then listening as the roar of its engines gradually faded into the distance.

One day, Zou Jiawen called a comrade who had enlisted in the same cohort. On the other end of the line, that comrade—serving in a submarine unit—was at that moment writing a "last letter" (遗书) because he was about to execute an urgent mission. A ripple broke across the calm surface of Zou Jiawen's heart: "If it were me, what would I do?"

At the time, Zou Jiawen had no answer to that question.

During the National Day holiday, the detachment organized officers and soldiers to climb the mountain together. Zou Jiawen puffed and panted his way to the front of the group. Sweat ran down his forehead; he wiped it away with his hand and kept charging toward the summit.

No one knew why the quiet Zou Jiawen was so determined to be first. At that moment, only one thought filled his mind: "Climb to the top and see what lies on the other side of the mountain."

Reaching the summit, the view opened up before him. Liu Ye pointed into the distance and said: "Jiawen, that over there is our airfield. Our ordnance is loaded there, then flies with the warbirds out over the sea and sky."

Gazing in the direction of the airfield, Zou Jiawen was silent for a long moment before saying: "Detachment leader, next time you go to the airfield for a support mission, can you take me along?"

The green pines on the mountain still stand tall; the sailor at the mountain's foot has quietly grown. Today, Zou Jiawen can not only complete ordnance technical support missions at his own level, but also regularly accompanies the mission detachment to execute missions hundreds of kilometers away. "These past two years, sometimes I feel time passes very slowly, and sometimes I feel it flies by," Zou Jiawen said. "During ordnance technical preparation, if I'm slow by one small step, the steps that follow could be slow by a large one. I have to fight for every second."

Last winter, Zou Jiawen accompanied the mission detachment to a naval port to execute an ordnance transfer mission.

The ordnance transport vehicle drove along the asphalt road of the naval port, and Zou Jiawen pressed his face against the window, transfixed by the warships moored alongside. It was a sight he had never seen before—so much so that even as the ordnance transport vehicle drove into the testing hall, he was still craning his neck to look back at the warships, now half-hidden from view.

Not long afterward, Zou Jiawen received notice that the unit was about to organize a certain exercise mission. What excited him was that his mission detachment, as one link in the support chain, appeared on the list of participating units.

During the mission briefing, listening to the commanding officer's resounding words, a surge of heat rolled through Zou Jiawen's chest. In that moment, he felt like a warrior on the eve of battle, waiting only for the order to be given.

In the struggle of day after day, the ordnance soldiers are also continuously coming to understand anew the posts they hold.

Lieutenant Zhang Yalun is a directly recruited officer. Three years ago, upon learning there was an opportunity to join the "Blue Formation" (蓝色方阵), he and his university class leader both applied. In the end, Zhang Yalun came to this ordnance support unit at the foot of the mountain.

"If war comes tomorrow!" This is the warning Zhang Yalun wrote on the inside cover of his notebook. The large exclamation mark reflects the sense of urgency in his heart—that time waits for no one. "No matter what post you hold, you must fulfill your due responsibilities and be prepared to fight," he said.

Due responsibilities are forged through ten thousand repetitions of tempering, through the relentless pursuit of excellence.

Excellence in the details—in daily training, Zhang Yalun led the transfer detachment to streamline and optimize the deployment procedure; in pursuit of being "just a bit faster," they refined their movements to the step and broke down the timing to the second.

Excellence in speed—in remote support missions, Zhang Yalun led the mission detachment to repeatedly refine the emergency deployment process, cutting ordnance technical preparation time again and again.

"Raising an exam score from 60 to 80 may be relatively easy, but raising it from 90 to 100 is a different order of difficulty," Zhang Yalun said. An outstanding soldier must sprint with full force to leave rivals behind, and stride forward to surpass himself.

The More You Train, the More Your Potential Becomes Confidence; With Confidence, No Difficulty on the Road Ahead Can Stop You

*Ordnance soldiers record measurement data. Photo by Ma Jing*

On the airfield, several warbirds stand in neat formation, ready to take off at any moment.

In the ordnance technical preparation room at one corner of the airfield, ordnance soldier Wang Biao is carrying out ordnance technical preparation work. Speaking of the new changes that have emerged in training over the past two years, Wang Biao has one vivid impression: "The preparation time left for us ordnance soldiers is getting shorter and shorter."

Opening crates for inspection, loading ammunition, signal testing—at this moment, the ordnance soldiers have just completed technical inspection and accessory mounting, and are preparing to move the ordnance out of storage. Wang Biao operates a forklift in precise coordination with the commander, steadily transferring the ordnance packing crates onto the transport vehicle.

The ordnance transport vehicle then arrives at the apron and pulls up to the loading position in front of the aircraft. Wang Biao uses the forklift to place the ordnance at the designated position and hands it off to the aircraft maintenance soldiers.

Immediately afterward, Wang Biao reaches into the breast pocket of his combat uniform, pulls out a stopwatch, and presses pause. Looking at the number on the stopwatch, he curls the corners of his mouth slightly upward and reports to the commander: "This ordnance transfer took five minutes less than last time!"

Over the years, "running the stopwatch" has become a training habit for Wang Biao and his comrades. The moment he receives the transfer order and steps into the ordnance technical preparation room, Wang Biao starts the stopwatch.

"Time is most generous—it gives everyone 24 hours; time is also most miserly—it gives no one more than 24 hours." Wang Biao is fond of this saying. He firmly believes that "diligence can compensate for lack of talent" (勤能补拙), and he tells younger comrades: "If you're not fast enough, practice it again and again."

Recently, the mission detachment Wang Biao belongs to voluntarily raised the pressure on itself and organized nighttime emergency ordnance support training. During the training, Wang Biao and his comrades coordinated with high efficiency and ultimately succeeded in significantly shortening nighttime ordnance transfer preparation time. Wang Biao said: "Aircraft flight preparation time is getting shorter and shorter today, and that requires us ordnance soldiers to accelerate our own transformation."

One ordnance soldier's view of transformation reflects that the concept of transforming toward combat (向战转型) has already taken deep root in the hearts of the soldiers. In recent years, the detachment has comprehensively launched ordnance management data collection and collation work, building a supporting database to lay the data foundation for achieving informatized ammunition management, and has substantially raised the level of weapons and equipment management and support.

Sergeant Bao Chaoxuan deals with data almost every day. Last year, the detachment was equipped with a quality monitoring information management system, and from that point on, Bao Chaoxuan became the ordnance's "information steward."

"I always feel like there isn't enough time!" Following Bao Chaoxuan's hurried footsteps, this reporter walked into the detachment's quality control room.

It is not large—a room of more than 40 square meters, holding four computers and a rectangular conference table.

It is not small either—a small screen, a view of the whole picture. Open the information management system, click the mouse, and ordnance information from hundreds of kilometers away is immediately laid out at a glance.

"This place is like an information relay station—all ordnance information converges here, and is then distributed and processed," Bao Chaoxuan said.

The telephone in the quality control room rings frequently. Bao Chaoxuan brings the receiver to his ear, then tucks it against his left shoulder, his fingers flying across the keyboard—entering information, or looking up data. The sense of responsibility and mission toward his post drives Bao Chaoxuan to keep winding his own "mainspring" (发条): vast quantities of ordnance information need to be mastered, so he takes the time to categorize and memorize it; when he encounters unfamiliar situations, he goes to the relevant specialist departments to ask for guidance.

A truly strong person is never all-powerful—only able to do their utmost. He tells newly arrived comrades in the detachment: "The more you train, the more your potential becomes confidence; with confidence, no difficulty on the road ahead can stop you."

In the Words of a Witness

"One Second Faster in Ordnance Preparation, One Point More of Battlefield Advantage"

■ Staff Sergeant Third Class Liu Ye

Speed can be described as "fast" or "slow." If the speed of ordnance transport and support were placed in a dialectical relationship with each other, what would that look like?

Not long ago, I participated in an ordnance support mission. As usual, I coordinated with the aircraft maintenance officers and soldiers according to the prescribed procedures, rapidly completing ordnance preparation. Only when I heard the flight control tower transmit the order "weapons successfully released" did the furrow in my brow finally ease.

For this ordnance support mission, from receiving the order to completing ordnance loading, my comrades and I had only a brief 25-minute window. Over the past two years, as multi-aircraft-type support missions have become routine, this kind of "rush job" has become our daily bread.

A light breeze blew; the aircraft, loaded with ordnance, roared into the sky. Gazing in the direction the aircraft had gone, I thought about how to further strengthen coordination with the aircraft maintenance officers and soldiers in the next step, to make the ordnance loading speed just a bit faster.

The speed of ordnance loading is directly tied to the speed at which aircraft can sortie. After years of striving at the ordnance support post, I have my own understanding of the word "speed": "One second faster in ordnance preparation, one point more of battlefield advantage."

That year, as a new type of aircraft was inducted into the unit, we took on a new type of ordnance support mission.

Facing new missions and new equipment, everyone kept winding their own "mainspring." How to complete ordnance loading at greater speed while ensuring safety was a question we thought about constantly. Through in-depth research, we placed our breakthrough focus on the installation of air-drop accessories.

The installation of air-drop accessories is directly related to whether the aircraft can stably and safely carry the ordnance, and it is a severe test of the ordnance soldier's technical level. In the end, my comrades and I, targeting the new ordnance support requirements, sorted out and summarized a set of ordnance operating standards and standard loading procedures for different environments. Later, this set of standards and procedures was promoted and applied across the unit, raising the speed of ordnance loading.

Today, I have become an ordnance support technician capable of supporting multiple aircraft types, multiple ordnance types, and multiple equipment types. Looking to the future, I still hold firmly to that saying: "One second faster in ordnance preparation, one point more of battlefield advantage!"

离大海很远，离战鹰很近 ■马 静 这群雷弹兵距离大海很远。 他们坚守在鲜为人知的深山中，大多不善言辞。他们身上有很多不凡的故事，却习惯了轻描淡写。 “每个岗位都很重要！”采访期间，雷弹兵们常常把这句话挂在嘴边。笔者试图解读他们的内心世界，也随着采访的深入渐渐明白：山下的坚守，托举天上的冲锋；幕后的奉献，铸就云端的精彩。虽然他们不在聚光灯下，但正是因为有了他们的付出，才有了战鹰一次次展翅高飞。雷弹兵们说，只要看到战机挂载雷弹平安起飞，就觉得一切付出都是值得的。 采访结束，已是傍晚，笔者即将乘车离开。红日西垂，夕阳透过松林间的缝隙，在地面洒下斑驳的光影。战士们在营院门口站成一排，和笔者挥手告别。笔者摇下车窗，忽然发现，他们正如山上那些默默挺立的青松，默默把青春献给军营，用精湛过硬的技术完成好每一次雷弹保障任务，助力战机平安远航。 光荣从不专属于大人物、大时刻与大叙事。山海无声处，雷弹兵们坚守与奋斗的弦歌不辍。汽车缓慢驶离营区，笔者回头看向这条崎岖的山路，忽然意识到——这条路，是雷弹兵们的青春之路、成长之路，也是雷弹兵们的奋斗之路、梦想之路。 雷弹兵：心系海天，胸怀雷霆 ■马 静 孙 超 收到指令后，雷弹兵紧急出动。马 静摄 一座四方的营院，坐落在山脚下。 这里，是海军航空兵某场站弹药队的驻地。营院里，一群年轻的雷弹兵驻守于此。储存、维护、转运……一枚枚雷弹经过技术准备，被他们送到机翼之下，等待随时奔赴海空。雷弹兵们的舞台离大海很远，但他们却以另一种姿态托举战鹰起飞。在雷弹兵的世界里，一枚雷弹的状态，一头连着战鹰的翅膀，一头连着海空的安宁。 心系海天，胸怀雷霆。当利剑出鞘，那呼啸、那轰鸣，正是雷弹兵无声的誓言、初心的回响。 播种和收获中间隔着一段名叫“坚持”的路 “军港的夜啊静悄悄，海浪把战舰轻轻地摇，年轻的水兵头枕着波涛，睡梦中露出甜美的微笑……”一首《军港之夜》不知点燃了多少有志青年的海军梦。 当年，18岁的刘野怀揣海军梦来到部队，没想到在这山脚下扎了根，还一干就是19年。刚来到这里时，寂寞、无聊的情绪像一只只蚂蚁，时不时地爬过他年轻的心。 可不记得从哪天起，刘野慢慢喜欢上了这里。时光流逝，皱纹爬上了眼角，他的眼神却日益坚定。 雷弹兵常年与武器弹药打交道，危险系数高，容不得半点差错。 那年夏天，在进行雷弹技术准备过程中，操作员加固雷弹尾翼部位时，一枚细小的螺钉突然出现滑丝。即便雷弹已经过前期检测，刘野心里还是“咯噔”了一下——这枚出现滑丝的螺钉，就是不确定因素。 刘野和战友一边上报情况，一边做好更换螺钉的准备。然而，这枚螺钉位置特殊，他们找遍了工具间，都没有找到合适的取螺钉工具。 情急之下，刘野和战友拿起短柄螺丝刀，再垫上一张摩擦力较大的薄纸，直接用手掌顶着螺丝刀转动。终于，他们将出现滑丝的螺钉取下，更换了新螺钉。 明明按照操作说明书操作，为何还会出现滑丝？反复研究后，刘野发现了问题所在——说明书上的操作说明，是建立在操作人员能熟练操作设备的基础上，如果操作人员技术不熟练，控制不好力度，就很有可能出现螺钉滑丝的情况。刘野向上级上报情况，很快，厂家结合实际优化了该型设备的相关工艺。 “兵龄越来越长，胆子却变得越来越小。”刘野坦言，只要一到战位，自己就会立刻紧张起来，全身心投入到检测操作中，生怕未察觉到细微的隐患。 去年冬天，刘野作为任务分队骨干，被上级派去执行异地雷弹保障任务。他和战友从山脚下出发，几经辗转终于抵达任务机场。 进驻第1天，站在机场边上，刘野被眼前的场景震撼：长长的跑道上，各型战机或是起飞奔赴训练空域，或是结束训练归航降落……这些都是刘野未曾见过的景象。 他又走进雷弹技术准备间，信息化设备科技感十足，刘野连连赞叹“太高级了”。 眼睛里有看不完的新奇，心中的波澜也随之层层推开。那天晚上，他躺在床上辗转反侧，总有一种“不真实”的感觉，多年前的场景浮现在眼前…… 那年，刘野执行雷弹押运任务。初春的夜晚依旧寒冷，刘野和战友们把帐篷搭在火车车厢里，睡在雷弹包装箱旁。火车车厢十分闷热，他们晚上根本睡不着。最终，这条押运之路，刘野和战友们走了10多天，顺利完成了雷弹的押运。 从简陋闷热的火车车厢，到科技感十足的雷弹技术准备间，刘野感受到了这些年部队的飞速发展，也一路收获着自己成长的果实。 当兵19年，他始终坚信，播种和收获中间隔着一段名叫“坚持”的路—— 他在寒来暑往中穿梭，带领任务分队创新优化雷弹应急保障模式，有效缩短了弹药装载准备时间。 他在春去秋来中坚守，带领任务分队探索形成训练与实战、战术与技术相结合的精细化保障组训模式。 一次次平安转运雷弹，一次次守护战鹰起飞。与此同时，一种声音也在刘野心中越来越清晰：山脚下这片方寸天地，就是他值得全力奔赴的远方。 既要全力冲刺甩开对手，也要大步流星超越自己 下士邹家文是一名直招军士。初来弹药队那天，邹家文打量着面前这幢明黄色的3层小楼，眼睛里闪过一丝失落。 “每天待在这里的意义是什么呢？”闲暇时，邹家文常常坐在院子里眺望连绵起伏的群山，等待着战机从营院上空飞过，又听着战机的轰鸣声渐渐远去…… 那天，邹家文拨通了同批入伍战友的电话。电话那头，这名服役于潜艇部队的战友，彼时正因即将执行紧急任务写下“遗书”。邹家文平静的心湖中突然泛起涟漪——“如果换作是我，我会怎么做？” 这个问题，邹家文当时并没有答案。 国庆假期时，队里组织官兵一同登山。邹家文呼哧呼哧喘着气，跑在最前面。汗水沿着额头流下，他用手擦了一把汗，继续向山顶冲锋。 没有人知道，不爱说话的邹家文为什么非要冲在最前面。而彼时的邹家文脑海中只有一个想法，“爬到山顶，看看山的那边是什么”。 登上山顶，眼前的景色豁然开朗。刘野指着远处说：“家文，你看那边就是我们的机场。咱们的雷弹就是在那里挂载，然后跟随战机飞向海空。” 看着机场的方向，邹家文沉默良久后说：“分队长，下次去机场保障，能不能带上我？” 山上的青松依然挺立，山下的水兵悄然成长。如今，邹家文不仅可以完成本级雷弹技术保障任务，还常常跟随任务分队奔赴数百公里外执行任务。“这两年，有时觉得时间过得很慢，有时又觉得时光飞逝。”邹家文说，“在进行雷弹技术准备时，如果我这里慢一小步，后边的流程就可能慢一大步，我必须分秒必争。” 去年冬天，邹家文跟随任务分队前往某军港执行转运雷弹任务。 雷弹运输车行驶在军港的柏油路上，邹家文趴在车窗玻璃上，看着靠泊的战舰入了神。 这是邹家文从未见过的景象，以至于雷弹运输车驶入测试大厅时，他依然回头看着若隐若现的战舰…… 不久后，邹家文接到通知——部队即将组织某演习任务。令他兴奋的是，他所在的任务分队，作为保障链条上的一环，出现在了参演名单中。 任务部署时，听着带队领导掷地有声的话语，邹家文心中翻滚着一股热浪。那一刻，他感觉自己像是即将出征的勇士，只待一声令下。 在日复一日的奋斗中，雷弹兵们也在不断重新认识自己坚守的战位。 中尉张亚伦是一名直招军官。3年前，得知有机会加入“蓝色方阵”，他和大学班长一起报了名。最终，张亚伦来到了山脚下这个雷弹保障单位。 “如果明天就打仗！”这是张亚伦写在笔记本扉页上的警示语。一个大大的感叹号，折射出他心中时不我待的紧迫感。“不管身处哪个岗位，都必须担好应尽之责、做好打仗准备。”他说。 应尽之责，凝结在千万次的磨砺中，凝结在精益求精的执着追求中。 精在细微——日常训练中，张亚伦带领转运分队精简优化出动程序，为了“再快一点”，他们精确到步、细化到秒。 精在速度——异地保障中，张亚伦带领任务分队反复打磨应急出动流程，雷弹技术准备用时被一次次缩短。 “考试成绩从60分提高到80分或许容易，但要从90分提高到100分，难度就不一样了。”张亚伦说，一名优秀的军人，既要全力冲刺甩开对手，也要大步流星超越自己。 练得多，身上的潜力就会变为底气；有了底气，前进路上便不惧困难 雷弹兵记录测量数据。马 静摄 机场上，数架战机整齐列阵，随时做好升空准备。 机场一隅的雷弹技术准备间中，雷弹兵王彪正在进行雷弹技术准备工作。谈及这两年训练中出现的新变化，王彪有一个鲜明的感受：“留给我们雷弹兵的准备时间越来越短。” 开箱检查、弹药挂载、信号测试……此刻，雷弹兵们刚刚完成技术检测和附件挂载，准备雷弹出库。王彪驾驶叉车与指挥员精准配合，将雷弹包装箱稳稳转运到运输车上。 随后，雷弹运输车抵达停机坪，停靠在战机前的挂弹位置。王彪用叉车将雷弹放于指定位置，与机务兵进行交接。 紧接着，王彪从作训服上衣口袋中掏出秒表，按下了暂停键。看着秒表上的数字，他微微上扬嘴角，向指挥员汇报：“这次转运雷弹，用时比上次缩短了5分钟！” 这些年，“卡秒表”已成为王彪和战友们的训练习惯。接到转运命令，在进入雷弹技术准备间的那一刻，王彪就会按下秒表的计时键。 “时间最慷慨，给任何人都是24小时；时间也最吝啬，给任何人都不是24小时。”王彪很喜欢这句话，他坚信“勤能补拙”，他还告诉年轻战友：“如果不够快，那就反复练。” 前段时间，王彪所在任务分队主动加压，组织夜间雷弹应急保障训练。训练中，王彪和战友们高效协同，最终有效缩短了夜间雷弹转运准备时间。王彪说：“如今战机的飞行准备时间越来越短，这要求我们雷弹兵也要加快转型。” 一名雷弹兵的转型观，折射出向战转型的观念早已深入兵心。近年来，该队全面展开雷弹管理数据梳理采集工作，建设配套数据库，为实现弹药管理信息化奠定数据基础，大幅提升了武器装备管理保障水平。 中士鲍潮煊几乎每天都要和数据“打交道”。去年，该队配备了质量监控信息管理系统，自此，鲍潮煊成了雷弹的“信息管家”。 “总感觉时间不够用！”跟着鲍潮煊急匆匆的脚步，笔者走进了该队的质量控制室。 这里说大不大——40多平方米的房间中，摆放着4台电脑和一个长方形的会议桌。 这里说小不小——方寸屏幕，纵观全局。打开信息管理系统，只需轻点鼠标，远在数百公里外的雷弹信息就一目了然。 “这里像是一个信息中转站，所有雷弹信息都在这里汇集，然后再进行分发处理。”鲍潮煊说。 质量控制室的电话铃声频繁响起，鲍潮煊将听筒靠近耳朵，然后夹在左肩，手指在键盘上快速跳动，或是录入信息，或是查阅数据……对战位的责任感和使命感，催着鲍潮煊不断给自己上紧“发条”：大量雷弹信息需要掌握，他就花时间去分类记忆；遇到陌生情况，他就去相关专业部门请教…… 真正的强者从来不是无所不能，而是尽我所能。他对队里新来的战友说：“练得多，身上的潜力就会变为底气；有了底气，前进路上便不惧困难。” 亲历者说 “雷弹准备快一秒，战场胜算多一分” ■三级军士长 刘 野 速度，可以用“快”与“慢”来形容。如果将速度的快慢，与雷弹的运输保障联系起来，将会是怎样的辩证关系？ 前不久，我参与执行雷弹保障任务。像往常一样，我按照规定流程与机务官兵配合，快速完成雷弹准备工作。直到听到飞行塔台传来“武器顺利投放”的指令，我紧锁的眉头终于得以舒展。 此次雷弹保障任务，从受领任务到完成雷弹挂载，我和战友们只有短短25分钟的处置时间。这两年，随着多机型保障任务常态化展开，这种“急活”对我们来说，已是“家常便饭”。 微风轻拂，战机挂载着雷弹呼啸升空。望向战机远去的方向，我思考着下一步如何加强与机务官兵的配合，让挂载雷弹的速度再快一些。 挂载雷弹速度的快慢，关系着战机出动的速度。在雷弹保障岗位奋斗多年，我对“速度”这两个字有自己的理解——“雷弹准备快一秒，战场胜算多一分”。 那年，伴随新型战机列装部队，我们迎来了新型雷弹保障任务。 面对新任务、新装备，大家不断给自己上紧“发条”。如何在确保安全的前提下，以更快的速度完成雷弹挂载，是我们一直思考的课题。通过深入研究，我们把攻关重点放在空投附件的安装上。 空投附件的安装，关系到战机能否稳定安全地挂载雷弹，非常考验雷弹兵的技术水平。最终，我和战友瞄准新型雷弹保障要求，梳理总结出一套不同环境下雷弹操作规范和标准挂载程序。后来，这套规范和程序在单位推广运用，提高了挂载雷弹的速度。 如今，我已成为保障多机型、多弹种、多设备的雷弹保障技师。面向未来，我还是坚信那句话：“雷弹准备快一秒，战场胜算多一分！” （马静整理）
