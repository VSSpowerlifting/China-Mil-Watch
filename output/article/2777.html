The piece is standard PLA political-work content of the 'model scientist-soldier' genre; its value is as a record of how official military media frames perseverance in defense research under the 强国强军 (strong nation, strong military) rubric, not as evidence of any specific program or capability. Wang Ji's research focus is not named, so the article documents the institutional messaging pattern without revealing what work is being valorized.

Reporter's Notes: The Silent Roar

■ China Military Net Reporter Zhang Yimiao

As a military reporter, I am accustomed to capturing sound. The roar of armored vehicles grinding across the Gobi, the shriek of fighter jets tearing through cloud cover, the battle cries of youthful formations on the training ground… For a long time, I firmly believed that heroism was thunderous and earth-shaking.

Carrying that assumption, I set off for Changsha to interview Wang Ji, a researcher at the National University of Defense Technology. On the high-speed train, I read through Professor Wang Ji's biographical materials and saw a man whose time had been cleaved brutally in two by fate—and then squeezed ever tighter. I naturally prepared a narrative framework: a story about suffering and struggle.

But once I was actually sitting across from Wang Ji, the questions I had prepared in advance felt somewhat out of place.

He wheeled himself in—light, without much sound. Although the brown marks left by years of dialysis on his arms were plainly visible, the first words out of his mouth were easy in tone: "Just finished dialysis yesterday; feeling alright today." When I steeled myself and raised the subject of "suffering," he said: "Suffering comes from choice. When a person has no choice, there is actually no suffering." No sentimentality, no tragic grandeur, not the slightest trace of forcing himself to hold up.

Wang Ji is "silent" (寂静). This silence is not numbness; it is a steadiness held firmly aloft by love. It is not that he has no choice—it is that he does not want to easily abandon his original choice just because of illness. He says his second life was won back for him by everyone around him: "Whatever can be done must be done without delay." What others regard as the heaviest of burdens, he faces and shoulders in the most unhurried way.

Yet behind this "lightness" lies an unimaginable "weight." One hundred and forty-seven days in intensive care, more than ten surgeries, over seven hundred dialysis sessions—none of it stopped Wang Ji from moving in a triangle between the ward, the laboratory, and the classroom, pressing on with his research. Colleagues say he is "the least worry and the most worry"—"least worry" because he never complains about the injustice of fate; "most worry" because he is burning himself up, racing against every second.

On the high-speed train home, I recalled a small story from Wang Ji's childhood that had come up during the interview: his mother made a pact with him that he could only go out to play after getting every math problem right. Other children found the "water tank filling and draining at the same time" type of problem baffling; he loved peeling back the conditions layer by layer until he found the answer.

Watching mountains, rivers, fields, and villages flash past the train window, I suddenly felt that this detail—which I had not paid much attention to during the interview—now carried a deeper meaning: a person's life holds many unknowns; it is a matter of solving one problem after another. In his youth Wang Ji solved math exercises; as a young man he solved academic research problems; in middle age he has been solving the life problem that fate suddenly hurled at him—the kind no one wants to catch. And the hardest problem of all, he solved using the most familiar method: peel it back layer by layer, find the key, fix the direction, then keep moving forward with the original aspiration unchanged.

The engine of the charge roars on ceaselessly in the mind, surging with power—this is the bedrock of a soldier, a point man. No matter how difficult, bitter, or dangerous, for the sake of a strong nation and a strong military (强国强军), one must resolutely charge forward and steadfastly press on. Wang Ji has compressed what others would call earth-shaking feats into each ordinary day, making the "silence" no longer soundless.

The deepest strength is often hidden in the most tranquil heart, burning like subterranean fire.

记者手记：寂静的轰鸣 ■中国军网记者 张艺藐 作为一名军事记者，我习惯捕捉声音。战车碾过戈壁的轰鸣，战机撕裂云层的尖啸，演训场上青春方阵的呐喊……长久以来，我笃信英雄主义是轰轰烈烈的。 带着这种预设，我赶往长沙，去采访国防科技大学研究员王戟。高铁上，我翻阅王戟教授的事迹材料，看见一个人所拥有的时间被命运硬生生一劈两半，而且吝啬地越收越紧。我理所当然地准备了一个叙事框架：一个关于苦难与抗争的故事。 但真正坐到王戟面前，提前准备好的问题显得有些不合时宜。 他摇着轮椅进来，轻快，没有多大声响。尽管手臂上长期透析留下的褐色痕迹清晰可见，他开口的第一句话，却语气轻松：“昨天刚做完透析，今天状态还行。”我硬着头皮提起“苦难”，他说：“痛苦来源于选择。当一个人没有选择的时候，反而就不痛苦了。”没有煽情，没有悲壮，也没有一丝强撑的痕迹。 王戟是“寂静”的。这份寂静并非麻木，而是一种被爱稳稳托举着的笃定。他不是没有选择，而是他不想因为病痛就轻易改变最初的选择。他说自己的第二次生命是大家帮忙抢回来的，“能做的事，要抓紧做”。在旁人看来最沉重的东西，他用最轻松的方式面对并担负了起来。 可这份“轻”的背后，是难以想象的“重”。147天重症监护，10余次手术，700多次透析，都挡不住王戟从病房到实验室再到教室，三点一线，继续攻关。同事说他“最让人省心，也最让人不放心”——“省心”是因为他从不抱怨什么命运不公，“不放心”是因为他争分夺秒在燃烧自己。 返程的高铁上，我想起采访中听到的王戟的一件童年小事：母亲和王戟约定，数学题全做对了才能出去玩。别的小朋友觉得“水池一边进水一边放水”的题目绕人，他却喜欢把条件一层层拆开，直到找到答案。 看着车窗外一闪而过的山川河流、田野村庄，我突然觉得，采访时并未在意的这个细节，此时有了更深的蕴味：人的一生有许多未知，就是在解一道又一道题。王戟少年时解数学习题，青年时解学术课题，中年之后解的是命运突然抛来的、没人想接的人生难题。而他解最难的那道，也用了最熟悉的方式——一层层拆开，找出关键，确定方向，然后初心不改，继续往前走。 冲锋的引擎始终在头脑中轰鸣，动力澎湃——这是一个军人、一名尖兵的底色。即使再难再苦再险，为了强国强军，也要坚决冲上去，坚定走下去。王戟将常人眼中的惊天动地，压缩进了每一个寻常日子，让“寂静”不再无声。 最深沉的力量，往往藏在最宁静的心田，像地火一样燃烧。
