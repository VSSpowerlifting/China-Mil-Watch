The Radar Station Has a "Mountain Path"

雷达站有条“上山路”
PLA Daily (解放军报) 21 May 2026
View original source ↗
About this analysis
Summary
A Central Theater Command Air Force radar station at roughly 1,700 meters elevation — designated a pilot unit earlier this year — moved its duty positions from the summit down to a mid-slope command room, cutting response times and reducing daily summit traffic to antenna maintenance and routine inspections; the 399-step stone stairway hand-chiseled by the garrison over decades remains deliberately maintained as a physical and institutional artifact. The editor's note frames the core tension explicitly: as equipment upgrades and data-link optimization improve conditions at PLA grassroots units (基层), how does the institution sustain what it calls the 'charging posture of struggle' when the hardship that historically produced that posture is engineered away? The piece documents a recurring PLA political-work problem — preserving unit ethos through material transition — and its value is as a record of how the institution uses local tradition-building and physical heritage to answer that problem at the station level, not as evidence of a broader doctrinal or posture shift.
Translation

The Radar Station Has a "Mountain Path"

■ PLA Daily Reporter Liu Min Illustrated by Zeng Yuming

Winding up along a rugged mountain road, after more than an hour the vehicle came to a slow stop in front of the garrison of a Central Theater Command Air Force radar station situated on a mountainside. In early summer, wildflowers were just beginning to bloom at an elevation of more than 1,700 meters — the seasons here run nearly a month behind those at the foot of the mountain.

The radar station garrison is small but built in an orderly and well-organized manner. The radar shelter is deployed right within the compound, and all manner of facilities are fully in place. After a quick look around, the reporter thought to himself: the officers and soldiers standing watch in an environment like this have it fairly convenient.

At that moment, Station Commander Li Siqi pointed toward the rear of the mountain: "Come on, let's go 'up the mountain' and have a look." The reporter was momentarily puzzled — weren't they already on the mountain?

Rounding the barracks, a stairway built along the mountainside came into view. The steps were neatly constructed, the cement joints still fresh — clearly someone had maintained them recently. Li Siqi told the reporter that this was the path leading to the radar position at the summit, and the station's officers and soldiers called it the "mountain path." In the past, the radar shelter was set up at the summit, and officers and soldiers relied entirely on this stairway to go up and down for duty, training, and emergency repairs. At the beginning of this year, the station was designated a pilot unit and moved its duty positions from the summit down to the command room on the mountainside. Combat readiness response became more rapid, and the need to go up the mountain dropped sharply. On a daily basis, only a small number of personnel need to go up to maintain the radar antenna and carry out routine inspections.

The reporter could not help but ask curiously: "Now that the duty positions have been moved down, and people don't go up very often anymore, is it really necessary to go to the trouble of maintaining this stretch of steps?"

Li Siqi did not answer the question directly. Instead, he brought up First Sergeant (Grade One) Jiang Lifeng, who has since retired. Sergeant Jiang spent nearly 30 years at the station, witnessed this path built from nothing, and walked up these steps to the summit every single day. Sometimes he went up alone to inspect the equipment; sometimes he brought a few new soldiers along, talking as they walked, recounting stories from years past.

The reporter's group encountered First Sergeant (Grade One) Jia Xinzhao — Jiang Lifeng's apprentice — on the mountain, in the middle of maintaining the steps. He worked with practiced ease while new soldiers assisted at his side. During a rest break, Jia Xinzhao pointed at the ground beneath his feet and recounted the origins of this path:

When the radar station was first built, there was simply no path here. The equipment was at the summit, and getting up and down meant forcing your way through rocks and weeds on two legs. When it rained, the dirt slope turned to mud, and taking a fall was commonplace. One night during an emergency mobilization drill, a soldier ran up in the dark, sprained his ankle, and still pushed through to reach his position. After that day, Sergeant Jiang and his comrades made up their minds: a path that everyone had to walk every day absolutely had to be built. With no machinery, they relied on hammer and chisel alone. From the mountainside to the summit, 399 steps — that is how the path was carved out.

"Because of mission requirements, we had to walk this path back and forth several times a day. The moment the combat readiness alarm sounded, we had to charge straight up the mountain — arriving at our positions gasping for breath, with no time to recover before going on duty." Jia Xinzhao said that having gone through experiences like that, the station later developed a tradition: with each new intake of soldiers, the veterans first take them on a walk up this path.

"When we were initially deliberating over whether it was necessary to build this stretch of steps, the station Party branch held a special meeting," Li Siqi said. Consensus was reached quickly: build it — and not only build it, but build it well. This path is not merely a passageway to the summit; it also represents the mission and sense of duty of radar soldiers. As long as there are still duty positions or equipment up there, this path must be kept in good repair.

Climbing up along the steps, the wind at the summit suddenly grew fierce, buffeting those who stood there. Radar Technician, Second-Class Senior Sergeant Xue Ren said this wind was already considered mild. One winter during an emergency repair, temperatures dropped to below minus 30 degrees Celsius, and icy wind carrying sand particles lashed at them so hard they could barely open their eyes. He and the station commander ran up from below, their hands stiffened with cold; the two of them would warm their hands together and then keep working. After more than three hours of continuous effort, they finally resolved a sudden malfunction in a new-type radar. When the task was complete, they gathered the young backbone personnel and compiled the lessons learned from that emergency repair under low-temperature conditions into a set of procedures, which became an important reference for the station's equipment support work.

As dusk fell, the mountains grew still and quiet, and lights came on in several rooms of the garrison. In the operations research room, the station commander led the "study-and-research team" in war-gaming exercise plans and working through the handling of special situations and anti-jamming operations. In the study room, Plotter Meng Xiangsen was putting in extra practice on intelligence transmission, his fingers blistered but unwilling to stop. He said his scores had just reached the passing mark, and there was still a gap between him and excellence.

Today, though the duty positions have been moved down from the mountain, the officers and soldiers have set out on another "mountain path" — the equipment has been upgraded, conditions have improved, but there is no end to enhancing professional capability. They carry forward the same drive that once went into building those steps, and apply it to studying and planning for war: every training subject is a new step on the stairway, and every technical challenge is a stone that must be chiseled and shaped. The officers and soldiers say this path must be walked — and walked well.

Standing at the summit, gazing at the radar antenna rising above the surrounding peaks, the reporter suddenly recalled a detail noticed on the way up — the sea-buckthorn trees lining both sides of the steps were in bloom, clusters of small yellow-green flowers opening all along the way. The officers and soldiers said that sea-buckthorn fears neither barren soil, nor wind and sand, nor bitter cold, and from this they have drawn a lesson: the more barren the soil, the deeper the roots; the fiercer the wind and rain, the stronger the will; the more bitter the cold, the sweeter the reward. Is this not another expression of the spirit of the "mountain path"? It was so when they were chiseling stone and laying the path back then; it is equally so now when they work through the night in the command room tackling hard problems: grit your teeth when conditions are harsh, and don't let up when conditions improve.

Walking back down from the summit, the reporter turned for one last look at that stretch of path. The 399 steps lay quietly embedded in the mountainside — one end connected to the hardships of the past, the other leading to today's duty positions. Every officer and soldier at the radar station knows clearly that before them lies another path toward higher standards, faster response, and stronger capability on the road to a stronger military — and every step of that journey must be taken with solid footing.

Editor's Note

The Symphony of "Two Paths"

■ Liu Weiqi

In the television drama "Soldiers Sortie" (士兵突击), there is a classic scene: Xu Sanduo, alone, quietly builds a road across a desolate and lonely grassland. It is precisely this road that helps the soldiers of "Grassland Fifth Squad" recover the bearing that befits a military man.

Following the reporter's footsteps on this visit, this editor felt the same kind of steadfast resolve at this radar station. The duty positions have been moved down the mountain — but should the steps leading up still be maintained? The officers and soldiers answered without hesitation: yes — not only maintain them, but maintain them well. Some things may seem "useless," yet they can elevate the spirit; some paths may seem "hard to walk," yet they are precisely what tempers character.

The changes at the radar station reflect a new challenge for grassroots-level (基层) development: equipment has been upgraded, data links optimized, and working and training conditions are incomparably better than before — so how does one maintain, as ever, the charging posture of struggle?

"We must treat the grassroots as fertile ground for carrying forward fine traditions, actively explore the laws and beneficial practices of grassroots-level development under new circumstances, attach importance to work that is fundamental, beneficial in the long run, and builds lasting momentum, and lay a more solid foundation for force development and combat effectiveness." These words point to the key to solving the problem — the officers' and soldiers' duty positions may be adjusted, but the "duty position" of the spirit must not be lost; technical means may be iterated, but the underlying quality of hard struggle must not fade.

The real-world "mountain path" carries memory and inheritance; the "mountain path" of capability leads toward victory and winning in war — and the symphony of these "two paths" thereby becomes all the more concrete. On the journey to a stronger military, we must continuously enrich the spiritual connotations of the revolutionary soldier of the new era, and let the tenacity of "hammer and chisel" (一锤一凿) blaze with new vitality at new duty positions. The goal of the centenary of the founding of the People's Liberation Army is pressing and imminent. From every step on the stairway to every duty position, what is needed are those who press forward in continuous struggle.

Original Chinese
雷达站有条“上山路” ■解放军报记者 刘 敏 曾宇明 绘 沿着崎岖的山路盘旋而上，一个多小时后，车辆在位于半山腰的中部战区空军某雷达站营区前缓缓停稳。入夏时节，海拔1700多米的山上繁花初绽，这里的季节比山下迟了将近一个月。 雷达站营区不大，却建设得规整有序，雷达方舱就部署在营院，各类设施一应俱全。溜了一圈，记者心想，官兵们在这样的环境里执勤，倒也便利。 此时，站长李斯琪朝后山指了指：“走，咱们‘上山’看看。”记者一时有些纳闷——这不都已经在山上了吗？ 绕过营房，一段依山而建的阶梯出现在眼前。台阶修得整齐，水泥勾缝还很新，显然是近期有人维护过。李斯琪告诉记者，这是通往山顶雷达阵地的路，站里官兵管它叫“上山路”。过去雷达方舱架在山顶，官兵们执勤、训练、抢修，全凭这段阶梯上上下下。今年年初，站里作为试点单位，把值班战位从山顶搬到了半山腰的指挥室，战备响应更加迅速，上山的需求一下子少了很多，日常只需要派人去维护雷达天线，做好常规检查。 记者不禁好奇问道：“战位搬下来之后，上去的次数不多了，还有必要费劲维护这段台阶吗？” 对这个问题，李斯琪没有直接回答，而是提起了已退休的一级军士长姜利锋。姜班长在站里待了将近30年，见证了这条路从无到有建设起来，每天都沿着这段台阶到山顶走一趟。有时一个人上去检查装备，有时带几个新兵，边走边聊，讲当年的故事。 记者一行在山上碰见了正维护台阶的一级上士、姜利锋的徒弟贾新召。他活干得娴熟，新兵跟在旁边打下手。歇息时间，贾新召指着脚下讲起了这段路的来历—— 雷达站建设之初，这里压根没有路。装备在山顶，上下一趟全靠两腿在乱石杂草间硬蹚，遇上下雨，土坡变泥坡，摔跟头是常事。有一回夜间紧急拉动，一个战士摸黑往上跑，崴了脚还硬撑着冲到战位。那天之后，姜班长和战友们下了决心：大家天天要走的路，说什么也得把它修出来。没有机械，就靠一锤一凿。从山腰到山顶，399级台阶，就这样开辟了出来。 “由于任务需求，我们每天要在这条路来回走上好几趟。战备警报一响，必须立刻往山上冲，跑到战位气喘吁吁，顾不上休整就上机执勤。”贾新召说，有过这样的经历，站里后来形成了一个传统：每批新兵到站，老兵先带他们走一趟这条路。 “当初研究有没有必要修这段台阶路时，站党支部专门开了个会。”李斯琪说，大家意见很快达成一致：修，不仅要修，还要修好。这条路不光是通往山顶的过道，也代表着雷达兵的使命担当。只要还有战位或装备，这条路就要维护好。 沿着阶梯一路攀登，山顶的风陡然猛烈起来，人被吹得有些晃。雷达技师、二级上士薛仁说，这风已经算小的了。有一年冬天紧急抢修，气温低至零下30多摄氏度，寒风裹着沙粒打得人睁不开眼。他和站长从山下一路跑上来，手冻得发僵，两人就焐一焐手接着干，连续奋战3个多小时，终于排除了某新型雷达的突发故障。任务结束，他们带着年轻骨干把这次低温条件下的抢修心得梳理成流程，成了站里装备保障的重要参考。 天色渐暗，大山沉静下来，营区几个房间亮起了灯。作战研究室里，站长带着“学研小分队”推演演训方案，对处置特情和反干扰操作进行攻关；学习室里，标记员孟祥森加练情报拍发，手指磨出了水泡也不肯停，他说成绩刚合格，离优秀还有距离。 如今，值班战位虽然搬下了山，官兵们却踏上了另一条“上山路”——装备更新了、条件改善了，但提升专业能力没有止境。他们把当年修台阶的劲头延续下来，用在了研战谋战上：每一个课目都是一级新台阶，每一道技术难题都是一块需要雕琢的石头。官兵们说，这条路必须走，还得走好。 站在山顶，望着矗立在群峰之上的雷达天线，记者忽然想起上山时注意到的一个细节——台阶两旁的沙棘树正在花期，黄绿色的小花一簇一簇开着。官兵们说，沙棘不怕贫瘠、抗风沙、耐严寒，他们从中领悟到：越是贫瘠越扎根，越是风雨越顽强，越是苦寒越甘甜。这何尝不是“上山路”精神的另一种表达？当年凿石铺路是这样，如今在指挥室里彻夜攻关也是这样：条件差的时候咬紧牙，条件改善后不松劲。 从山顶往山下走时，记者回头看了一眼那段路。399级台阶静静地嵌在山间，一头连着过去的艰苦，一头通向今天的战位。雷达站每名官兵心里都清楚，自己面前还有一条通向更高标准、更快反应、更强能力的强军路——必须把征程上的每一步扎扎实实走好。 编辑感言 “两条路”的交响 ■刘伟祺 电视剧《士兵突击》里有个经典情节：许三多独自一人，在荒凉寂寞的草原上默默修出了一条路。正是这条路，让“草原五班”的战士找回了身为军人应有的姿态。 随着记者的脚步探访，这座雷达站，编者也感受到了同样一种执著。战位搬下山，上山的台阶还修不修？官兵们回答干脆：修，不仅修，更要修好。有些事看似“无用”，却能升华精神；有些路看似“难走”，恰能淬炼品格。 雷达站的变化，折射出基层建设的新课题：装备迭代了、链路优化了，工作训练条件今非昔比，如何一如既往保持奋斗的冲锋姿态？ “要把基层作为赓续优良传统的沃土，积极探索新形势下基层建设规律和有益做法，重视抓好管根本、利长远、增后劲的工作，把部队建设和战斗力基础打得更加牢靠。”这句话道出了解题关键——官兵战位可以调整，精神的“战位”不能失守；技术手段可以迭代，艰苦奋斗的底色不能褪去。 现实中的“上山路”承载记忆与传承，能力上的“上山路”通向制胜与打赢，“两条路”的交响也由此更加具象化。强军征程上，我们要不断丰富新时代革命军人的精神内涵，让“一锤一凿”的韧劲在新战位焕发新的生命力。实现建军一百年奋斗目标迫在眉睫，从每一级台阶到每一个战位，都需要接续奋斗的赶路人。
